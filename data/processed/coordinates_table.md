# The disputed ports: state of the question

For each port, the proposals on record and the coordinates each source gives.

Ptolemy's figures are reproduced as he gives them, in his own frame — longitudes
reckoned from the Fortunate Isles, latitudes systematically compressed — and are
marked °P. They are not commensurable with the modern coordinates in the same
columns. On the Indian coast his longitudes correlate closely with the true values
(r = 0.97, residual c. 77 km), while his latitudes do not (r = 0.48, residual
c. 470 km): he assigns Mouziris, Podouke and Kolkhoi latitudes of 14–15° where the
true figures are 10.2°, 11.9° and 8.6°.

## Leuke Kome

The identification with Aynunah, and specifically with the coastal settlement at
Khuraybah rather than the oasis a short way inland, rests on two independent grounds.
Strabo (16.4.23–24) places Leuke Kome on a caravan route running inland to Petra, which
suits the northern Hijaz and suits no harbour as far south as al-Wajh; and the
Saudi–Polish excavations of 2014–18 recovered Nabataean and Roman pottery, substantial
masonry, and a necropolis of more than a hundred tombs. Nappo (2010) nevertheless
maintains the case for al-Wajh on topographical grounds, though no Hellenistic or Roman
material has been reported from that site.

Two difficulties remain. The excavation was directed at a site already expected to be
Leuke Kome, so the recovery of Nabataean material there establishes occupation rather
than identity. More seriously, the two ancient notices of distance have never been
reconciled: the Periplus (19) reckons two or three days' sail from Myos Hormos, while
Strabo reports that the fleet of Aelius Gallus required fourteen days to reach the same
port. No account of the discrepancy has been offered.

## Barbarikon

Barbarikon is the least securely placed of the six. Banbhore, on the Gharo Creek some
thirty kilometres inland of the present shoreline, has been the majority candidate since
Cunningham proposed it in the nineteenth century, but the identity of that site is itself
contested: it has been claimed for Barbarikon, for the Sasanian and early Islamic Daybul,
and for both in succession. No inscription or find names the place.

The obstacle is geomorphological rather than textual. Casson (1989) cites the judgement
that the changes in the rivers of Sind over eighteen centuries "preclude the possibility
of satisfactory identifications" of either Barbarikon or Minnagar. The Periplus (38–39)
supplies one structural datum, that Barbarikon lay on the middle of the seven mouths of
the Indus, and Ptolemy (7.1) lists all seven with coordinates, which makes the fourth,
Cariphi, the object of the search. Testing that proposition requires a reconstruction of
the first-century channel network rather than a rereading of the text.

## Rhapta

Casson's treatment is explicitly conditional. The Periplus (16) places Rhapta two runs
beyond Menuthias; if Menuthias is Pemba, Rhapta lies in the neighbourhood of Dar es
Salaam, and if Menuthias is Zanzibar, at the mouth of the Rufiji. Since Menuthias is
itself unidentified, the one sailing interval available is measured from an unknown point.

Datoo (1970) concluded that the evidence permits the delimitation of a locality, between
the mouths of the Pangani and the Rufiji, but not the identification of a site. Chami has
reported Roman-period material from the Rufiji mainland and submerged structures off
north-west Mafia; this is the strongest physical evidence yet adduced, though no excavated
site has been shown to be Rhapta. Ptolemy (4.7) distinguishes three features — a Rhapta
metropolis "a short distance from the sea", the mouth of the Rhaptus river, and a Rhaptum
promontory — which at least confirms a riverine setting, and assigns the metropolis a
latitude of 7° S, between Dar es Salaam and the Rufiji and well south of the Pangani.

## Tyndis

The question remains open. Casson (1989, App. 5) puts the odds "slightly in favour of
Ponnani", which is as far as the evidence takes him; the competing proposals — Tanur,
Kadalundi and Beypore, Koyilandy — are distributed over some eighty kilometres of coast.
The argument for Ponnani is logistical rather than archaeological: it is the harbour
nearest the gap through the Ghats at Palghat, and therefore the natural coastal terminus
of the overland route across the peninsula.

No candidate has yielded an excavated port of the Roman period. Tyndis is also the most
thinly attested of the ports considered here, appearing in Ptolemy (7.1.8) but in neither
Pliny nor Strabo.

## Muziris

Muziris is commonly described as securely located, and the description now requires
qualification. Casson (1989) placed it at Cranganore (Kodungallur) on the authority of a
Tamil poem that sets Muziris at the mouth of the Periyar, which was a reasonable
conclusion on the evidence then available. Excavation since 2007 has complicated it:
Pattanam, some seven kilometres to the south, has produced the Indo-Roman assemblage —
amphorae, terra sigillata, rouletted ware, glass and beads — that Kodungallur has not.

What is established is that a substantial Indo-Roman port complex operated in the lower
Periyar in the first centuries AD. Which settlement within that complex carried the name
is not established. Shajan, Tomber, Selvakumar and Cherian (2004) argue the case for
Pattanam, while more recent sedimentological work treats Pattanam as a satellite of a port
at Kodungallur; the course of the Periyar has itself shifted since antiquity. Muziris
cannot therefore serve as a fixed point in a calibration set without importing an open
question into it.

## Nelkynda

Casson's preference for Niranam rests on internal consistency rather than on material
evidence. The Periplus (54–55) places Nelkynda 120 stadia up the river at whose mouth
Bakare stood, and of the candidates only Niranam sets the two settlements on a single
stream, the Pambiyar, at approximately the stated interval. Schoff's identification with
Kottayam fails on both counts, since Kottayam lies on the Minachil rather than the
Pambiyar and considerably further than 120 stadia from Pirakkad. Kollam and Neendakara,
each some four kilometres from the sea, are excluded by the stated distance.

No archaeological evidence supports any of the candidates. A further complication is that
Ptolemy (7.1.9) enters Melkynda among the Aioi rather than in Limyrike, assigning it to a
different kingdom from the one the Periplus gives it.

## Bakare

Bakare and Nelkynda must be treated together, since the Periplus (55) defines Bakare as
the mouth of the river on which Nelkynda lay. Casson takes Pirakkad. Pliny (6.105)
independently associates Becare with the Neacyndi, which corroborates the pairing from a
source other than the Periplus.

The name is itself a conjecture. The single manuscript reads Bararē; Casson's correction
to Bakarē follows Ptolemy's Bakarē (7.1.8) and Pliny's Becare. The emendation is very
probably right, but it is a reconstruction and not a transmitted reading.
