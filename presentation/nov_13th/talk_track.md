# Talk track, 13 November

**1. Title**
- Where were the Periplus ports, and how did they work?

**2. Two port clusters on one sea** (s02a → b → c)
- The Periplus, c. AD 50: a merchant's guide from Egypt to India.
- Two case studies: the Malabar ports (Tyndis, Muziris, Nelkynda, Bakarē) and Ed-Dur in the Gulf.
- Today: Malabar.

**3. How the thesis is built**
- A statistical framework, then two case studies, each quantitative and qualitative.
- Today: Malabar, both halves.

**4. The 1857 survey** (s04a → b → c)
- A British Admiralty survey of 1851–53, measured with a lead line.
- The oldest measured depths for this coast.
- Zoom to the Kodungallur mouth, the traditional Muziris area.

**5. Reading the chart into data** (s05a → b → c)
- Every number on the chart is a depth in fathoms.
- 428 soundings read by hand and checked a second time.

**6. The soundings in 3D**
- Each point is a measured depth.

**7. The modelled seabed**
- A Gaussian process fills in the seabed between the soundings, with uncertainty.
- It allows for a beach slope near shore and extra roughness at the river mouth.

**8. Does it work?** (s08_texas_pair)
- Proof of concept: a modern Texas inlet whose whole seabed is known.
- We survey it as sparsely as Kodungallur was surveyed in 1853, then rebuild it.
- It comes back to within about 30 cm in open water and 40 cm along the shore. In the inlet itself, about 1.2 m.
- If asked: we also hid the mouth soundings at Kodungallur and predicted them. 8 of 9 fell inside the predicted range.

**9. Where an ancient ship could float** (s09a0 → s09a → s09a2)
- The same seabed, recoloured.
- Blue is deep enough for a ship drawing 3 m. Sand is too shallow.
- At Kodungallur, ship-worthy water starts about 1.3 km out.
- Pliny says the same of Muziris: ships anchored far from land, and boats carried the cargo.
- The 1866 Admiralty *Pilot*: the bar was "only available for small coasting craft".

**10. The whole coast** (s10a → b)
- Seven candidate mouths, all modelled the same way.
- At every one, ship water is 0.3 to 1.6 km out.
- Every mouth worked as an offshore anchorage, so depth does not pick the port.

**11. So how did these ports work?**
- If ships lay offshore, the question is the turnaround: how a ship was handled.

**12. Argument 2: the turnaround** (s12a → b → c, then wharf photos)
- Leave Egypt about 20 July and arrive about 28 September, just as the coast reopens.
- Loading takes days to about three weeks, but ships must leave by 13 January.
- So most of the stay was waiting.
- The pepper harvest is not in until after the deadline, so cargoes came from store, held by locals.
- At Pattanam: a dugout canoe and a wharf.

**13. Argument 2: who stayed** (objects, then s13e → f → g)
- Indians in Egyptian ports left cooking pots and their names. Romans at Pattanam left trade goods only.
- Tamil poems: at Muciṟi, Yavana ships come and go.
- At Madurai, Yavanas are guards and wine suppliers. Only at Puhar do foreigners "dwell".
- A port run by locals for seasonal crews.

**14. Argument 1: when the crossing began** (s14a → b → c)
- North-west India was crossing before Rome.
- South India appears only from the late 1st century BC.
- The pottery shows an early South Asian network, not an early Malabar crossing.

**15. Argument 3: after Rome** (s15a → b → c)
- Roman contact peaks in the 1st century AD.
- Gulf pottery follows, 3rd to 8th centuries. By about 550, Cosmas no longer names Muziris.
- The trade moves: to Kollam (849), the Chera capital at Kodungallur, then Kochi.
- The harbour zone moved rather than died.

**16. What we can say**
- The seabed explains how ships were handled. It does not pick the port.
- Foreign crews came seasonally and locals ran the port.
- South India joined the crossing late.
- The port zone outlived Rome by shifting along the coast.

**17. Next: Ed-Dur**
- A lagoon port, now about 4 km inland.
- The same methods: shoreline, depth, and the material record.
