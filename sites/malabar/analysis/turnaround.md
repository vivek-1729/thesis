# The turnaround at Muziris: how a ship was handled from arrival to departure

*Research and assembly for reframing argument 2 of `ceramics_and_ports.md`. Prepared 11 November 2026. This is not the rewrite of argument 2; `argument2_westerners_at_muziris.md` is unchanged.*

---

## The question, and why it reframes argument 2

Argument 2 currently asks whether Westerners lived at Muziris, and answers that the evidence fits seasonal crews better than a resident colony. That answer is close to existing sceptical scholarship. Gurukkal and Whittaker already wrote that "the shippers and their crews disembarked and lived in the ports while waiting several months for the return monsoon winds, while the trading organisations and warehouses must have been local" (2001: 336). **[Supplementary]** On its own the conclusion is not new.

This document asks a different question: **how was a ship actually handled at Muziris, from the day it arrived to the day it left?** The evidence for each step sits in a separate literature: Latin and Greek geography, a Greek papyrus from Egypt, Tamil poetry, the Pattanam excavations, nineteenth-century Admiralty pilots, the project's own bathymetry and wind model, and Portuguese and Arab travellers' accounts. Nobody appears to have put them together as a single sequence with a time budget. Doing so turns the residence question into something that can be measured. If handling a ship took a small fraction of the time a ship had to stay, then crews spent most of the season waiting, and the port's work was done by local boats, local stores and local officials. The seasonal reading then follows from the logistics rather than from an absence of evidence.

**The identification problem.** "Muziris" below means the port named in the texts. Pattanam is a candidate for it, and the identification is disputed. Evidence from Pattanam is reported as evidence from Pattanam, and it bears on Muziris only if the identification holds. Nothing here is a claim about where Muziris was.

**Labels.**

- **[Agent-read]**: read by this research agent or its sub-agents, in the project library (`../library/text-cache/`) or in a source saved to `readings/turnaround/`, and not re-checked by hand.
- **[Supplementary]**: from the local readings in `readings/supplementary/` (PAMA catalogue, Cherian, Gurukkal and Whittaker, Cobb 2023, Tomber).
- **[Secondhand]**: known only from another author's citation or a review.
- **[Project]**: a result of this repository's own data or models.

**Confidence** in the tables: *high* means a primary text or a measured result read directly; *medium* means read directly but open to more than one reading, or a later analogy; *low* means secondhand, poorly dated or inferred.

**Dates of the main texts.** The Periplus is c. AD 50. Pliny wrote c. AD 77, and De Romanis dates his India data to AD 48/49–51/52 (De Romanis 2014: 86). **[Agent-read]** The Muziris papyrus is mid-second century AD (Casson 1990). **[Agent-read]** The Sangam poems are conventionally 300 BC–AD 300, with the dating disputed (see argument 2, §1d).

**What is solid.** Three independent sources, Pliny, the Periplus (for Bakarē) and *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 343, describe the same arrangement: the big ship lies off, and local boats move the cargo. The nineteenth-century bar was impassable to anything but small craft, and the project's model agrees as a lower bound.

**What is gap.** No source gives the distance offshore in antiquity. Pliny's *longe* is relative. The 1850s bar is not the first-century bar: the 1341 flood reorganised the Periyar mouth, and the Narayana ancient shoreline is undated (FINDINGS; `new_findings_v2.md` §6). No draught for an Indian Ocean ship is measured. The bar and the anchorage are therefore known in kind, not in number, for the first century.

**What is gap.** There is no evidence for customs, a customs house or port officials at Muziris. The only Tamil customs passage describes Puhar. The asymmetry is itself a finding: the documents that survive are Egyptian, and they record what the Roman state needed to tax.

**The stay.** Arrival about 28 September and departure between 8 December and 13 January give a stay of about **70 to 107 days**. Casson's "no earlier than September" and Cobb's "latter half of September" give the same range to within a couple of weeks.

**The harvest.** The plains harvest runs from about November to February and the hill harvest from January into March or April. Buchanan's cultivators delivered in January or February. A ship that had to leave by 13 January could load the new crop only from early lowland vines, and must otherwise have loaded pepper from the previous season, held in store. Casson (1989: 220) saw no difficulty because he put the picking in October or November, earlier than the agronomic sources do.

**What is solid.** The outer limits of the stay: the coast reopens in mid-September, the deadline is 13 January, and the open-sea wind is not the binding constraint at either end.

**What is gap.** No ancient source says when pepper was bought or how much was in store. The project's model does not represent the coast, so it cannot itself say when the coast reopened in the first century.

## 7. Comparanda: analogy, not evidence

*Everything in this section describes other ports or later centuries. It shows what the practice could look like where the ancient sources are silent. It is not evidence for Muziris.*

### 7a. The Red Sea end

- **Berenike.** Two early Roman seawalls, one with "a number of small wooden bollards [that] projected out of the harbor bottom, suggesting that smaller craft tied up alongside the seawall". Sidebotham cannot tell whether the northern inlet took "only smaller tender vessels associated with unloading and loading larger ships anchored out in deeper water" or small coastal craft; "the more substantial southern harbor served larger merchantmen. Such segregation would have made control for customs procedures much simpler" (Sidebotham 2011: 60–61). Strabo suggests Berenike was a roadstead; Pliny, some decades later, suggests a harbour (2011: 61). **[Agent-read]** The same mix of tenders and bollards as at Pattanam, at the other end of the route, with a customs purpose stated.
- **Myos Hormos.** A "rudimentary" late 1st c. BC to early 1st c. AD waterfront of amphorae packed into the intertidal mud, a "hard" and a jetty "believed to have facilitated access from ship to shore" (Peacock and Blue 2011: 37–38). **[Agent-read]** Sidebotham: both Red Sea harbours were "extremely primitive and jerry-built ... due to the practical issue of moving cargoes through the ports quickly and efficiently" (2011: 61). **[Agent-read]**
- **The papyrus logistics.** At the Egyptian end the turnaround is written down step by step (§3).

### 7b. Barygaza

PME 44–46: royal fishermen with *trappaga* and *kotymba* meet ships as far out as Syrastrene, tow them through the shoals to "predetermined stopping places", move with the flood and anchor in "basins" on the ebb, over about 300 stadia of river; anchors drag in the tidal bore, and small craft capsize (Casson 1989: 77–79). **[Agent-read]** Casson notes a spring range at the head of the gulf of "no less than twenty-nine and one-half feet" (Casson 1989, commentary on 45:15.16). **[Agent-read]** Barygaza's turnaround was governed by the tide and run by the king's men. Muziris, with a 0.58 m spring range **[Project]**, could not have worked that way.

### 7c. The east coast

Puhar has customs, stores and the tiger seal (*Paṭṭiṉappālai*, §3) and a foreigners' quarter (argument 2 §1d). Arikamedu has a brick structure that Wheeler called a "warehouse", 50 m long, and timber port works (Pillai 2010, a popular summary). **[Agent-read]** Gurukkal and Whittaker cite Arikamedu and Kaveripumpattinam for "warehouses and perhaps even a separate merchant quarter" (2001: 339). **[Supplementary]**

**What the analogies suggest.** Across six centuries the same pattern recurs on this coast: big ships at anchor some kilometres off, local boats doing all the work, rulers or their clerks collecting dues at the beach, the coast closed from about June to mid-September, and foreign crews waiting ashore for the season. Each of these practices fills a gap in §§1–6. None of them is evidence for the first century.

## 8. Synthesis: a Muziris turnaround as a sequence

This reconstruction joins §§1–6. Where a step has no ancient evidence the analogy used is named. Numbers are ranges built from stated assumptions. They are meant to show orders of magnitude, not to estimate the true values.

### 8a. The sequence

| Step | What happens | When | Based on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Leave Berenike or Myos Hormos | about 20 July | PME 56; Pliny 6.104; De Romanis 2014 |
| 2 | Water at Ocelis or Kanē, then cross | August to September | PME 25; Pliny; De Romanis 2014 n. 44 |
| 3 | Arrive off Muziris, after the coast reopens | about 28 September (Pliny's 70 days); "latter half of September" (Cobb) | Pliny; Casson 1989: 290; 1909 *Pilot* closure dates as analogy |
| 4 | Anchor in the open roadstead, some distance off the river mouth | on arrival | Pliny 6.104; PME 55 (Bakarē); 1850s bar for small craft only |
| 5 | Unload the inbound cargo (coin, metals, coral, glass, wine, grain) into local boats | first days | Pliny ("in and out in boats"); *Puṟam* 343 (gold ashore in *kaḻit toṇi*) |
| 6 | Pay any dues and deal through local merchants | early in the stay | No ancient evidence for Muziris; Puhar, Ibn Battuta, Gama and Pyrard as analogy |
| 7 | Crews wait ashore, eating imported grain | most of the stay | PME 56; Pattanam wharf wheat (undated); Ibn Battuta's three months as analogy |
| 8 | Buy pepper, much of it held in store from the previous season; malabathron and nard brought by sea from the east | through the stay | PME 56; Pliny 6.105; *Puṟam* 343 sacks in the houses; harvest dates |
| 9 | Load the outbound cargo from local boats | some days to some weeks; see 8b | Pliny; PME 55 |
| 10 | Sail with the north-east monsoon | 8 December to 13 January | Pliny 6.106; De Romanis 2014; voyage model |

**The stay** is about 70 to 107 days (§6).

### 8b. How long does the loading take?

**The cargo.** A Hermapollon-sized return cargo is about 185 t or more on Morelli's figures for pepper and malabathron alone (131.5 t and at least 49 t), and about 620–635 t on De Romanis's (De Romanis 2015: 139; Ruffing 2021). The Hermapollon is a mid-second-century ship, a century after the Periplus.

**The boats** (assumptions, not sourced capacities):

- *A.* A dugout of the Pattanam canoe's class: 0.3–1 t a trip.
- *B.* A plank-built lighter or large dugout: 2–5 t a trip.
- *C.* A boat the size of the 1911 *kettuvallam*, 18 × 2.1 × 2.4 m: 10–20 t a trip. This is my rough envelope from the dimensions, not a published capacity.

**The trip.** If the cargo was loaded at the town, each trip runs about 3–4 km down the river (the 20 stadia of PME 54) and out to an anchorage plausibly 2–4 km off the mouth (Cochin 2–2½ miles, Calicut 2 miles, in 1909–11; Gama's league and a half in 1498). That is 12–16 km for the round trip. Paddled at 3–5 km/h, with loading at both ends, and with boat work stopping in the stormy late afternoon (Ibn Battuta), the working assumption is **one round trip per boat per day**.

**The fleet.** Thirty boats per ship is taken from Gama's thirty *almadias*. A hundred boats is an upper case, consistent with De Romanis's "several hundred riverboats" shared among fewer than a dozen ships.

| Cargo | Boat class | Trips needed | Days with 30 boats | Days with 100 boats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 185 t | A, 0.3–1 t | 185–617 | 6–21 | 2–6 |
| 185 t | B, 2–5 t | 37–92 | 1–3 | under 1 |
| 185 t | C, 10–20 t | 9–18 | under 1 | under 1 |
| 635 t | A, 0.3–1 t | 635–2,117 | 21–71 | 6–21 |
| 635 t | B, 2–5 t | 127–318 | 4–11 | 1–3 |
| 635 t | C, 10–20 t | 32–64 | 1–2 | under 1 |

**What the table says.** Under almost every combination, loading takes between a day and three weeks, against a stay of 70 to 107 days. The exception matters. If the only lighters were dugouts the size of the Pattanam canoe, loading a 635 t cargo with thirty boats would take three to ten weeks, most of the stay. So either larger lighters existed (the *toṇi* and *paḵṟi* of the poems may be them; no plank-built boat has been excavated), or the fleet serving each ship was much larger than thirty, or the Hermapollon's cargo is at the low end. Each of these can be tested (§10).

The table leaves out the inbound cargo, stowage on board, and weather days. These would add time, perhaps doubling it, without changing the order of magnitude except in the dugout case.

**Grain for the wait.** Assuming crews of 30–150 people (illustrative; no crew figure is known) and 0.7–1.0 kg of grain a person a day (the range usually used for Roman rations; not checked here), a stay of 70–107 days needs about **1.5–16 t of grain per ship**. That is under 3 per cent of a 635 t cargo and under 9 per cent of a 185 t one. It is a quantity a ship could carry out as cargo, which is what PME 56 says happened.

### 8c. What the time budget implies

1. **Most of the stay was waiting.** Handling a ship took days to a few weeks. The rest of a three-month stay was set by the calendar: the coast reopened in mid-September, and the Roman ships could not wait for the full harvest because of the 13 January deadline.
2. **The port's work was local.** Every step at the Indian end used local boats, local stores and local supply chains. The texts and the poems agree on this, and the analogies show the same pattern for six centuries.
3. **Pepper had to be in store before the ships came.** The new crop is not all in until after the Roman deadline. A cargo of hundreds of tonnes must largely have been held from the previous season by people who were already there. This is the strongest logistical reason for a local merchant class at Muziris, and it does not need a resident Western colony to do the holding.
4. **Foreign crews were present ashore for about three months a year.** That matches the evidence in argument 2: provisions at the waterfront, no domestic Mediterranean assemblage. The question for argument 2 then becomes where on the waterfront the crews' traces should fall, which is a coastline question.

## 9. What is new here

**Connections already made in the literature, and so not claimed:**

- Lighterage at Muziris from Pliny 6.104 and *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 343 together: Gurukkal and Whittaker 2001: 336, 339; the PAMA catalogue, p. 29; Selvakumar 2017; De Romanis 2015: 148 n. 69.
- The Pattanam canoe and wharf as lighterage, and the canoe as a possible *kaḻit toṇi*: Cherian 2012: 28; Selvakumar 2011: 4.
- Arrival in September because the coast is closed in the monsoon: Casson 1989: 290 (insurance rates); De Romanis 2014: 83 n. 44; McLaughlin 2014: 151.
- The 13 January deadline in the loan contracts, and the comparison with later Calicut and Portuguese departures: De Romanis 2014: 81–86; De Romanis 2020 ch. 2.
- The scale of the local boat fleet: De Romanis 2015: 139, "several hundred riverboats", as an image, without a calculation.
- Crews waiting for months at the port: Gurukkal and Whittaker 2001: 336.

**What appears to be new, as far as the searches went:**

1. **A time budget for the turnaround.** No estimate of lighter trips or loading time for an Indo-Roman ship was found. The Roman-side sub-agent searched specifically and found none. Setting the loading time (days to weeks) against the forced stay (70 to 107 days) turns "crews waited" from an assumption into a result, under stated assumptions.
2. **The canoe as a constraint.** If the Pattanam dugout represents the lighters, a Hermapollon-sized cargo becomes hard to load in the time available. This turns the size of the lighters into a testable prediction.
3. **The harvest against the deadline.** Modern agronomy puts the plains harvest at November to February and the hills later, and Buchanan's deliveries fall in January and February. Casson's October-November picking date, which he used to say ships could load "any time after", is too early. With a 13 January deadline, most of a Roman cargo had to come from stock. **Caution:** De Romanis 2020 ch. 2 contrasts the Roman and medieval departure windows, and ch. 4 discusses Muziris-based dealers. Only the abstracts have been read. He may well draw this inference, and the chapters must be checked before this point is claimed.
4. **The open sea is not the constraint; the coast is.** The project's wind model shows a fair wind to Muziris from May to September and home from November to February. The window that ships actually used is therefore set by the closure of the coast and by the Red Sea leg and the contracts, not by the Arabian Sea. Casson and De Romanis say the coast was dangerous; the quantitative contrast with the open-sea wind field is the project's own.
5. **The documentary asymmetry, stated as a turnaround.** The Egyptian end of the papyrus is documented step by step, and the Periplus records a Roman customs station at Leukē Kōmē and royal pilots at Barygaza. It records no officials at Muziris. Nobody seems to have laid the two ends side by side. This is a modest point and may be implicit in De Romanis 2020.
6. **The Kodungallur salt ships of 1911** standing off and lightered at the very Periyar mouth in question appear not to have been cited in the Muziris literature. They are an analogy only.

## 10. What would test the reconstruction, and open leads

**Tests.**

- **Lighter size.** Any excavated plank-built boat, boat fittings or boat timbers at Pattanam or another Periyar site. A find of only small dugouts would push the reconstruction toward the low cargo reading or a larger fleet.
- **Dates.** AMS dates on the Pattanam wharf structure itself, on the wheat grains, and on the Georgia samples the PAMA catalogue mentions. If the waterfront is pre-Periplus, Pattanam's evidence drops out of the turnaround altogether.
- **Storage.** Pepper or storage jars in quantity in a non-wharf context at Pattanam would support stockpiling. The Kajale botanical reports (2007, 2008, unpublished) would say whether pepper is widespread or concentrated at the wharf.
- **The bar.** Transcribing more of the 1857 soundings, and dating the Narayana shoreline, would say whether a loaded ship could ever have crossed a Periyar mouth. If a first-century channel was deep, Pliny's *longe a terra* needs another explanation.
- **The cargo.** Which reading of the Hermapollon's pepper is right, Morelli's or De Romanis's, shifts the loading time by a factor of three.

**Open leads.**

- De Romanis 2020, chapters 2, 4 and 12–13 (the calendar, Muziris dealers, customs, the shipowner and charterers). Through the Harvard library.
- Rathbone 2000 (BSAA 46) and Morelli 2011 (Tyche 26), not online.
- Cobb 2018 (Brill), on the chronology and the stay in India.
- Nappo's review of De Romanis 2020 in *Classical Review* (paywalled) and Van Aerde in *JRA* 35 (open access, download blocked).
- Tomé Pires, Castanheda, Correia, Linschoten and the VOC Cochin records for loading times and lighter numbers. These were not reached.
- Logan's *Malabar Manual* (restricted on archive.org) for lighter capacities at Calicut.
- Crew sizes for large Roman merchantmen: Casson, *Ships and Seamanship*, and the Portuguese *naus* of similar tonnage as an analogy.
- A sea-state or surf term in the voyage model, so it can say when the Malabar coast becomes workable rather than only when the open sea can be sailed.
