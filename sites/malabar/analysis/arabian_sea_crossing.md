# When did South India start crossing the Arabian Sea?

*A test of Part 4, argument 1, of `ceramics_and_ports.md`. Prepared 10 November 2026.*

---

## The question

Argument 1 says the pottery proves a South Asian exchange network before Rome on the Bay of Bengal leg, but does not prove a crossing of the Arabian Sea before Rome. Two accounts fit the Arabian evidence:

- **The early story.** Indians were crossing to Arabia before the Roman trade began around 30 BC. Their earliest deposits at Sumhuram were later churned up, which is why they are mixed with Roman material.
- **The late story.** Indians first crossed around the same time as the Romans, some of them carrying old tableware.

This file collects everything we could find for and against each story. It reads the six supplied readings, the project library and about twenty further sources found online, including three PhD theses.

The search showed early on that "the crossing" has to be split into three questions. The literature tends to run them together:

1. **Did any Indian ships reach Arabia before Rome?**
2. **Did Western, Greek or Egyptian ships reach India before Rome?**
3. **Did people and goods from *south* India, including Malabar, cross the Arabian Sea before Rome?**

The thesis needs the third. The first two are context.

**Labels.**

- **[Read]**: I read the passage myself in the source, and checked the quotation.
- **[Agent-read]**: a research agent read the passage and quoted it; I have not re-checked it against the source.
- **[Reported]**: the source is known only through another author.
- **EARLY**, **LATE**, **NEUTRAL**: which story the item supports.

---

## The answer in brief

**1. Indian ships reached Arabia before Rome. The late story is wrong in its strong form.**

- The Periplus says that in earlier days "vessels from India did not go on to Egypt", and that those from Egypt came no further than Eudaimon Arabia (Aden), which "used to receive the cargoes of both" (PME 26; Casson 1989: 65).
- Pottery from Maharashtra and Gujarat reached Mleiha, in south-east Arabia, in the 3rd to mid-2nd century BC (Reddy 2013: 32).
- Agatharchides, in the 2nd century BC, has merchants from Patala, in the Indus delta, sailing to Socotra (Cobb 2023: 371).

All of this points to **north-western India**: the Indus, Gujarat and the Konkan.

**2. Western ships reached India before Rome, but rarely.**

- **Texts.**
  - Eudoxus's voyages around 116 BC.
  - A Ptolemaic official "of the Indian and Red Sea" by the 1st century BC.
  - Casson's judgement that Greek ships went to India after Eudoxus "only in limited numbers, and this remained the case until Augustus" (Casson 1989: 12).
- **Archaeology at the Egyptian end.** Almost nothing from securely Ptolemaic contexts: "Archaeological evidence for imported goods at the Egyptian sites during the Ptolemaic period is virtually non-existent" (Tomber 2008: 71).

**3. For south India, the late story holds on present evidence.**

- Every *dated* context for south or east Indian material in Arabia and Egypt begins in the late first century BC or later.
- So does every foreign object at Pattanam: "As of now, no foreign ceramic material clearly datable to before the late 1st century BC has been found at this site" (Selvakumar, Shajan and Tomber 2010: 34).
- The exceptions are of two kinds:
  - **Objects whose early date is borrowed from Sri Lanka.** These are the Sumhuram Rouletted Ware.
  - **Objects with no published context.** These are the two Megalithic vessels at Myos Hormos.

**What this changes in argument 1.** The argument should be restated more precisely:

> Indians were crossing the Arabian Sea before Rome, from the north-west. South India, Malabar included, appears in the archaeology of the Arabian Sea and the Red Sea only from the late first century BC, at the same time as the Roman trade. The pre-Roman evidence for a south Indian crossing rests on dates borrowed from Sri Lanka for a ware that was kept for generations, or whose production may have run into the second century AD.

That is a stronger and more defensible claim than the original. It also bears directly on the thesis: **Malabar's overseas connections, on the ceramic evidence, begin with the Roman trade rather than before it.**

## The best case for each story

**The early story at its strongest.**

- The Periplus says Indian ships came to Aden before the direct route.
- Mleiha has Indian pottery in the 3rd–2nd century BC.
- Agatharchides has Indus merchants at Socotra.
- The Italian Mission places Rouletted Ware in Sumhuram's first inhabited strata, with Aegean amphorae "earlier than 1st century AD".
- Two Megalithic vessels at Myos Hormos belong to a ware older than the Early Historic period.
- Schenk dates the end of Rouletted Ware production to the 1st century BC.

On that reading, Indians including southerners were in Arabia by the 2nd century BC.

**The late story at its strongest.**

- **North-western only.** Every pre-Roman item that is securely dated, whether text or pottery, comes from north-western India.
- **Arabia.** Every south or east Indian object with a dated context lies in late 1st-century BC or later deposits:
  - Sumhuram's oldest layers mix Indian pottery with 1st-century BC/AD amphorae, and Rouletted Ware there is associated with Dressel 2-4.
  - Qana has nothing before the late 1st century BC.
  - Socotra has no Indian visitor before the 1st century AD, and no Tamil one at all.
  - HAS1 begins in the 1st century BC.
- **Egypt.** Ptolemaic imports are virtually absent. Indian fine wares are in late 1st-century BC or 1st-century AD contexts, some curated or reworked.
- **Malabar itself.** Pattanam has no foreign ceramic clearly datable before the late 1st century BC.
- **The marker ware.** Rouletted Ware's end date is disputed. On the Anuradhapura and Arikamedu evidence it was still being made into the 2nd century AD.

**What fits neither cleanly.**

- The Myos Hormos Megalithic vessels, which have no published context.
- The Koan amphorae at Arikamedu, whose date is disputed.
- The turquoise glazed ware in Pattanam's transition layers, at a heavily disturbed site.
- Avanzini's statement that the Kos and Rhodes amphorae at Sumhuram are "earlier than 1st century AD".

## Myos Hormos and the joining-fragments test

Argument 1 proposed a test for Sumhuram. Sherds that join back into vessels mean those vessels broke where they were found. Isolated, worn sherds mean residual material.

At Myos Hormos the result is already published. The Rouletted Ware there is "sixteen sherds possibly representing two or three vessels", all "from the same deposit which is associated with the late Augustan period" (Shoebridge 2017: 112–113, citing Tomber 2002: 28).

Sixteen sherds from two or three vessels in one deposit is the pattern of whole vessels broken on site. So at the Egyptian end, Rouletted Ware vessels were in use in the late Augustan period. On Schenk's chronology they were old when they broke, which is the curation reading. On the longer chronology they may have been nearly new. Either way, they arrived with the Roman trade and not before it.

The same test at Sumhuram would settle the question there.

## What would settle it

In order of how directly each bears on the question:

1. **Sumhuram stratigraphy.** Buffa (ed.) 2019, *Khor Rori Report 4*, pp. 247–273: which phase each Indian sherd is in, and whether any join. Also the full radiocarbon appendix of Khor Rori Report 2, pp. 727–736.
2. **The Yashhuril coins.** Sedov 2008c, and Sedov's paper on two coins from Mleiha.
3. **The Myos Hormos Megalithic vessels.** Their primary publication, probably Tomber 2002 or the Myos Hormos ceramic reports.
4. **Schenk's criteria for the early and late varieties, and her end date.** Schenk 2006; Pavan and Schenk 2012; Schenk 2023.
5. **The long chronology for Rouletted Ware.** Magee 2010, *Antiquity* 84; Krishnan and Balvally 2016; Coningham 2006 on Anuradhapura.
6. **Arikamedu's Koan amphorae.** Will 2004 against Slane's dating.
