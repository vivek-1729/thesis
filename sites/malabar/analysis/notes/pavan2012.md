## 2. Method

### 2.1 How wares are identified

**Fabric and texture of clay, by eye.** RW is identified by paste: "All the decorated
fragments and many of the according rims were found to have been made of a very ﬁne grey
paste (Fig. 6/1). They belong to a group of pottery types at Tissamaharama that are
clearly linked by the similarity in texture of the clay from which they were all made
(Schenk 2001; 2006)." (p. 194) [Doc]

**Rim form plus decoration.** RW "is simple to distinguish by its typically beaked rim
(Figs 3/1–2; 6/1–4) and the eponymous decoration impressed on the ﬂat bottom inside the
dish (Fig. 3/3)." (p. 194) [Doc]

**Cross-section and temper separate true RW from imitation.** "The treatment of the
surface of this imitation RW was often of similarly high quality, but a cross-sectional
examination of the fragments will always reveal the differences both in the colour and
the high temper content. Moreover, the base fragments of coarser quality were never
decorated." (p. 195) [Doc]

**Firing atmosphere.** "Both qualities were made using the ﬁring technique of
Black-and-Red ware (BRW)" (p. 194). "The local clays of the region, however, never turn
grey after ﬁring under reduced, inverted conditions. Instead they either turn black on
the inside and red on the outside (BRW), or red inside and out (Fine Red Ware), or black
inside and out (Black Ware)." (p. 195) [Doc/Int]

**Slip and its preservation as a chronological criterion.** Early RW: "The cross section
of the earlier group of RW is almost entirely grey with just a thin red partition towards
the outside (Fig. 6/1–2). The surface is remarkably well preserved due to hard ﬁring and
usually has a brilliant gloss. In contrast, the visible slip of the later variety is
easily scratched and therefore usually quite worn (Fig. 6/4). On the inside of the vessel
the slip is often ﬂaking." (p. 196). Imitation RW: early has "a remarkable, shiny,
two-layered slip"; late "The slip has been applied in a single layer, in keeping with the
traditional BRW technique." (p. 196) [Doc]

**Wall profile as a chronological criterion.** "The shape of the earlier RW is
characterised by a straight upper wall (Fig. 6/1–2) in contrast to the later group with
an inward inclining proﬁle (Fig. 6/4)." (p. 196) [Doc]

**Paddle impressions and their orientation.** "The rim sherds of storage pots
illustrated in Figure 5/12–15, for example, exhibit the vertically applied impressions
typical of Tissa Phases a to c1, in contrast to Phase c2, when the pattern runs in a
horizontal direction. Others are only datable when the form of the vessel can be
classiﬁed." (p. 197) [Doc]

**Rims, not body sherds.** "It can only be classiﬁed and dated with certainty on the
basis of the rim shapes. Body sherds alone can be misleading and do not provide a basis
for ﬁrm classiﬁcations." (p. 199) [Int, a methodological rule]

**RPW, as redefined by Sedov and Benvenuti.** [Rep: Sedov & Benvenuti 2003] RPW is
"appropriate only to speciﬁc categories of tableware characterised by 'levigated paste,
usually ﬁred to a light or reddish yellow colour, and slipped surfaces notable for their
highly burnished quality'." The Sumhuram cooking pots fail this: "the clay used was medium
compact, sometimes even coarse, with a considerable amount of vegetal and mica temper
together with white (lime bits) and black (grit) inclusions (Sedov & Benvenuti 2003:
189)." (p. 192)

**Petrography / material analysis.** None by the authors in this article. They report
Gogte's XRD work [Rep: Gogte 2001]: "The study ﬁnally identiﬁed a source for the clay
used in 'Fine Grey Pottery' located in northern India, the author hypothesising a site on
the Ganga Plain (Gogte 2001: 199)." (p. 195) They call for sampling outside the supposed
source region: "In order to deﬁne a source area and its parameters, however, the analysis
should include samples from beyond the hypothesised limits of the region in question."
(p. 195) They also report Tomber, Cartwright and Gupta 2011 (rice temper) and Magee 2010
(one production area of imitation RW) without detail.

**Paste differs while form matches.** "Indeed, some of the fragments from Sumhuram that
bear form similarities to the Tissamaharama sequence differ in paste and fabric,
indicating different regional sources." (p. 194) [Int]

### 2.2 How the sequences are dated

**Tissamaharama: stratigraphy plus radiocarbon.** "the more complex urban architecture
built in the fourth century BC, as indicated by 14C dating." (p. 194). Footnote 10: "The
14C dates of Tissa 1 and 2 have already been published (Görsdorf 2001); an addendum to the
dates of Tissa 3 will be presented in the forthcoming main excavation reports." (p. 194)

Footnote 18 (p. 196), the phase dates: "The absolute dating of the earliest
Tissamaharama Phases based on 14C dates: Phase a (ﬁfth/fourth century BC); Phase b (third
century BC); Phase c1 (second century BC); and Phase c2 (ﬁrst century BC). This
represents a preliminary version of the sequence (Schenk 2001: 61, ﬁg. 73), which is now
being revised and updated."

Inconsistency inside the article: the body text on the same page says "More than half of
the pottery from Phases a and b (fourth–third centuries BC)" (p. 196), and the
discussion says Tissa Form A occurs "during Phase a to Phase c2 (ﬁfth/fourth–ﬁrst century
BC)" (p. 198). Phase a is given as fifth/fourth in one place and folded into
"fourth–third" in another.

No individual radiocarbon dates, laboratory numbers or calibrations are given in the
article.

**Why household pottery dates well.** [Int] "Vessels used in daily household routines
often broke and needed to be replaced, leading to moments of intense production in
response to demand at the potter's workshop. This heightens the probability that most of
the wares accumulated within each context or layer are representative of the forms and
typologies of that particular period." (p. 194)

**Sumhuram: relative sequence only.** "Although evaluation of the absolute chronology of
the excavated layers of the city has not yet been completed, a sequence of the different
construction periods has been published in connection with area A (residential quarter),
area F (temple area) and area A13 (trench outside the city wall) (Buffa & Sedov 2008;
Sedov 2008a; 2008b). Based on this proposed sequence of phases in the building and
occupation of the site, it is possible to establish a relative chronology of the strata
and architectural structures of the settlement." (pp. 192–193) [Rep: Buffa & Sedov; Sedov]

**Sumhuram: coins as associated finds.** "Of particular interest are the ﬁndings in room
A30 in BF1, including a coin from Sumhuram (Co334) and two coins from Yashuril (Co337,
Co338),7 together with the above-mentioned base from a Wheeler type 18 ceramic vessel
(Fig. 4/1), which conﬁrm the antiquity of the artefacts." (p. 193). Footnote 7: "Many
exemplars of the coinage of Yashuril have been found in Sumhuram in layers datable to the
second century BC (Sedov 2008c: 279–281)." [Rep: Sedov 2008c]

**Sumhuram is dated *by* Tissa.** The direction of inference matters: the Sumhuram
layers have no independent absolute dates in this paper, and the Indian sherds are dated
by matching them to the Tissa phases. "A comparison of the pottery from the earliest
occupation layers of Sumhuram with the Tissamaharama sequence conﬁrms an early dating for
this South Arabian harbour site." (p. 194) [Int]

**The scope of the Sumhuram sample.** "For the sake of clarity, we will limit our
analysis of the pottery from Sumhuram to the ﬁndings from a single, stratigraphically
deﬁnable context (KR I) corresponding to the earliest occupation levels of the city in
the BC layers (Fig. 1)." (p. 192). "KR I" is not further defined in the article.

### 2.3 Local versus imported

At Tissa: true RW and other "Fine Grey Pottery" are imports from northern India; BRW,
Red Ware, Fine Red Ware, Black Ware and imitation RW are local or regional southern
products. "Imitation RW is a less reﬁned, local production from all over southern India
and Sri Lanka where BRW tradition existed. It must be distinguished from specimens of
imported 'true' RW." (p. 195)

At Sumhuram: every piece discussed is an import from South Asia. Specific source within
South Asia is not established for the household wares: "Their source remains to be
established, somewhere in the southern part of India or Sri Lanka." (p. 198). For the
cooking pots: "Their rather coarse clay texture, however, has nothing in common with the
paste and technical quality of the Fine Red Ware from Tissamaharama, apart from its
reddish colour." (p. 198)

### 2.4 Residuality

At Sumhuram: "The presence of ancient sherds in late levels inside and outside the city
is doubtless to be considered as residual, as is frequently observed in sites of long
occupation. In Sumhuram this phenomenon was intensiﬁed by the small dimensions of the site
and the recurrent land uses after the settlement was abandoned. This is why some
potsherds belonging to the earliest cultural period of the city have been found in later
layers, e.g. the fragments of RW and imitation RW excavated near the surface." (p. 193)

Spoil from the 1950s American excavation: "Albright and Cleveland discarded most of the
pottery material outside the city wall. For this reason, the upper layers of the soil
pile located outside the settlement include a large amount of mixed material (pottery and
small ﬁnds) while the artefacts in the lower levels may be considered contemporary to the
occupation of the city." (p. 193)

At Tissa, worn later fragments of Fine Grey: "Later fragments are small and worn and
consist mostly of isolated pieces, in contrast to the material in the earlier layers."
(p. 195). The rule drawn from it [Int]: "the occurrence of RW fragments or other types of
'Fine Grey Pottery' can be taken as an indication that a given settlement existed prior to
the ﬁrst century BC. This applies also to specimens found in later strata and is true in
most cases for imitation RW as well." (pp. 195–196)

## 4. Arguments

### 4.1 Pre-Periplus exchange

- [Int] "The archaeological ﬁnds described here from the layers of the ﬁrst occupational
  phases of Sumhuram constitute deﬁnite archaeological proof that there were links between
  South Asia and the port city, links that predated the period of trade recounted in the
  Periplus." (p. 198)
- [Int] "RW and Wheeler type 18 must have arrived in Sumhuram prior to the ﬁrst century
  BC." (p. 198)
- [Int] "The ﬁne imported pottery from the layers of the ﬁrst occupation at Sumhuram
  belongs to the latter period of Tissa Phase c1 or, at the latest, to the early Phase c2.
  Phase c1 has been dated to the second century BC. Foreign vessels had reached the South
  Arabian coast by this time, if not earlier" (p. 198)
- [Int, with reported support] "Consequently, they constitute evidence of a pre-Periplus
  trade (Begley 1992; Ray 2006; Schenk 2006), even if fragments have also been found in
  mixed or later layers." (p. 197). Note the premise stated just before: "Assuming that
  this premise holds true for the entire region of southern India and not only for
  Tissamaharama" (p. 197). The argument is conditional on Tissa's production window
  being universal.
- [Int] On absence: "Of course, the absence of evidence from an earlier period does not
  constitute proof of the absence of commercial exchange. Trade might have been conducted
  earlier in perishable goods (Tomber 2008: 16, 54–55)." (p. 198)
- [Int] "Most of the principal hubs were established long before the Periplus documented
  the consolidated network" (p. 200)
- RW redated [Rep, consensus]: "RW was long considered to be a marker only for the
  Indo-Roman trade. It is now widely accepted, however, that RW ﬁrst emerged in the
  third–second centuries BC. The ﬁndings at Tissamaharama conﬁrm this early dating and
  indicate that production was limited (Schenk 2006)." (p. 195)

### 4.2 Critique of Roman-centred interpretation and Wheeler

- "There is a long tradition of studies of the Indian Ocean trade that began in the
  middle of the last century with Wheeler (1951; 1954) and Raschke (1975; 1978). They
  focused almost exclusively, however, on the trade between Roman Egypt and India, which is
  described in detail in the Periplus Maris Erythraei (Casson 1989)." (p. 191)
- Balanced against: "The economic importance of the period spanning the ﬁrst century AD
  should not be dismissed or underestimated" (p. 191)
- Wheeler is also used approvingly: "Sir Mortimer Wheeler also made a distinction between
  the local pottery and imported ceramics produced from this different type of clay in his
  Arikamedu report.11" (p. 194)
- Begley's redating of Arikamedu is credited (p. 191).
- The critique is mild. Wheeler is named as the start of a Roman-centred tradition, not
  argued against point by point.

### 4.3 South Asian agency

- [Int] "Above all, our study demonstrates the active role played by South Asia in trade
  even in this early period." (p. 201)
- [Int] "In fact, a much larger number came from India than was previously thought."
  (p. 201), referring to "the origins of some of the foreign merchants and sailors who
  arrived at Sumhuram".
- [Int] Utilitarian pottery as evidence of people: "If we wish to use ceramics as an
  indicator of the physical presence of Indian sailors in a speciﬁc place, however,
  utilitarian pottery could offer a more reliable gauge. Certainly, such pots may have been
  brought to Sumhuram by Western sailors who had acquired them to replace belongings of
  their own that had been broken, but grafﬁti in Brahmi script ... seem to provide
  incontrovertible proof that their owners were individuals of Indian origin." (p. 200).
  Note the Brahmi graffiti date "at the earliest to the late ﬁrst century BC/early ﬁrst
  century AD", later than the KR I material.
- [Rep: Strauch & Bukharin 2004] "Inscriptions written in a version of Brahmi that was
  current in the second–fourth centuries AD conﬁrm the uninterrupted visits of South Asian
  sailors to cave Hoq on the island of Soqotra" (p. 200)
- South Arabian agency is **not** argued. Sumhuram is treated as a receiver.

### 4.4 Fine wares as gifts or personal belongings, not commerce

[Int] "The scarcity of 'Fine Grey Pottery' and the fact that it represented an
exceptionally ﬁne type of tableware lead to the conclusion that this type of ceramic did
not arrive through ordinary commercial trade. That such objects could have formed part of
the personal belongings of Indian merchants or sailors living in enclaves abroad is a
tempting hypothesis (Tomber 2008: 74), but other as yet unconﬁrmed uses — for instance,
as prestigious gifts suitable for presentation to persons of high rank — should not be
excluded (Schenk 2001: 128; 2006: 138–140)." (p. 200)

### 4.5 Routes, ports as intermediaries, and Muziris

- [Int] "The distribution pattern of true RW and related forms of pottery reﬂects the
  trade routes that traversed India, but the principal route ran along the eastern coast
  from Bengal down to the island of Sri Lanka (Schenk 2006: 130, ﬁg. 3; Tomber 2008: 119,
  ﬁg. 19; Lahiri 1992: 368, map 45). Therefore, although RW originated somewhere in the
  Ganges valley, the pottery would have reached Sumhuram via the southern tip of India.
  The region around Muziris, rather than Barygaza, was the nexus point on the subcontinent
  for Indian Ocean trade at the time of RW." (p. 200)
- The preceding sentences (pp. 199–200): "In the ﬁrst century AD the author of the
  Periplus recorded contacts with two different regions, mentioning ports such as Muziris
  (Pattanam) in the south and Barygaza in the north-west. By contrast — at times of RW —
  Muziris was primarily involved in the trade." The authors write "Muziris (Pattanam)" as
  an identification, without argument.
- Sri Lanka as indirect: "Although Taprobane was at that time only indirectly connected —
  via South India — with the Indian Ocean network (Walburg 2008: 319–342)" (p. 193)
- Pattanam as test case: "Pattanam, where ongoing excavations have brought to light the
  ﬁrst fragments of RW to be found along the Malabar Coast, could add some crucial pieces
  to the picture that is being compiled. Several interim reports have mentioned ﬁnding
  increasing numbers of RW, most recently by Cherian et al. (2011)." (p. 196) [Rep:
  Cherian et al. 2011]
- [Rep: Tomber 2008] "recently two regional sources for the specimens of South Asian
  pottery found in Egypt have been identiﬁed – Gujarat (Tomber, Cartwright & Gupta 2011)
  and Pattanam in Kerala (Tomber 2008: 47, 140–144)." (p. 198)
- Imitation RW as the better tracer [Int]: "In contrast to studies on true RW — only
  trespassing along the east coast of India from somewhere in the north — analysis of the
  southern copy of RW might far better indicate the regions engaged in commerce at this
  time." (p. 200)
- Paddle-impressed ware [Rep: Tomber & Begley 1999; Tomber 2000] at "Sumhuram but also
  Berenike and Myos Hormos, where RW has been found as well ... They are apparently typical
  of regions along the eastern coast of India and in Sri Lanka" (p. 197). Note: "eastern
  coast", not Malabar.

**Caution for the thesis.** The Muziris claim is an inference from (a) a route drawn
along the east coast and (b) the need to round the southern tip. No Sumhuram sherd in the
article is sourced to Malabar. The paddle-impressed sherds are tied to the *east* coast
and Sri Lanka; the one fabric comparison that points to a region (Fig. 2/1) points to
*Gujarat*. The step from "via the southern tip" to "the region around Muziris" is not
supported by any object in this paper.

### 4.6 Overlapping networks

- "a quasi-global network" (p. 193); Indonesia: "there was a high demand in the West for
  plants and related products that were indigenous to certain areas of Indonesia (Ray 2003:
  216)." (p. 198)
- Household pottery could extend the picture "far into the post-Periplus centuries
  (Horton 1996: 454; Ray 2006: 143; Schenk 2007: 70–71; Tomber 2007; 2008: 152–170)."
  (p. 200)

### 4.7 Disagreements with named scholars

- **Yule & Kervran 1993**: their "Indian Red Polished Ware" cooking pots are
  reclassified, following Sedov & Benvenuti (p. 192).
- **Earlier RW provenance studies (unnamed)**: "The origin of RW is still disputed,
  probably due to the failure in the past to differentiate between imported 'true' RW made
  of ﬁne clay and the coarser imitations" and "some scholars have only used samples from
  the southern region of South Asia" (p. 195). Gogte 2001 is endorsed. Magee 2010 is cited
  for an imitation-RW production area, not disputed.
- **Schenk's own earlier view** revised: fn. 15 "The local imitation RW was originally
  thought to be a late development in BRW (Schenk 2006: 132)"; fn. 19 "The group of
  imitation RW ceramics was earlier considered to be one of the key types of Phase c2
  pottery in Tissamaharama (Schenk 2006: 132)." Now dated as contemporary with true RW.
- **Tomber 2008**: her "personal belongings" hypothesis called "tempting" but gift use
  "should not be excluded" (p. 200). A soft qualification.

## 6. Comparisons a historian could build on

Each pair below is a direct Sumhuram–Tissamaharama parallel drawn by the authors. The
Tissa date is from stratigraphy plus radiocarbon; the Sumhuram date is borrowed from
Tissa (and, for 4/1, from coins).

1. **Late true RW: Sumhuram Fig. 3/1–2 ↔ Tissa Fig. 6/4.** Sumhuram: two rims, context
   not stated, some RW "excavated near the surface". Tissa: late group, inward-inclining
   wall, worn slip, "discarded not before late Phase c1 (towards the end of the second
   century BC)". The match is morphological. The Sumhuram RW belongs to the *last*
   product line, not the first. That constrains the earliest Sumhuram contact more tightly
   than "pre-Periplus" does: on this evidence it is late second century BC, not third.

2. **Worn decorated RW base: Sumhuram Fig. 3/3 ↔ five tiny Tissa bases, "at the earliest
   to Phase c2".** Sumhuram: "later stratum". The Tissa comparanda are not illustrated.
   This is the piece that most clearly sits in the first century BC, and it shows RW still
   circulating (or residual) after its production ended.

3. **Wheeler type 18, red-fired: Sumhuram Fig. 4/1 (room A30, BF1, with coins Co334,
   Co337, Co338) ↔ Tissa Fig. 4/2–3 (Schenk 2007: 68, 82, figs 8–9).** The only
   Sumhuram import in the paper with a room, associated numismatic evidence and an inked
   inventory label ("SU11058 / US-159,5", my reading). Two independent clocks, Yashuril
   coins from second-century-BC layers (Sedov 2008c) and the Tissa ceramic date, can be
   checked against each other here, provided the contradiction about its "later context"
   is resolved from Sedov 2008a.

4. **Imitation RW, riveted: one Sumhuram fragment (not illustrated) ↔ riveted RW at Tissa
   (Schenk 2006: 124, fig. 1c).** Repair behaviour in both places. A southern imitation
   was valued enough to rivet at Sumhuram. That is a stronger argument for the southern
   origin of people or goods than true RW, which is northern.

5. **Household assemblage: Sumhuram Fig. 1 ↔ Tissa Fig. 5 (one Phase c1 context).**
   Pairs: 1/1–3 ↔ 5/1–3 (Tissa Form A); 1/4 ↔ 5/11 (Tissa Form I, red-fired, c1); 1/5 ↔
   5/12–15 (storage jar, vertical paddle impressions, Phases a–c1); 1/6 ↔ 5/5 (lid); 1/7
   ↔ 5/4 (flaring bowl, late c1); 1/8–9 ↔ Black Ware; 1/10–12 ↔ 5/7–10 (BRW). The
   authors' own caveat is that the paste does not match, so the comparison shows a shared
   southern canon of forms, not Tissa as source. The firing shift from BRW to all-red,
   which Tissa puts in Phase c1 to c2, gives a datable technological marker that could be
   looked for at Pattanam.

6. **The vertical-versus-horizontal paddle-impression rule.** Vertical in Tissa Phases a
   to c1, horizontal in c2 (p. 197). Sumhuram 1/5 is vertical. This is a cheap test any
   published paddle-impressed sherd from Pattanam, Berenike or Myos Hormos can be put
   through. It is a rule from one site, so it is a hypothesis for Malabar, not a fact.

7. **Gujarat fabric at Sumhuram: Fig. 2/1 ↔ Tissa Fig. 2/2–3 (bulbous jar rims, red slip,
   Phases a–b).** The caption calls it Tissa Form F; the text says there is "no direct
   parallel". Its fine clay is likened to Gujarat "RPW", and that group is found "only ...
   in the later layers of Sumhuram". This piece cuts against the paper's own
   Muziris-not-Barygaza conclusion and deserves a separate look.

8. **The Muziris claim as a thing to test.** The paper says "Muziris (Pattanam)" was the
   nexus "at the time of RW" (p. 200), but no object in it is sourced to Malabar. It also
   notes RW at Pattanam (Cherian et al. 2011) and Pattanam as a source of South Asian
   pottery in Egypt (Tomber 2008: 47, 140–144). A thesis could test the claim directly by
   asking whether Pattanam's RW is true or imitation, early or late by the Tissa criteria
   (wall profile, slip, section colour). The paper supplies the criteria but not the
   Pattanam data.
