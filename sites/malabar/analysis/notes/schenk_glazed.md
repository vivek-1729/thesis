## 2. Method material

### 2.1 What was found, and where

**[Documented]** p. 59 (PDF 3): "Up to now, altogether 38 fragments of glazed vessels have been found within the excavations carried out on the Tissamaharama Citadel since 1992 (Fig. 2–5; 6 a–c, e–k)."

The 38 equals the number of catalogue entries in Fig. 2–5 and Fig. 6 a–c, e–k (33 from Tissa 3, 5 from Tissa 1). Fig. 6 d (lustre ware) is excluded. Four entries are each "Two body sherds" (Fig. 3 e, 3 h, 5 d, 6 a), so the count is of entries or vessels-in-a-context, not strictly of sherds. See §3.3 for a count that does not reconcile.

Excavation areas. **[Documented]** p. 60 (PDF 4): "The published chronology based mainly on the excavation areas Tissa 1 and Tissa 2 and has meanwhile been enhanced and re-checked at Tissa 3 where the investigation is still going on". Fn. 5: "An upgraded pottery sequence will be published soon after having reached bedrock at Tissa 3."

Taphonomy of Tissa 3. **[Documented]** p. 60: "As opposed to Tissa 1, erosion has completely washed away all layers later than the second half of the 6th century, and find assemblages of later date are preserved in a few deeper pits only at Tissa 3."

### 2.2 Fabric (body)

**[Documented]** p. 59: "The porous and sandy paste is commonly pale yellow (Munsell 2.5Y 8 / 3), beside two pieces (Fig. 2 e; 3 i) with a slightly pinkish texture (Munsell 7.5YR 8 / 2). The supposed fragments of Islamic dating (Fig. 6 f–i) have a dense and a rather soft, chalky texture tending to a white shade (Munsell 7.5Y 8 / 2)."

(The catalogue gives the Islamic sherds as "white (Munsell 2.5Y 8/2)", not 7.5Y as in the running text. Small inconsistency in the source.)

**[Documented]** p. 60: "The paste of such fragments differs clearly from the sandy texture of the ones from Tissa 3". And of Fig. 6 k: "A further sherd with a bluish shade on a rather deteriorated glaze from Tissa 1 of phase h has the same sandy paste comparable to the older fragments. It could well be a Sasanian and therefore residual in its context (Fig. 6 k)."

So the working distinction between the early group and the Islamic group is fabric: porous sandy pale-yellow paste versus dense chalky white paste. This is a hand-specimen (visual/tactile) distinction. No petrographic or chemical analysis of the Tissamaharama sherds is stated anywhere in the article.

### 2.3 Glaze colour and weathering

**[Documented]** p. 60: "Turquoise-blue glaze is rare, but to see on all the few specimens from Tissa 1 (Fig. 6 f–i) with a dating not earlier than phase h (8th / 9th century A. D.)."

**[Documented]** p. 60: "Some fragments from Tissa 3 also have a blue tinted glaze (Fig. 3 b, c, k; 4 g) and two others were altered by accidental fire (Fig. 4 h, i). The others, up to 2007 comprising 27 fragments and exclusively found at Tissa 3, have a green or a more or less deteriorated golden-yellow or whitish-yellow or brownish-yellow glaze (Fig. 2 a–e; 3 a, d–i; 4 a–f, k; 5; 6 a–c, e)."

**[Reported: Kennet, Haerinck] + [Interpretation]** p. 61 (PDF 5): "The descriptions of the glaze often show a clear correspondence to glazes observed at Tissamaharama. This applies especially to the iridescent deterioration of the glaze but also to fabric of the clay. This weathering is also typical for later Sasanian and Islamic glazed pottery according to D. Kennet (Kennet 2002: 154). The colour of glaze varies to a great extent (Haerinck 1983: 43) and Kennet rightly stresses the importance of vessel forms for reliable distinction. Nevertheless, as observed in Tissamaharama, he also points to an apparent difference at least between the weathered colour of the earlier glazed wares and the Islamic sherds."

This passage concedes that the iridescent weathering is not diagnostic of date and that, per Kennet, form matters more than colour. Most of the Tissamaharama pieces are undiagnostic body sherds.

### 2.4 Alkaline glaze and Mesopotamian origin

**[Reported: Vogelsang, Haerinck]** p. 60: "Glazed pottery, namely alkaline glaze, has its roots in Mesopotamia appearing as early as the 8th/7th century B. C., but apparently it becomes more popular after post-Achaemenian times (Vogelsang 1985: 158–161)." And: "E. Haerinck describes glazed pottery in his 'Phase ancienne' (250–150 B. C.) of the Parthian period as characteristic for the Western Iran Zone and Southern Iran bordering the Persian Gulf (Haerinck 1983: 241 carte 4: Zones I, II; IX). The vessel forms have their sources in the Hellenistic world."

The Tissamaharama glaze is not stated to have been analysed as alkaline. "Alkaline glaze" is asserted of the class, not tested on these sherds.

**[Reported: de Paepe / Rutten / Vrydaghs / Haerinck 2003]** p. 61: "At Gulf sites, glazed wares are considered to originate in Mesopotamia. At ed-Dur, Parthian glazed pottery had been analyzed and the result at least matched the only further available analysis conducted on southern Mesopotamian pottery from 5th to 2nd millennium B. C. (de Paepe / Rutten / Vrydaghs / Haerinck 2003: 224)."

**[Reported: Højlund / Andersen]** p. 61: "A few pottery wasters at Qal'at al-Bahrain may also indicate an independent production of glazed pottery outside of the Mesopotamian region" (fn. 10: "Qal'at al-Bahrain: Højlund / Andersen 1994: 370 and, fig. 1815–1816; p. 478.").

**Identification by consultation.** p. 60: "No doubt the sherds from Tissamaharama match the description of glazed pottery from the Persian Gulf region and Mesopotamia". Fn. 6: "I would like to thank Alexander V. Sedov who has supported this identification. He knows such pottery well from Qana (Yemen) and Sumhuram-Khor Rori (Oman)."

So the Mesopotamian provenance of the Tissamaharama sherds rests on (i) visual match to published descriptions, (ii) Sedov's opinion, and (iii) an analysis of ed-Dur material compared with much earlier Mesopotamian pottery. Nothing was analysed from Sri Lanka.

### 2.5 Form and typological dating

**[Documented]** p. 60: "Of the thirty-four sherds with yellowish-greenish glaze from Tissa 3, three rim sherds (Fig. 2 a–c), a handle (Fig. 2 d) and three base fragments (Fig. 2 e, 3 a–b) are of diagnostic value. They represent seven ascertained individual vessels."

**[Interpretation]** p. 61: "An overview of published pottery for Mesopotamian and Arabian sites provided only a few direct parallels especially with regard to the three rim sherds of a jar or amphora with a flaring rim and softly grooved lip (Fig. 2 a–c). Two of the rims show the fitting of the handle below the rim area and to the neck. This applies at least to the amphora or jar with Greek tradition."

p. 61: "A comparison with the published assemblages of glazed pottery in Mesopotamia and neighbouring regions proves rather difficult given the fragmentary state of the specimen in Tissamaharama. Rim shapes are not easily to trace among illustrations of mainly complete vessels."

Parallels cited for the rims (pp. 61–62):
- "The best analogies to the rim shapes from Tissamaharama were observed among the published material for Qal'at al-Bahrain, period VIIIa and Uruk-Warka with a dating at least to the 2nd and 1st century B. C." (fn. 15: Lombard / Kervran 1993: 137, "period VIII a (middle 3rd century – beginning 1st century B. C."; fn. 16: Finkbeiner 1991a, 1991b, 1993; Strommenger 1967).
- Bahrain jugs: "the rims of two jugs or 'small bottles' introduced for a suggested 'Bahrain horizon' or 'Middle Tylos-1' (1st century B. C. – early 1st century A. D.) are also quite near" (Herling / Salles 1993: 173, fig. 6, 34. 40). Schenk notes "the drawings are quite sketchy".
- Seleucia: "Two glazed jugs from Seleucia but of later Parthian context also have affinities" (Debevoise 1934: 93 fig. 256, 260).
- Ras al-Khaimah: a pilgrim flask "from a 'superficial' deposit near Ghalilah tomb 1 ... identified as 'undoubtedly Parthian' and described as a parallel to a vessel in a coin-dated burial at Dura Europos to 52–37 B. C." (fn. 17: De Cardi 1984: 201–2; p. 208, fig. 2, 12).
- Dura-Europos: "Some jugs from Dura-Europos show affinities to the rim shapes from Tissamaharama, but for the pottery at large a terminus ante quem of 256 A. D. is possible only (Toll 1943: 5–6)."
- Haerinck: "Similarities are also noticed among the glazed Parthian pottery compiled by Haerinck for his phase ancienne but as well among the indeterminate phase ancienne, moyenne et récente."

**Negative comparison (ed-Dur and late Parthian ridged rims).** p. 62: "Of greater importance may rather be the comparison with the pottery from ed-Dur that had been deserted until the second half of the 1st B. C. when it was re-founded (Haerinck 2003: 200). Interestingly, all the published jugs or jars apparently are confined to rim shapes having a distinct outer ridge akin to the lip types A–C at Dura Europos. Ridged rims are also exemplified for Kervran's phase IX b and Højlund's phase V c–d at Qala´at al-Bahrain ... both phases belong to the late Parthian period just as Mleiha Per. IIIB with similar jugs ... BI-Ware from Failaka seems also be confined to such rim shapes. Decoration such as zigzag on the shoulders, typical for Haerinck's phase récente and also present at ed-Dur and among the BI-Ware, was not noted on any of the sherds from Tissamaharama."

The argument is by absence: the Tissamaharama rims lack the ridged lip and zigzag decoration of the late Parthian (1st–early 3rd c. AD) Gulf material, so they should be earlier.

**Bases (fishplates).** p. 62: "The two base sherds from Tissamaharama bear concentric grooves inside on the bottom. Such grooves are mainly characteristic for so-called fish plates but also appear on other bowls and plates (Hannestad 1983: 28–32 and fn 277). According to Haerinck, the form disappears at least before the middle of the 1st century A. D. (Haerinck 1983: 44). Fish plates are said to be abundant among the 1st century A. D. pottery of ed-Dur (Salles 1990: 316). However, a possible further chronological classification depends but on complete vessels. A bottom fragment with concentric grooves is also known from Sumhuram, 'covered with greenish gold, originally green, glaze' (Sedov / Benvenuti 2002: 186)."

Note: the text says "two base sherds", earlier "three base fragments (Fig. 2 e, 3 a–b)". Haerinck's end date (before mid-1st c. AD) and Salles' report of abundance in 1st-century AD ed-Dur pull in different directions; Schenk does not resolve this.

**Production date conclusion.** p. 62: "In summary, the dating range of the vessels imported to Tissamaharama points to the 1st century B. C. up to the first half of the 1st century A. D. Nevertheless, within the 2nd century A. D. they were finally discarded into Sri Lankan soil. Like all exceptional vessels, coming from afar, it is unlikely that they fell out of use just after their arrival."

### 2.6 Stratigraphic dating at Tissamaharama: the principle

**[Documented, methodological statement]** p. 59: "As a basic principle, imported or rare wares as well as small finds like coins are not considered to be reliable indicators for dating due to their limited number (Schenk 2001: 63, 2006: 136). This completely supports Chiumei Ho, who stated although with regard to Chinese pottery but regardless appropriate: 'too often Chinese ceramics are relied upon to date the cultural layers of archaeological sites even though it might be better to use the sites to date the ceramics' (Ho 1994: 192)."

p. 59–60: "At Tissamaharama, the glazed pottery has been assessed within its stratigraphical context and the associated pottery. A local pottery sequence has been established on the site."

The phase dates (c1 = 2nd c. BC; c2 = 1st c. BC; d2 = 2nd c. AD; e = 3rd c. AD; f = 4th c.–450; g1 = 450–6th c.; g = 6th/7th c.; h = 8th/9th c.; i = mixed upper surface) are taken from the local pottery sequence published in Schenk 2001. **How the phases are absolutely dated (radiocarbon, coins, or cross-dating) is not stated in this article.**

Phase f is "4th century A. D." in the running text (p. 60) but "4th cent. – 450 A. D." in the catalogue.

### 2.7 How phase determines the label "Parthian" vs "Sasanian"

**[Documented]** p. 60: "Five sherds (Fig. 3 a–b; 4 f, h–i) stem from layers of phase f (4th century A. D.), but fourteen belong to phase e – 3rd century A. D. – (Fig. 2 b, d–e; 3 c, f–i; 4 a–c, k; 5 a. d) and therefore identified as of Sasanian origin. One fragment derived from a transition layer of phase d 2 / e (Fig. 3 k)."

p. 60: "Then, four sherds (Fig. 2 a, c; 3 d–e) emerged in features used in phase d2 (2nd century A. D.) apparently backfilled for construction levelling at the beginning of phase e (3rd century A. D.). However, the pottery within the refilled soil still belongs to the phase d2."

The words "and therefore identified as of Sasanian origin" show that the attribution was made from the date of the context, not from the sherd. The catalogue follows this: d2 sherds are "Parthian", phase e to g1 sherds "Parthian / Sasanian". Fig. 2 b is a rim of the same type as Fig. 2 a and c but is labelled "Parthian / Sasanian" because it came from phase e.

The eight phase g1 entries (Fig. 4 d, e, g; 5 b, c; 6 a, b, e) are in the catalogue but are not mentioned in the find-circumstances paragraph on p. 60.

### 2.8 Distinguishing from local wares

Glazed wares are not confused with local wares in this article; the problem of telling imports from local wares is discussed instead for "Red Polished Ware" and for "Hellenistic" pottery.

**[Interpretation]** p. 66 (PDF 10): "The mere mentioning of 'wares' is useless and not significant. The study of stratified find assemblages only allows an understanding of a changing pattern when taking into account details of fabrication (wares) in combination with vessel forms."

p. 66: "A good surface treatment but is also noted among the locally produced pottery depending on vessel form and period like sherds of a water pot found in a pit of phase h (Fig. 7 f) or in layers of the 1st century B. C. (Fig. 7 g)."

p. 69 (PDF 13): "So-called Hellenistic pottery is reported from Anuradhapura (Bouzek / Deraniyagala 1985). Meanwhile, such fragments with stand ring have been also discovered at Tissamaharama with a dating to the 2nd/1st century B. C. However, the paste is certainly identical to a group of vessel shapes with a Western affinity like RW and Wheeler types 10 and 18. This entire group is certainly locally made in India."

**Security.** 2 a and 2 c carry the article's whole "Parthian" claim. Their context is a backfill: features "used in phase d2 ... apparently backfilled for construction levelling at the beginning of phase e" (p. 60). The deposit was laid down at the start of phase e, so the sherds were redeposited. The date is a stratigraphic terminus for discard, not a date of use. The rim profiles allow typological comparison, but Schenk herself says direct parallels are few and "Rim shapes are not easily to trace among illustrations of mainly complete vessels" (p. 61). **Partly secure:** typology is checkable from the drawings; the absolute phase date depends on Schenk 2001, which is not reproduced here.

Note that 2 a was excavated in 2006 (label '06). This fits p. 58: the pottery was found "then (since 2006) even to Parthian times". 2 d and 2 e were excavated in 2002.

The 33 Tissa 3 entries plus 5 Tissa 1 entries make 38. But p. 60 also says "Of the thirty-four sherds with yellowish-greenish glaze from Tissa 3". ## 4. Arguments

### 4.1 Mesopotamian glazed wares reached Sri Lanka in the Parthian period

**[Interpretation]** Abstract, p. 57: "Stratified finds from Tissamaharama, Sri Lanka, now confirm the already assumed occurrence of Parthian glazed pottery in South Asia. Comparisons lead to a Mesopotamian origin dated to the 1st century B. C. and beginning 1st century A. D. Within the 2nd century A. D. they were finally discarded into Sri Lankan soil. Such a dating affirms earlier comments on Indian Ocean trade activities preceding the testimonial of the Periplus as archaeologically evidenced by Rouletted Ware originating from India."

p. 70 (Summary): "At Tissamaharama, they occurred in soil containing pottery of the 2nd century A. D. used to backfill a feature used in the same period. Persian glazed pottery found in South Asia may therefore represent not only Sasanian or Islamic contacts but also of Parthian ones."

Note the gap between the abstract ("confirm") and the evidence: four entries from one backfill context, and the label "Parthian" assigned from phase.

### 4.2 The route: Red Sea and South Arabia, not the Gulf

**[Interpretation, building on Rutten 2007]** Abstract: "The distribution pattern of both pottery groups demonstrate the importance of the ports on the South Arabian coasts as main terminals leading to North western India and South India thus omitting the direct participation of ports in the Persian Gulf at that time. This was already suggested by another study on Sigillata at that sites."

p. 64: "Apparently, the Gulf routing had lost its relevance with regard to direct oversea connection, but trade still continued via the South Arabian ports."

**[Reported: Rutten 2007]** p. 64: "Based on a study of Sigillata found at ed-Dur, K. Rutten had recently suggested that Mesopotamia was not directly involved to the Indian Ocean trade and was connected only through South Arabian ports (Rutten 2007). She points to the important role of the south Arabian ports such as Qana and Sumhuram where both trade routes conjoin taking over the goods to and from India."

p. 64: "The occurrence of Italian Sigillata and of Eastern Sigillata B-1 (ESB-1) at ed-Dur but is significant. Both Sigillata groups are common along the Red Sea route and on the South Arabian coast, though being else absent at the Persian Gulf, indicate that ed-Dur obtained goods from Roman Egypt through the South Arabian ports either by sea or overland. This scenario is furthermore supported by coins and pottery from Hadramawt at ed-Dur (Haerinck 1998: 284–286; Rutten 2007: 19) and on the other hand by Mesopotamian glazed pottery at Qana and Sumhuram."

p. 70: "The South Arabian ports served as reloading points for goods such as the Mesopotamian glazed vessels, most probably with some content. In this way, the former routing through the Persian Gulf was indeed omitted as reported then in the middle of the 1st century A. D. by the Periplus and confirmed by recent studies on Sigillata found at ed-Dur."

Rouletted Ware as the control. p. 65: "The distribution pattern of the very characteristic 'Rouletted Ware' (RW) and related vessels as Wheeler type 10 also confirms the predominance of the Red Sea routing. They occur at Berenike, Coptos, Myos Hormos (Quseir al-Qadim) and at Qana and Sumhuram but are not traced at the Gulf sites (Schenk 2006: 131, fig. 4)." And p. 70: "Acknowledging the end of its production in the 1st century B. C., it shows that the trans-shipment centres like Qana and Sumhuram already worked in that time passing the goods from Mesopotamia."

**Gulf sites.** [Reported: Whitehouse] p. 64: "Ed-Dur itself contains 40% of pottery coming from that region (Whitehouse 2000: 122)." Dibba [Interpretation from Jasim 2006], pp. 64–65: "Although within the reach of the Periplus trade route, vessels of Sigillata and amphorae seem absent. A supply from Dibba as intermediary is therefore unlikely."

Sumhuram [Reported]. Fn. 9: "30 percent of the tableware belong to glazed wares at Sumhuram: Sedov / Benvenuti 2002: 184."

Failaka [Reported: Hannestad] p. 61: "'by the first half of the 1st century A. D. it had become the most popular fine ware'" (fn. 9: Hannestad 1983, 15).

### 4.3 Chronology relative to Roman trade

p. 63: "Myos Hormos was as well known as a great harbour already at about 116 B. C. (Peacock / Blue 2006: 3)." Berenike "afterwards enlarged when sailors under Roman rule had taken over the profitable trade at 30 B. C. (Sidebotham 1996: 288)." "Since Hellenistic times, the actual entrepreneurs of the trade were South Arabs, Indians, Chinese and other 'Easterners' visiting and residing in the Mediterranean region (Sidebotham 1996: 299; Tomber 2004)."

p. 64: "Already around the beginning of the 1st century A. D., Strabo had mentioned the port of Muziris situated down at the western coast of South India. However, the written log certainly only registered an already established sea passage."

p. 70–71: "The Indian Ocean trade beginning in Hellenistic times, continues into the first century A. D., and followed a route from Alexandria to ports on the South Arabian coast and the Horn of Africa. From there ships sailed either on the upper routing or the southern passage to India."

On coins [Reported: MacDowall, Berghaus], p. 70: "Besides pottery, Early Roman coins in India are often used as evidence for early trade. Yet it has been conclusively argued that those coins came to India sometime later than their time of issue (fn. 66: MacDowall 1991: esp. 145–147; also suggested by Berghaus ...). However, the distribution pattern in fact reflects above-mentioned regions as evidenced already earlier by amphorae indicating continuity (MacDowall 1996: 90, figs. 4; 8; 9)."

### 4.4 Sri Lanka's access through intermediaries

**[Reported: Weerakkody, Walburg, Faller]** p. 64: "The southern Pandyan kingdoms served as intermediaries on the maritime trade, either due to their well-established international commercial connections (Weerakkody 1997: 49) or rather because they did not allow a direct participation (Walburg 1991: 165). Direct contact between Sri Lanka and the West is reported not before the 2nd half of the 5th century or at least not before the end of 5th century A. D. (Walburg 2008: 319–342)."

p. 70: "Following ancient written sources, it was not before the 1st century A. D. that Romans had direct knowledge of Sri Lanka or even contact, limited only to the northern coastal region. The island was nevertheless connected to the West-East trade via South India and middlemen such as the Pandyans."

Fn. 32: "This routing seems still in use at least in the 4th century A. D.: Walburg 2008: 319–342."

The physical route of the glazed vessels from South Arabia to Tissamaharama is not established. The article argues only that it was via South Arabian ports and then "via South India and middlemen".

### 4.6 The Red Polished Ware critique (the article's other main argument)

**[Interpretation]** p. 65: "Following a rather simplified assessment, this pottery is thought to emerge in the 1st century B. C. in India and commonly dated to the crucial first centuries A. D. A distortion of scientific and subsequently historical interpretations seems apparent with regard to the historical evaluation of both trade routes."

p. 66, against Rao on Amreli: "This dating bases on RPW found together with a coin of the 1st century B. C. at Amreli (Rao 1966: 16). In my opinion, after having looked thoroughly into the report, there is no evidence for dating the site before the 3rd / 4th century A. D."

### 4.7 Disagreements with named scholars

- **Charvát**: proposed "an introduction of glazed wares to Sri Lanka in the 3rd / 4th century A. D., 'probably by merchants of Sassanian Persia' (Charvát 1993a: 89)" (p. 58). Schenk pushes this earlier, to the Parthian period.
- **Ratnayake / the Mahavamsa** on the Jetavana: "Although the chronicle Mahavamsa states the foundation of the dagoba by King Mahasena in the 3rd century A. D., the Jetavana treasure and the actual existing structures apparently belong to a later period" (p. 58).
- **Rao 1966** on Amreli: she rejects the 1st c. BC date (p. 66).
- **Shajan / Selvakumar / Tomber 2005** and **Shinde / Gupta / Rajgor 2002**: they propose a Sasanian provenance for the glaze (p. 58). Schenk implies that Parthian is now possible but does not explicitly re-assign their sherds.
- **Authors who use RPW as an early-contact marker** (fn. 35, 37: Yule 1993; Salles 1995; Kervran / Hiebert 1991; Haerinck / Phillips / Potts / Stevens 1993; Kervran 1996; Zarins 2001; Davidde / Petriaggi / Williams 2004; Hardy-Guilbert 2005): their statements "either follow an outdated reception of the renowned Indian site of Arikamedu ... or a simplified classification" (pp. 65–66). Sedov / Benvenuti 2002 are credited with "Already realizing the problems of RPW".
- **Suresh 2004**: fn. 58, "The presented example from Alagankulam on p. 112 plate 12 certainly is a 'misfired' rim of RW".
- **Nagaswamy 1995** on Karur "Arretine": "only one sherd with characteristics for Sigillata such as an egg-and-dart moulding. This decoration is common rather to younger Sigillata" (p. 69).
- **Horton 1997**: fn. 68, his RPW reference "now has to be revised".
- **Potts 1993** on Thaj: not a disagreement with Potts, who "does not refer to them as such", but with the label "rouletted" (p. 65).

## 6. Observations a historian could build on

1. **The Parthian date rests on four catalogue entries from one kind of deposit, a backfill.** Fig. 2 a, 2 c, 3 d and 3 e come from "features used in phase d2 ... apparently backfilled for construction levelling at the beginning of phase e" (p. 60). Everything else is phase e or later. The arrival date is not established. What is established is that some sandy-fabric glazed vessels were broken and discarded in Sri Lanka by the end of phase d2 on the local sequence. Schenk's own principle (p. 59) is that imports should be dated by the site, and by that principle the site dates these sherds to the 2nd century AD. The 1st c. BC–early 1st c. AD date comes from typological parallels for three rims.

2. **"Parthian" and "Sasanian" are context labels, not fabric labels.** "fourteen belong to phase e ... and therefore identified as of Sasanian origin" (p. 60). The same rim type is "Parthian" in phase d2 (2 a, 2 c) and "Parthian / Sasanian" in phase e (2 b). Taken together, the whole early group is one fabric found across phases d2 to g1, plus 6 c in phase h and 6 k residual. That pattern fits long curation or redeposition of a small number of vessels. The catalogue records reuse: seven early-fabric entries are "used as tool" (3 a, 4 a, 4 d, 4 g, 4 k, 5 d, 6 b), as is the Islamic 6 h. This bears directly on how any Pattanam glaze is dated.

3. **Pattanam is only a reported mention.** Schenk records the Pattanam glazed sherds and the attribution by Shajan, Selvakumar and Tomber 2005: 70 to a "Sasanian provenance" (p. 58). She does not examine them. Her argument opens the possibility that early-fabric (sandy, pale yellow, green or golden glaze) sherds at Pattanam are Parthian rather than Sasanian. The test it implies is checkable: Pattanam's turquoise or green glaze should be split by fabric, sandy pale-yellow versus dense chalky white (p. 59), before it is dated. For Muziris this matters because Parthian-period glaze would be contemporary with the Periplus, while Sasanian glaze would not.

4. **Mantai was already described as "Parthian" glaze, without stratigraphy.** Fn. 1: "Mantai: Wijeyapala / Prickett 1984 ... Charvát 1993b ... both described as 'Parthian' glaze." The Wijeyapala / Prickett reference is missing from the bibliography. Mantai is the obvious Sri Lankan counterpart to the Malabar ports, and this is a lead worth following.

5. **The route argument is a three-way distribution argument that can be extended to Malabar.** Three ware groups are used: Mesopotamian glaze (Gulf sites, South Arabia, Sri Lanka), Rouletted Ware (Red Sea, South Arabia, India, Sri Lanka, not the Gulf), and Italian Sigillata / ESB-1 (Red Sea, South Arabia, ed-Dur, Arikamedu, not elsewhere in the Gulf). Read together, South Arabia (Qana, Sumhuram) is the node where the groups meet. A Malabar port should carry the same Red Sea signature if it was a Red Sea terminal. The Periplus distinction between the Barygaza and Malabar courses could be tested ceramically on this basis.

6. **RPW is unreliable as a date marker for the Malabar coast.** Schenk argues that much published "RPW" is medieval, that the Arikamedu cooking-pot types are Phase H (11th/12th–14th/15th c.), and that RPW in its typical sprinkler form is not found before 600 AD at Tissamaharama (fn. 41). Kennet 2007: 99 is cited for continuous use to the 8th century. Any candidate site dated to the Periplus period on RPW should be treated as undated. This fits the CLAUDE.md finding that every Kerala candidate is a documented later port with no first-century evidence.

7. **Absolute dating of the Tissamaharama phases is not in this article.** Phase dates come from Schenk 2001 and a promised "upgraded pottery sequence" (fn. 5). Anyone using these sherds as a chronological anchor must go to Schenk 2001 and any later Tissamaharama publication to see how phases c1–h are dated.

8. **The Dressel 2–4 at Tissamaharama is asserted without context** (p. 71, fn. 71, identification by Tomber). It is the only Roman find in the article tied to the Parthian-period argument. Its phase should be obtained from Tomber's publication of the Tissamaharama amphorae before it is used.

9. **Anuradhapura Citadel has a 14C-dated context (49) with "Sasanian" pale blue-green glaze in the 3rd century AD** (Deraniyagala 1992: 713; Deraniyagala / Abeyratne 2000: 772 table 1, reported on p. 58). Coningham 2006 worries that early-period turquoise sherds are intrusive. Taken together, the Sri Lankan record has a radiocarbon-linked case near Tissamaharama's phase e. Whether the context-49 sherd is sandy-fabric or chalky-fabric is not stated.

10. **Unexamined details visible in the plates.** Fig. 5 d shows a raised horizontal ridge that the catalogue does not describe. Fig. 6 b has "grooved vertical decoration", the only decorated early sherd. Fig. 3 k has a stoneware-like pale brown fabric unlike the rest. Fig. 7 g, a phase c2 (1st c. BC) local water pot, carries an untranscribed graffito. The excavation-year labels show finds from 1998 to 2007, with the "Parthian" rim 2 a found in 2006, consistent with p. 58.
