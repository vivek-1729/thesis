# Tomber 2008, *Indo-Roman Trade: From Pots to Pepper*: reading notes, PDF pages 111–220

Source text: `analysis/text/tomber2008.txt` (OCR text layer). PDF: `readings/Tomber - Indo-Roman trade  from pots to pepper.pdf` (220 PDF pages).
Every page of PDF 111–220 was read in the text layer. Figures, tables and the endnote table were rendered and checked against the page images (renders in `analysis/renders/tomber_p*.png`). The key Pattanam and Muziris passages (PDF 142, 144) were also checked against the page image.

Conventions used below:
- **p. N** = printed page number. PDF page is given as "PDF N".
- Quotations are verbatim. Obvious OCR slips (e.g. "Megalfthic", "Bliny", "PME 58-5") have been silently corrected **only where I checked the page image** (PDF 142 reads "Pliny" and "PME 53-5"; PDF 144 reads "c. 1 km²"). Elsewhere, OCR noise that does not affect meaning has been normalised: broken words rejoined, stray characters removed.
- **[T-fact]** = Tomber states as fact. **[T-reports X]** = Tomber reports another scholar's view or data. **[T-interp]** = Tomber's own interpretation or argument.

**Important scope warning.** This half of the book has **no ware-description chapter and no plates of individual sherds**. The pottery typology chapter (Chapter 2: amphorae, sigillata, Indian wares, South Arabian wares, Aksumite wares) falls inside PDF 1–110, and so does Chapter 3 (the Red Sea ports). The index puts these at printed pp. 38–54 (see §1.3). The only figures in my range are four maps/drawings (Figs 18–21) and two tables (Tables 2–3). Sections 2 and 3 below are therefore built from what Chapters 4 (end), 5 and 6 say in passing. They are thinner than the brief anticipated, and that thinness is a fact about the book, not an omission.

## 2. Method material (identification, imports versus imitations, residuality, dating)

None of this range sets out fabric descriptions, inclusions, slips or rim typologies. That is all in Ch. 2 (part 1). What follows is every methodological statement made in passing in PDF 111–220, grouped for teaching.

### 2.1 Identification depends on how easy a fabric is to recognise, and that biases counts

- **Campanian fabric is easy to spot, so it is over-represented.** "The Campanian wine amphora is the single most common type throughout the Indian Ocean. In part this reflects the ease with which the clay fabric can be identified, but also the quantity of Campanian vessels travelling from west to east between the late first century BC and the first century AD and the magnitude of the Campanian wine industry before the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79." (p. 156, PDF 158) [T-interp]
- **Koan amphorae are hard to identify, so they may be over-counted.** "Arikamedu is the only site where Koan amphorae constitute a large proportion of the assemblage. Given the difficulty in identifying them, their quantity may be slightly overestimated; nevertheless at present the findings are unparalleled elsewhere in India." (p. 150, PDF 152) [T-interp on Will's figures]
- **Undecorated Aksumite sherds:** "Despite the unprepossessing appearance of undecorated Aksumite sherds, making them hard to identify, both undecorated and decorated ones have been recorded outside Aksumite contexts." (p. 163, PDF 165). Also: "The relatively few Aksumite finds in India are likely to increase substantially as they become better understood and more readily identified." (p. 164, PDF 166)
- **Egyptian coarse wares:** these "are unlikely to have been exported systematically and may have been the personal possessions of sailors and merchants. They should therefore have special potential for understanding routes between Egypt and India. Unfortunately at present their recognition may be uneven and their distribution pattern outside Egypt unreliable." (p. 160, PDF 162) [T-interp]
- **Manufacturing technique as a diagnostic, and the problem of unpublished technique:** "Further support comes from the Keralan organically gouged CRSW found in Egypt. Elsewhere in the Indian Ocean it is difficult to trace this distinctive type since manufacturing techniques are not published. Organic Black Ware from Gujarat is easier to trace by shape and has been identified at Aila, Myos Hormos and Berenike, but not in South India." (p. 155, PDF 157) [T-fact/T-interp]
- **Surface treatment as a Kerala marker (Tomber's own identification):** "among the CRSW were sherds with the distinctive internal wiping marks seen at Berenike and Myos Hormos, but not elsewhere in India outside Kerala." (p. 142, PDF 144; checked on render) [T-fact, first person]. The text does not reconcile "internal wiping marks" here with "organically gouged" on p. 155. They are probably the same feature described twice, but Tomber does not say so.
- **The source of some CRSW is unknown:** "At the same time, the source of some CRSW found in Egypt is unknown and could come from central or North India." (p. 155, PDF 157)

### 2.2 Misidentification and re-identification: the main lesson of Ch. 5

Tomber repeatedly corrects published identifications. These are good teaching cases.
- **Torpedo jars mistaken for Roman amphorae:** "Many amphora sherds in India previously thought to be Roman have been shown to be Mesopotamian Torpedo jars." (p. 167, PDF 169; OCR "eeoubugly" = "previously", inferred from sense). And: "Already the recognition of Mesopotamian Torpedo jars has negatively impacted on the quantity of Roman sherds known from India, altered the distribution of Early Roman amphorae and increased what we know of trade via the Gulf." (p. 171, PDF 173). On Gujarat: "The identification of Torpedo jars as the most common of the three types overturns established patterns and underlines the importance of trade via the Gulf and Parthian/Sasanian influence in the region." (p. 126, PDF 128)
- **"Roman amphorae" in Bengal and Orissa that are not Roman:** "Roman amphorae have been reported on from Manikpatna (Gupta 1997: 353), Karnaji (Sengupta 1998: pl. 1) and Tamluk (Jahan 2006: 71). On examination, all are instead post-Roman in date: those from Tamluk and Manikpatna are salt-glazed vessels, while the Karnaji one is an Islamic earthenware jar. Jahan has also demonstrated that so-called amphorae from Chandraketugarh are local (ibid.: 72, fig. 2.7)." (p. 129, PDF 131) [T-fact, re-examination; Jahan reported]
- **African Red Slip reclassified as Indian Rouletted Ware:** "The presence of African Red Slip Ware at Alagankulam has been extensively cited in support of a Late Roman date, but it is now accepted as Indian RW (Sridhar 2005: 26-7)." (p. 139, PDF 141). This is a direct lesson in how a misidentified ware can manufacture a false date.
- **Unverifiable reports:** "Egyptian pots were noted by Vogel in 1952 (Casson 1989: 236), but since they were not illustrated cannot be substantiated." (p. 129, PDF 131). Kaveripattinam: "Despite reports of Roman amphorae (Rao et al. 1995-6), none have been located by this writer or by other archaeologists currently working in the region (S. Vasanthi, pers. comm.)." (p. 138, PDF 140)
- **Provenance of a sherd in a store:** "A sherd of LR1 in the M.S. Baroda University storeroom came from a tin labelled 'Broach' (Bharuch) that contained finds from a number of Mehta's finds, so the site attribution to Bharuch is only tentative." (p. 125, PDF 127). Shinde et al. are quoted describing "A thick well-fired fragment of a jar, possibly a Mediterranean amphora ..." (2002: 74) (p. 125). A tentative identification is left tentative.
- **Lost or unillustrated objects:** an Arikamedu intaglio "reported to bear a portrait of Augustus; it was taken to Hanoi, but although oft-cited was never illustrated and is now lost." (p. 133, PDF 135). Bengal: "Three inscriptions may be genuine imported finds, although they are no longer available for inspection (S. Jahan, D. Chakrabarti, pers. comm.)." (p. 129, PDF 131)
- **Script is not provenance:** "Greek, however, need not signal an import since it was used by Indians, particularly in the north-west where there are several Ashokan inscriptions in Greek and Aramaic (Thapar 2002: 179-83), as well as on imitated Greek coins in western India (Cribb 2005: 9)." (p. 129, PDF 131)

### 2.3 Telling imports from local imitations ("Roman-inspired" versus Roman)

- "Gandharan art has an effect throughout the continent, since its Classical affinities are in part responsible for the exaggeration of Roman finds in India. Many objects previously thought to be Roman are now considered Indian, some because of the influence of Gandhara such as architectural fragments decorated with acanthus from Devnimori … Others represent the copying of Classical themes or techniques by local craftsmen." (p. 123, PDF 125) [T-interp]
- Bengal: "Many, though — including terracotta figurines — are more likely Roman inspired than Roman." (pp. 128–9)
- Ter: "the finds from Ter or Tagara of the Periplus (PME 51) are Indian in origin, apart from some gold foil beads (Dikshit 1969: 44). The numerous ceramic lamps, which were originally interpreted as Roman … if classically influenced, derive from Hellenistic rather than Roman models (D. Bailey, pers. comm.)." (p. 132, PDF 134)
- Adam: "Published finds from the inland market town of Adam are likewise Roman-inspired rather than Roman in origin, apart from a mostly Tiberian coin hoard (Nath 1995: 152; Turner 1989: 46)." (p. 132)
- Arikamedu local pottery: "Some local pottery, too, was influenced by Hellenistic and Roman craftsmen or prototypes, as can be seen in a number of the vessel shapes (Wheeler et al. 1946: 40-1; Slane 1992: 209-10; Begley 1992: 157-76)." (pp. 137–8, PDF 139–40). The specific shapes are **not stated** here.
- Arikamedu gems: "Classically inspired carved gems, such as the cupid mentioned above, are now considered to be worked by local craftsmen with Greco-Roman influence (pp. 123, 133 and Fig. 20; Begley 1996: 29-30; Sidebotham 1996a)." (p. 137). Wheeler had "thought it was completed locally by Greco-Roman craftsmen (Wheeler et al. 1946: 101, pl. xxxiii, nos 11-12)." (p. 133)
- Vellalore jewellery: "It is difficult to assess the workmanship of the objects from publication alone, but clearly both Classical and Indian themes are represented." (p. 140, PDF 142). This is an explicit admission that the publication is insufficient for the analysis.
- Kolhapur bronzes: "a collection of indigenous and imported metal sculptures disentangled by De Puma (1992). Included alongside Indian metalwork are vessels likely to have been produced in Campania before the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79. The group, however, is not chronologically homogeneous as indicated by a statuette of Poseidon that may date as early as the third century BC" (pp. 131–2)
- Scientific sourcing appears only once in this range, for glass: "Microprobe analysis on a very small number of samples indicates an Egyptian source for some of the glass" (Begram, p. 123, PDF 125). On beads: "particularly as source areas for glass become better understood through compositional analysis (Lankton & Dussubieux 2006)." (p. 173). Petrography and XRD on pottery are not discussed in this range. The bibliography lists relevant works: Ford et al. 2005 (geochemistry of RW), Gogte 1997, 2001 (XRD), Begley 2004b (critique of Gogte's XRD), Whitbread 1995 and Williams 2000 (petrology). See §5.

### 2.4 Linings (pitch/resin) and reuse

- The only mention of linings in range: "There is no consistent methodology for recognising this process [reuse], but since lined, local Organic Storage Jars suitable for this purpose are commonly found in Egypt, and occasionally in India, there was no pressing need for reuse on a large scale." (p. 154, PDF 156). The lining material is **not stated** here.
- Reuse examples: "most dramatically the jetty at Myos Hormos (front cover), and from Berenike written instructions to decant local wine into a foreign amphora (Bagnall et al. 2000: 18). Sedov has suggested that a similar reuse took place at Qana, where Dressel 2-4 amphorae were filled with Arabian wine and then exported to India (2007: 102)." (p. 154). Tomber's caution: "Within India reuse of Roman amphorae may have occurred, perhaps as gift exchange, and this may be the explanation for single occurrences of imported amphorae." (p. 154) [T-interp]
- Reworked sherds: "At both Arikamedu (Will 2004: 324, n. 1) and Tissamaharama some amphora sherds were reused, as identified by their smoothed edges. While this selection for reuse may relate to the highly visible nature of these sherds and their durable (hard-fired) quality, it also suggests a degree of choice related to their scarcity." (pp. 150–1, PDF 152–3)
- Why reuse matters for distribution: "Since the emphasis is on pottery it is appropriate to say a few words about reuse, which may inflect distribution patterns." (p. 154)

### 2.5 Stamps, tituli, graffiti, dipinti and monograms

- Ed Dur: "Roman types include two amphora sherds, one a body sherd from an indeterminate Western source with a Latin stamp of 'LNV' (Papadopoulos 1994)." (p. 111, PDF 113)
- Arikamedu: "On the basis of two Knidian stamps, Tchernia suggested a start date of the early first century BC (1997b: 261)." (p. 137, PDF 139)
- Mathura and the Peticii: "Tchernia has brought together inscriptional evidence from Egypt's Eastern Desert and a stamped amphora from Mathura to show that the Italian Peticius family was directly involved in the supply of amphorae to India (1997a). Another Eastern Desert inscription confirms the participation of the Annii family of Puteoli (Tchernia 1997a: 239; De Romanis 1997a: 167-71)." (p. 152, PDF 154). The Mathura stamp's text is **not stated** here.
- Content inscription: "In support of wine, an Aqaba sherd from Berenike had an inscription reading oinos (Tomber 2004a: 398, not date wine as reported by Dolinka 2003: 95)." (p. 162, PDF 164). Tomber corrects Dolinka here.
- Christian marks: "Many Aqaba and LR1 amphorae have Christian monograms on them, including the chi-rho. Pieri has shown that Christian inscriptions on Mediterranean amphorae relate to ecclesiastical production and possibly also to where they were distributed (Pieri 2005: 175)." (p. 170, PDF 172)
- Graffiti as evidence of ownership: "Tangible evidence that imported goods were in Indian hands is provided by a sherd of sigillata from Arikamedu with a Tamil-Brahmi graffito (Begley 1996: 22; Comfort 1992: 141, fig. 8.13)." (p. 150, PDF 152)
- Graffiti as dating evidence: "there is evidence for later occupation from six Tamil-Brahmi graffiti dated to the third century (Begley 2004a: 11-12)" (p. 137)
- Graffiti and ethnicity: foreign residents' "presence can be detected textually, and archaeologically by graffiti of diverse linguistic groups and imported items that may represent personal possessions, whose use was identified with a particular ethnic group. Foreign residents probably comprised both merchants and sailors, although the two cannot be distinguished archaeologically." (p. 153, PDF 155)

### 2.6 Dating: how imports date sites, and how that goes wrong

- **Wheeler's import-based chronology** (p. 134, PDF 136): "His chronology was based on imported wares, from which he projected a sequence between the Augustan period and, on admittedly slim evidence, c. AD 200. He divided the occupation into three phases according to 38 sherds of Roman sigillata, all of which he thought was Arretine from modern Arezzo, Italy (Slane 1996: 351). These phases were defined as pre-Arretine, Arretine and post-Arretine and all contained Roman amphorae, of which he identified 116 sherds."
- **The re-dating of the sigillata** (p. 136, PDF 138): "Reassessment of this sigillata has demonstrated that it included vessels not only from Italy but also from the Eastern Mediterranean (ESA, ESB) and was not uniform in date but varied between c. 10 BC and AD 50 (Comfort 1992; Slane 1996). Thus the warehouse need not have belonged to the post-Arretine phase but may be slightly earlier, say between the second quarter and mid-first century AD (Begley 2004a: 6-7)."
- **Koan amphora dating dispute** (pp. 136–7, PDF 138–9): Will "dated most of these sherds, and subsequently the assemblage from Begley's excavation, between the second century BC and the later first century AD (1996: 320, 2004: 328-9). The second century BC date was derived from the Greek Koan amphorae, which Will calculated as representing over 40% and 56% of the assemblages (Will 2004: 326-9). Slane, however, has presented an argument preferred here, dating the Koan amphorae to the first century BC and Augustan period, with other amphorae continuing into the second century AD (1992: 204-5)." [Tomber sides with Slane against Will]
- **Single late sherds:** "the handle of a LR1 that could date to the fifth century (Will 2004: 383, no. 279). Two other potentially late sherds (Will 1996: nos 78 and 80) are discounted here." (p. 137). The reason for discounting them is **not stated**.
- **One amphora used to date a site sequence:** Mleiha, "an Egyptian amphora (ibid.: fig. 9.1) which the excavators rely upon to date their sequence to the late second/third century, but it could be from earlier in the second century." (p. 113, PDF 115) [Tomber disagrees with Benoist et al. 2003]
- **Residuality** (the only explicit use of the word in range): "Many Late Roman contexts at Berenike are difficult to assess given problems of residuality, but the finds from Shenshef, with its fifth/sixth century foundation, demonstrates continued importation during this period." (p. 165, PDF 167). This is the logic of using a single-period site as a control.
- **Surface assemblages:** Adulis and Elephanta "could easily extend into the seventh century, but their assemblages come from the surface and are difficult to date with precision." (pp. 166–7, PDF 168–9)
- **Torpedo jar dating:** "There is strong evidence that some of these are of Sasanian date (Tomber 2007b), but others lack secure contexts and may be Early Islamic, as those from Sanjan certainly are (Nanji in preparation)." (p. 167)
- **Primary versus secondary contexts:** on the Greek amphorae in Arikamedu's Northern Sector, "To address these questions, the individual find spots need to be evaluated: for example, were they from primary or secondary contexts?" (p. 149, PDF 151). Tomber poses this question and does not answer it.
- **Dates for LR2** depend on absences: "The absence of LR2 at Berenike, last mentioned in the Martyrium Sancti Arethae of AD 524-5 (Wild & Wild 2001: 211), is significant and suggests that LR2 did not circulate in the Indian Ocean trade until after this date." (pp. 165–6)
- **Defining the period from imports is circular:** "Conventionally, in the south a terminal date of AD 300 has been adopted, but this is largely based on what has been considered the end of the Roman contact period. Whether Roman contact ended then or, more importantly, whether this is an appropriate measure to define the Early Historic period, must be considered." (p. 120, PDF 122) [T-interp]

### 2.7 Quantification and recovery bias

- "Although in absolute quantity Ed Dur has many more imported finds than Mleiha, Mouton attributes this to intensive excavation and the type of features explored at Ed Dur (1999: 25)." (p. 113)
- "Although quantity cannot always be evaluated, Mesopotamian pottery is the most common, Roman and Indian are sparse … Glass and other finds quantitatively boost the Roman component." (p. 114, PDF 116)
- Nevasa: "Sixty-three sherds were collected by the excavators (Sankalia et al. 1960: 280). Between Sunil Gupta (Gupta et al. 2001) and myself it was possible to locate and study 15 of these, all of which date to between the first and third centuries AD." (p. 132, PDF 134). So 15 of 63 survive in a findable state.
- Endnote 2 (p. 176) records, site by site, whether Tomber examined the amphorae herself ("Examined by RT"). See §3.
- Coins are a poor proxy: "evaluating coinage on actual numbers will be misleading if the role of coinage differed through time or place." (p. 171)
- Pepper preservation: "Pepper was a transit item, but was also common at the Egyptian ports, partly identifiable due to preservation conditions." (p. 173)

## 4. Arguments

### 4.1 Wheeler and the Roman-centred reading of Arikamedu

- Agenda-setting: "Wheeler's single season of excavation in 1945 set the agenda for all subsequent research." (pp. 133–4) [T-fact]
- What Wheeler concluded: "From this Wheeler concluded that there was Mediterranean trade or involvement at Arikamedu throughout its entire history." (p. 134). On the warehouse: "In the Northern Sector Wheeler excavated a brick structure c. 150 ft long that he identified as a warehouse, which supported his overall interpretation of a Roman Emporium. He assigned the structure to the post-Arretine phase and dated its construction to c. AD 50" (p. 134). Southern Sector: "considered primarily industrial and dominated by two courtyards with associated brick tanks and culverts for dyeing muslin." (p. 134)
- "Wheeler assumed that there was a Roman colony at Arikamedu." (p. 148)
- Corrections, attributed:
  - Casal recognised multi-period occupation and an earlier Megalithic phase in the Southern Sector, "thereby isolating this area as the more ancient one active prior to overseas trade" (pp. 134–5). Begley says these revisions went "'largely unnoticed'" (1996: 4, 12-21) (p. 134).
  - Begley "challenged the function of the tanks for dyeing (Begley 1996: 17-18, 2004a: 10)" (p. 136).
  - Sigillata re-dating means "the warehouse need not have belonged to the post-Arretine phase but may be slightly earlier" (p. 136).
  - "a recent article by Ravitchandirane has demonstrated that its extent was seriously underestimated by previous scholars (2007)." (p. 136)
- The overall effect on the field: "Above all the Chola site of Arikamedu has dominated our perception of Indo-Roman trade." (p. 133). Also: "surprisingly, considering that until recently it has been the key archaeological site in terms of Roman finds, it received only one mention in this document [the Periplus]. Furthermore, it was mentioned not as an overseas harbour but as a place for local boats from the north and west of India, making it pivotal for understanding connections between the Malabar and Coromandel Coasts." (p. 135) [T-interp]
- Local population: "At Arikamedu the use of Megalithic pottery both before and after the introduction of traded Western goods indicates the continuing importance of the local population (Begley 1996: 18)." (p. 147)
- Tomber does not simply discard Wheeler's emporium idea, however. Her own position is that if Romans were resident at Muziris, then "the same applies at Arikamedu" (p. 149; see 4.4). She uses Wheeler's "terminal and transit" categories approvingly (p. 173), and she reports Whitehouse's Camulodunum analogy (p. 151) without criticism.

### 4.2 Pre-Roman maritime exchange and pre-existing networks

- "Where known, many had phases predating the Roman contact period that reinforce the importance of pre-existing trade routes in the spread of Roman artefacts." (p. 147) [T-interp]
- Pattanam: "as at Arikamedu, the site was operative before the overseas contact period" (p. 142).
- "Roman exchange attached itself to indigenous systems, especially for subsistence items, with their established framework for procuring and disposing of goods (Ray 1995b: 98, 1998: 188). This would have increased the demand for spices, raw materials and manufactured items and stimulated these pre-existing networks, but the full impact of this demand — for instance whether it encouraged urbanism — is unknown (Abraham 2007: 293-4)." (pp. 147–8)
- Divergent views on whether maritime commerce caused urbanisation, reported without a verdict: "Some scholars, such as Champakalakshmi (ibid.) see it as essential to growth, Ray and others view it as one of many stimuli (1995b; Thapar 1997: 21, 30), and Gurukkal as not affecting economic and social development (1995: 250)." (p. 121)
- Embassies: "Embassies between India and the West were known from Hellenistic/Mauryan times, and Ptolemy II received one" (p. 152).
- Pliny on earlier routes: "Previously they set sail from South Arabia to northern India, but now 'it will not be amiss to set out the whole of the voyage from Egypt' [to Muziris] (NH 6.101)" (p. 157).
- Pre-Roman maritime exchange in the full sense (Hellenistic, Begley 1992 "pre-Periplus trade") is not developed in this range beyond the Koan dating dispute and the Megalithic phases.

### 4.3 Indigenous commercial agency (Indian, Arabian, Persian, African)

- "Like Gupta (2007: 115) we support the active input of all the different groups: Romans, Arabs, Persians, Africans and Indians." (p. 160) [T-interp, explicit]
- "An assessment of the finds that travelled throughout the Indian Ocean reveals an active input from each region." (p. 171)
- Indian ships in the Red Sea: "PME 26 indicates that Indian ships sailed to the Red Sea (Casson 1989: 20). Indian sails and timbers found on the Red Sea coast (Vermeeren 2000: 340-1; Wild & Wild 2001) seemingly provide the tangible evidence for this" (p. 153)
- Indian residents in Egypt: "Direct voyages are also indicated by the presence of Indian residents, and the large quantity of Indian pottery found on the Egyptian Red Sea sites, but not penetrating further into the Roman world." (p. 157)
- Ray's synthesis: "most trade was conducted privately, but provided revenue for the ruling elite" (p. 148).
- Indian consumption of imports: the wine discussion (pp. 149–50), ending with "Regardless of whether Indians had a positive liking for imported foodstuffs, their acquisition would have carried status." (p. 150). On consumer choice: "It is hard to envisage a situation where specific types of wine, for example, had an Indian market, and easier to appreciate that wine as a generic class was desirable. While Romans resident in India may have had more specific demands, concentrations of individual amphora types in India could result merely from the single opportunistic trade deal of a merchant." (p. 172) [T-interp]
- Political agency: "the changing fortunes of the Chera and Pandya kingdoms had a direct bearing on which ports Roman ships patronised (De Romanis 1997b: 90-108)." (p. 171). "De Romanis has convincingly argued that a preference for Muziris or Bakare can be traced to intertribal disputes between the Chera and the Pandya (De Romanis 1997b: 90-108, esp. 107)." (p. 141)
- **The Late period is a counterweight in which Rome supplies the impetus:** "At present they [Aksumite finds in India] contrast sharply with the more common Roman finds, and suggest that while assisted particularly by Aksumite middlemen, the Romans still provided the impetus for commerce with the East." (p. 164). Also: "The Romans played an important role in Indo-Roman trade during both the early and late periods, although some journeys were indirect and assisted by middlemen." (p. 171)
- Who counts as "Roman": "In this study 'Roman' has been used as an umbrella term for different ethnic groups who resided within the Empire. Nevertheless, actual Romans were also involved." (p. 152)
- Organisation on the Roman side: "evidence from documents, sites and finds all point to the Roman component of trade being undertaken by private individuals. The enormous cost and complexity of the undertaking would not have precluded private enterprise, but did preclude small merchants acting on their own" (p. 153, citing Whittaker 2004: 169). On the Muziris papyrus: "a hierarchy in which a wealthy financier recruited merchants" (Rathbone 2001: 438) (p. 153).

### 4.4 Foreign residents at Muziris and Arikamedu

- Casson is reported: "Casson argues that at Barygaza, import-export was most likely handled by local merchants, in contrast to Muziris/Nelkynda where there was 'unmistakable' evidence for a resident foreign colony (1989: 24)." Casson's three grounds are the Temple of Augustus on the Peutinger Table, the Muziris papyrus, and PME 56 on grain (p. 148).
- Tomber's partial disagreement with Casson: "The Temple of Augustus as a real edifice has already been largely discounted (Chapter 2, p. 30). Evidence from the two documents is more compelling, but is still open to interpretation (Seland 2007b: 78). Regardless of where the Muziris papyrus was drawn up, it seems clear that foreigners were resident at Muziris, although it was not a colonial station. They would have had to stay, even if only while trade was conducted and ships were unloaded and provisioned for their return journey (ibid.: 79). Thus the Muziris community was heterogeneous, also including non-local Indian traders from the north-west (PME 54; De Romanis 1997b: 94)." (p. 148)
- **Disagreement with Meyer:** "Meyer has summarised the Coromandel sites in the Periplus, noting that no exports, but only imports from Limyrike, are itemised … He argues that trade with the east coast was indirect, and was not conducted by Romans … Yet as we learn more, the similarities between Pattanam and Arikamedu increase. It follows that if Romans were at least temporarily resident at Muziris, the same applies at Arikamedu." (pp. 148–9) [T-interp. The inference rests on similarity of assemblages; the similarities are not itemised.]
- Integration versus segregation: "In some places during medieval times, foreign traders were ghettoised … Elsewhere, though, as implied by the Deccan cave inscriptions, foreign traders integrated themselves within the local merchant community." (p. 149)

### 4.5 Overlapping regional networks (Red Sea, Gulf, Arabia, East Africa, Bay of Bengal)

- **Two strands, Red Sea and Gulf:** "Although they occur in the same region, they are only rarely found on the same site. Some may have travelled to India and Sri Lanka via Qana or Khor Rori, where they are present together (Sedov & Benvenuti 2002; Sedov 2007), but the overall pattern indicates two separate strands of trade: one from the Red Sea, the other via the Gulf." (p. 167) A possible mixed mechanism, select markets with both Roman and Sasanian goods (Walburg 2008), is acknowledged: "As yet I have seen no finds to support this view." (pp. 167–8)
- **The Gulf as separate:** "Of all the regions involved in Indo-Roman trade, the Gulf was the most separate, both geographically and politically. Mesopotamian ceramics — both Torpedo jars and Glazed Ware — dominate the assemblages." (p. 109). Roman goods in the Gulf "probably arrived indirectly either overland or via Qana (Rutten 2007: 19; Tomber 2007b: 983)" (p. 159).
- **Disagreement with Salles on routes into the Gulf:** "Salles has suggested that Roman items may have reached the Gulf as re-exported items from Barygaza, although Barygaza cannot be evaluated archaeologically (1993: 516-17). Schoff put forward a similar hypothesis for the re-export of Western copper from India to the Gulf (1912: 151) … there is no evidence that this is Western copper rather than Indian … If Roman material was filtered to the Gulf via non-Roman ports then Qana, which received Western copper (PME 28) and archaeologically has a wealth of Roman finds, was a more likely transhipment point than an Indian port." (p. 115) [T-interp, against Salles and Schoff]
- Also on Salles: the sparse Roman finds are "interpreted as reflecting the enmity between Parthians and Romans that continued into the Sasanian period (Bharucha-Irani 2002: 71-2; contra Salles 1993: 498)." (p. 114)
- Rutten reported approvingly ("In her excellent synthesis"): "some of the ESA reached Ed Dur via Mesopotamia during the late first century BC/AD, but … a second more important route distributed Roman fine wares to the Gulf through a Red Sea-South Arabian link, re-exported from Qana and Khor Rori." (p. 116)
- Mouton versus Haerinck on Ed Dur and Mleiha. Haerinck: Ed Dur was Mleiha's port, symbiotic. "Mouton, however, has challenged this interpretation, arguing that there is little evidence for seaborne trade at Ed Dur." (p. 113). Tomber does not adjudicate.
- **Direct and indirect routes to Malabar:** "So there were two ways to reach Muziris/Limyrike, direct and indirect." (p. 157). The aggregate evidence "supports some direct voyages between Egypt and particularly South India, where the greatest diversity in amphora sources is seen." (p. 157)
- **Short regional circuits:** "As described in the Periplus, itineraries existed independently of those between Rome-India and Arabia-India, particularly routes to Africa. These were shorter, some even local, took less time and investment and therefore could be undertaken by smaller-scale traders" (p. 158). "No single all-embracing explanation accounts for these short journeys, but they uphold the idea of separate regional itineraries introduced in the Periplus." (p. 160)
- **The Egypt–Qana circuit** (incense and basalt ballast): "This closed trip would have catered to the incense trade and the basalt, particularly, provided suitable ballast for ships whose main cargo was frankincense (Peacock et al. 2007: 62)." (p. 158)
- **East Africa separate from India:** at Ras Hafun, "The limited quantity of Asian ceramics at Ras Hafun suggests indirect supply and supports the premise that the East African route was separate from India at this time (Smith & Wright 1988: 139)." (p. 159)
- **Bay of Bengal:** "In this region Indo-Roman trade is considered indirect through internal trade routes, particularly with the Coromandel Coast" (p. 130). "More important for the region was trade with Southeast Asia and Sri Lanka, some of which was funnelled through South India" (p. 130). "There is no evidence that this cycle was linked to Roman fortunes, but instead to coastal contacts throughout peninsular India and Sri Lanka (Ray 1998: 30-1)." (p. 130)
- **Malabar–Coromandel link:** "Two main routes existed for this exchange. One was by sea, hugging Cape Comorin … The most accessible land route was through a break in the Western Ghats known as Palghat Gap … the coastal route can be seen as a quick means by which to reach the east coast trade, not by Roman ships but in small, local boats (PME 60)." (p. 144)
- **RW and northern routes:** "However, Rouletted Ware could also have travelled from the east coast via the north-west port of Barygaza (Schenk in press)" (p. 155). "Gogte's (1997) recent proposal that the Tamluk-Chandraketugarh area is the epicentre of RW production provides a more visible way to track exports." (p. 130). This is conditional on the Muziris papyrus reading; Tomber does not endorse Gogte outright.
- **Late period, Christian networks:** "Aila, Berenike, Aksum and Adulis were all thriving centres of Christianity, each the seat of a bishop, and together they form part of a Red Sea interaction zone (Tomber 2007a)." (p. 168). On Aqaba amphorae: "their distribution, clustering on sites facilitating Eastern trade, may underscore Church involvement or at the very least Christian merchants." (p. 170)

### 4.6 Ports as intermediaries (entrepots, transhipment)

- "Entrepots such as Alexandria, Qana and Muziris were vital for amassing and redistributing goods. In the first instance, ships sailing from Berenike and Myos Hormos depended on what was available in Alexandria. Muziris, as we have seen, acted an entrepot for all of India, Qana was a redistribution node for East and West, and played a particularly important role for the Gulf." (p. 172)
- **The entrepot masks provenance:** "The very nature of an entrepot such as Muziris, in bringing together goods from throughout India, serves to mask archaeological patterns, as do indirect routes." (p. 172) [T-interp; a key methodological statement]
- Muziris as transhipment centre: "Its role as a transhipment centre, as described in the Muziris papyrus (Rathbone 2001) and the Sangam poetry (De Romanis 1997b: 94-5), is verified by finds from elsewhere in India including beads from the Coimbatore region. The Muziris papyrus specifically mentions Gangetic nard and textiles, both organics and therefore poorly preserved, but imports from the same region can be traced by more tangible items from eastern India, such as RW and Paddle Impressed Wares." (p. 143)
- Port siting: "Given the ferocity of the waters surrounding India many ports are some distance from the coast, sited on river estuaries that served to facilitate inland trade. Ray attributes their prosperity not to their coastal location but to the wealth of their hinterland (1995a: 98). Siltation means that many sites are now further away from waterways than in Antiquity." (p. 122) [directly relevant to the coastline and river questions]
- Stopovers and cargo transformation: "If the break was short local products may have been added to the hold of a not quite full ship; longer stays may have required the complete unloading of the ship, whose initial cargo could then be radically transformed … Archaeological recognition of these different processes is frustratingly difficult and, as will be seen below, alternative explanations can frequently account for artefact distribution." (pp. 157–8)
- Two-level exchange: "Two levels of exchange may have existed at trading sites — one for locals and another for foreigners." (p. 172)

### 4.7 What pottery can and cannot say

**It can say:**
- Sources and routes, through types: e.g. Torpedo jars marking the Gulf strand, Aqaba amphorae marking the Red Sea and Christian sphere, CRSW marking Kerala.
- Ethnic presence, through personal-possession wares: Egyptian coarse wares "may have been the personal possessions of sailors and merchants" (p. 160). Indian pottery in Egypt "appears to have been primarily for use there, or on board ships" (p. 172).
- Direct versus indirect voyages, through the diversity of sources (p. 157).

**It cannot (or not yet) say:**
- Merchants from sailors: "Foreign residents probably comprised both merchants and sailors, although the two cannot be distinguished archaeologically." (p. 153)
- Whether groups shared voyages: "it is difficult to assess whether they collaborated on the same voyage or acted separately." (p. 153)
- Stopover processes: "Archaeological recognition of these different processes is frustratingly difficult" (p. 158)
- Absolute scale: "Overall it is difficult to assess the absolute scale of trade … Thus it is difficult even to assess the relative importance of each period." (p. 171)
- North versus South India: "some of the most basic questions are difficult to answer, such as the relative importance of North and South India." (p. 171)
- Snapshot problem: "texts and even archaeology frequently provide only a snapshot of a single point in time." (p. 171)
- Reuse: "There is no consistent methodology for recognising this process" (p. 154)
- Visibility: "Only a minority of the inhabited settlements of this period have imports, which in turn comprise only a small proportion of their assemblages. Most of the economy was based on local and regional trade networks, and even large centres did not necessarily participate in long-distance trade (Smith 2002), or at least this is not materially visible." (pp. 121–2)

### 4.8 Conclusions (Ch. 6 "Transit and terminal", pp. 171–4)

- "The Romans played an important role in Indo-Roman trade during both the early and late periods, although some journeys were indirect and assisted by middlemen." (p. 171)
- "Nevertheless, in terms of the identification of artefacts, South India appears to have been more important to Egypt." (p. 172)
- "The variable input of the different regions was largely determined by internal political and economic situations that impacted on their international relations (e.g. Salles 1998: 48)." (p. 171)
- Coins do not resolve the question of scale; late coins "may eventually exceed the early issues in quantity although not value" (p. 171).
- Early Roman ports: "It is paradoxical that during the Early Roman period when Indian Ocean trade was intense, the main trade ports lacked substantial infrastructure, which may provide another argument against Imperial control." (p. 154)
- Late Roman: the Red Sea sites "are more substantial and more complex during this period when Indo-Roman trade was previously thought to be greatly reduced." (p. 162)
- Early Roman amphorae in India are "more widely dispersed with clustering in the far south. The largest assemblages are at Arikamedu and Pattanam, the next largest at Nevasa; otherwise distribution is thin, occurring as 1-3 sherds" (p. 156). Previous maps (Ray 2003) showed a Gujarat and Arikamedu concentration; "By separating Early and Late Roman ones and distinguishing them from Mesopotamian Torpedo jars, this effect is lessened" (p. 156).

## 6. Points a historian could build an original argument on

Each item states what the text says and where. The bracketed note says what might be argued. None of this is Tomber's claim unless marked.

1. **Pattanam is a co-occurrence site, and the dating of its Torpedo jars matters for its end date.** Tomber identified "Glazed Ware and Torpedo jars of uncertain date from Mesopotamia" at Pattanam (p. 142). Elsewhere she argues that Torpedoes in India are "of Sasanian date" in some cases and Early Islamic in others (p. 167). She also says Late Roman amphorae and Torpedoes "are only rarely found on the same site" (p. 167). Yet Fig. 21 marks Pattanam (49) with both Early Roman and Torpedo symbols. Meanwhile the overseas-contact phase is dated "between approximately the late first century BC and third century AD" (p. 142), and Muziris is said to have "declined" by the Late Roman period (p. 165) and to be no longer functioning by the mid-sixth century (p. 143). [If the Pattanam Torpedoes are Sasanian, then the site, or something near it, received Gulf goods after the stated contact phase. Either the Early Historic end date is too early, or the Torpedoes come from the Early Medieval phase. The book cannot resolve this; the Pattanam stratigraphy can. This bears directly on whether "Muziris declined" is an artefact of looking only for Roman goods.]

2. **There is a Late Roman Malabar gap.** Table 3 lists no Kerala site for any Late Roman amphora type. Fig. 21 has no Late Roman symbol on the Malabar coast. Yet Cosmas names five Malabar ports in the sixth century (p. 165), and an Aksumite coin hoard with later Roman coins is reported from Mangalore (p. 140). [The absence of Late Roman amphorae on the Malabar coast sits beside textual evidence of active Malabar ports. This is an absence-of-excavation problem of the same kind as the project's finding that 13 of 22 candidates were never excavated. The sixth-century ports named by Cosmas have no archaeological counterpart in this book.]

3. **The Kerala CRSW is the only Indian ware Tomber ties to a specific coastal region and also finds in Egypt.** "among the CRSW were sherds with the distinctive internal wiping marks seen at Berenike and Myos Hormos, but not elsewhere in India outside Kerala" (p. 142). And: "Further support comes from the Keralan organically gouged CRSW found in Egypt. Elsewhere in the Indian Ocean it is difficult to trace this distinctive type since manufacturing techniques are not published." (p. 155). [Its absence from Qana and Khor Rori may be a publication artefact (techniques not published), not a real absence. That is testable. If Kerala CRSW is absent at Qana, it supports direct Egypt–Malabar sailing, which is Pliny's direct route.]

4. **The Muziris identification rests on a river-migration claim reported second-hand.** Tomber's case for Pattanam over Kodungallur depends on Shajan 1998 (a PhD thesis) for "a gradual migration from south to north" of the Periyar, "visible on satellite imagery", and on placing the c. 2000 BP main channel at the Paravur Thodu (p. 141). No date or mechanism for the shift is given. The negative evidence for Kodungallur is a single footnote: dredging "did not recover any Early Historic finds (Begley 1996: 11, n. 2)". [The thesis's river-network work can test the Paravur Thodu channel independently. The Kodungallur negative is very thin.]

5. **Tomber's two arguments about residence pull in different directions.** On Ed Dur, Rutten's view that Roman fine wares are "primarily from domestic contexts and thought to result from trade rather than being selected by and for Roman residents" (p. 112) is reported without objection. At Arikamedu, the clustering of Greek amphorae in the Northern Sector raises the question of segregated foreign merchants, which she leaves open (p. 149). On consumer choice, single-type concentrations "could result merely from the single opportunistic trade deal of a merchant" (p. 172). [These give three incompatible readings of the same kind of clustering: trade goods, segregated residents, one-off deal. There is room for a formal argument about what spatial clustering of imports can and cannot discriminate, which is a question with a statistical shape.]

6. **The inference from Muziris to Arikamedu is a chain of uncertainties.** "It follows that if Romans were at least temporarily resident at Muziris, the same applies at Arikamedu" (p. 149). The premise is itself established only as "it seems clear" from documents "open to interpretation" (p. 148), and the bridge is unspecified "similarities". The Periplus mentions Poduke only for local boats (p. 135).

7. **Coins and pottery diverge sharply at Arikamedu.** Coins are "rare" at Arikamedu (three denarii of questionable provenance, one bought copper), "in stark contrast with the other categories of finds and also with central Tamil Nadu and Kerala" (p. 137). Meanwhile Kerala, the coin-rich area, had no known Early Historic site before Pattanam (p. 141). [This fits the project's finding that coins are an interior phenomenon. The coin and pottery maps are almost complementary, which supports treating them as independent channels and not as proxies for one another.]

8. **One sherd carries much of the "Indian possession" argument.** "Tangible evidence that imported goods were in Indian hands is provided by a sherd of sigillata from Arikamedu with a Tamil-Brahmi graffito" (p. 150). It is a single sherd (Comfort 1992 fig. 8.13). The parallel at Berenike and Myos Hormos is Tamil-Brahmi graffiti on Indian pots in Egypt (p. 155, Mahadevan 1996, 2007). [The symmetry of Tamil script on Roman pots in India and on Indian pots in Egypt could be developed, but the Indian side has n = 1 in this book.]

9. **The number of examined sherds is very small.** Nevasa has 63 collected and 15 locatable (p. 132). Early Roman amphorae elsewhere occur "as 1-3 sherds" (p. 156). The Endnote 2 table shows which sites Tomber saw in person: Pattanam yes; Mantai, Anuradhapura, Taxila, Mathura, Kolhapur, Paithan no. [The whole distribution argument of Fig. 21 rests on presence or absence at sites with tiny counts and unequal examination. This is the same informative-missingness problem the thesis models.]

10. **Port siting statement.** "Given the ferocity of the waters surrounding India many ports are some distance from the coast, sited on river estuaries … Siltation means that many sites are now further away from waterways than in Antiquity." (p. 122). Tissamaharama is c. 10 km from the sea and "judged" connected by river on a personal communication (p. 146). [This is a general expectation that the thesis's coastline and river questions can test quantitatively for the Malabar candidates.]

11. **Fig. 19 places Tyndis, Bakare and Nelkynda without explanation.** The text gives no basis for the plotted positions (see §3.1). Do not cite Fig. 19 as Tomber locating these ports.

12. **The Elephanta puzzle:** "How the combination of these types reached Elephanta is of interest as no single entrepot for India contains all of them." (p. 128). [A western Indian site mixing the Red Sea strand and the Gulf strand contradicts the "two separate strands" model of p. 167 and deserves to be set against it.]
