## 2. Method

### 2.1 Site and excavation

"Fieldwork concentrated on the site Akurugoda situated on the eastern bank of ancient
water tank called Tissa Wewa. Aerial photography had revealed the rectangular citadel, a
shallow hill known as Akurugoda (Weisshaar 2001). It covers an area of 200 m × 600 m and
is today about 12 km far from the coast line." (p. 99) [Doc]

Fieldwork: begun 1992 (p. 95); "Fieldwork ended in 2010." (p. 95). (Pavan & Schenk 2012:
194 say "completed in 2009".)

Supplementary sites: "smaller excavations in the vicinity such as the monastery of
Godavaya (Weisshaar 2007; Bemmann / Weisshaar 2008) and the sacred area of
Tissamaharama-Old Market1." (pp. 100–101). Fn. 1: "Excavation by W. Wijepala (1998). A
report will be published with the Tissamaharama publication."

### 2.2 Dating: stratigraphy plus radiocarbon

"The pottery chronology is based on features in a continuous stratigraphical situation
and the entire sequence is enhanced by Radiocarbon dates2. The evaluation was facilitated
by well-preserved conditions that included numerous complete pots that remained
undisturbed in-situ in their contexts (ﬁg. 5)." (p. 101) [Doc]

**Phase list, fn. 2 (p. 101), verbatim:** "The phases of the settlement: phase a (5th /
4th century B. C. E.), phase b (3rd century B. C. E.), phase c1 (2nd century B. C. E.),
phase c2 (1st century B. C. E.), phase d1 (1st century C. E.), phase d2 (2nd century C.
E.), phase e (3rd century C. E.), phase f1 (4th century C. E.), phase f2 (5th century C.
E.), phase g1 (late 5th / 6th century C. E.), phase g2 (late 6th / 7th century C. E.),
phase h (8th / 9th century C. E.). As we expect more radiocarbon dates, the preliminary
chronology might be subject to minor changes."

| Phase | Date as printed |
|---|---|
| a | 5th / 4th century B.C.E. |
| b | 3rd century B.C.E. |
| c1 | 2nd century B.C.E. |
| c2 | 1st century B.C.E. |
| d1 | 1st century C.E. |
| d2 | 2nd century C.E. |
| e | 3rd century C.E. |
| f1 | 4th century C.E. |
| f2 | 5th century C.E. |

**Radiocarbon dates themselves: not stated.** No laboratory numbers, raw ages or
calibrated ranges appear in the article. No reference to Görsdorf 2001 appears in this
article's bibliography (Pavan & Schenk 2012 cite it for Tissa 1 and 2). Each phase is
assigned a whole century, which signals that the phase boundaries are rounded. The whole
sequence extends "from about 5th century B. C. E. to about 12th / 13th century C. E."
(p. 95), beyond phase h, through the supplementary sites.

**Settlement history [Doc]:** "The layout is preceded by wooden structures directly set
onto virgin soil at about 5th century B. C. E. and surrounded by an earthen rampart."
(p. 100). "The ﬁrst main phase (5th – 1st century B. C. E.) consists of group of buildings
with continuing use in the course of time which indicate steady ownership. Clearly visible
by change of pattern, the area was used as a hospital in the 1st–2nd century C. E.
Domestic brick structures were newly erected at the latest in the 4th / 5th century C. E.,
replaced in the 6th century by sacral buildings. At the same time, part of the population
left the area towards the southwest of the island." (p. 100)

### 2.3 Local versus imported: priority to local pottery

"The evaluation of the permanent modiﬁcation of locally produced vessels – the majority
of pottery ﬁnds – was the main focus of Tissamaharama research. Besides changing of types
and technical details of fabrication, this includes also the reconstruction of functional
sets in the course of time. Regional domestic pottery is considered of higher information
value than imported pottery. Such foreign pottery is usually preferred because of easy
recognisability, although very scarce in percentage." (p. 101) [Int, methodological]

### 2.4 Residuality

"However, these fragments have lost much of their value on dating, because commonly
preserved as worn, solitary pieces they only form part of residual stock in contexts."
(p. 101)

The criterion that separates in-use from residual Fine Grey [Doc]: in Phase c1 "Many
fragments of 'Rouletted Ware' were mendable, spread all over the layers horizontally"
(p. 101); in Phase c2 "the pieces were merely singular and worn. Rate of occurrence was
rapidly diminished in subsequent layers." (p. 113). The upper level "(phase c2) shows many
solitary pieces, some were recorded as very worn and re-used. At the time of their
disposal, they were certainly not anymore in use as complete vessels." (p. 101)

Inference drawn [Int]: "Accordingly, sites containing such pottery – although worn and
re-used fragments in later context – existed before 1st century B. C. E." (p. 113)

### 2.5 Ware identification

**Paste, by eye:** "'Rouletted Ware' forms part of a characteristic group of pottery
bonded together by clay of identical quality, clearly visible by naked eye. The extremely
ﬁne and light grey paste differs outright from the coarsely tempered potteries common in
the South of the Indian subcontinent (ﬁg. 8)." (p. 105) [Doc]

**Material analysis** [Rep: Gogte]: "Material analysis of samples from Tissamaharama and
further sites in India and South East Asia conﬁrms this and points to a production
somewhere in the Ganges valley (Gogte 1997; 2001)." (p. 105). Note Gogte 1997's title,
copied from the bibliography, names "The Chandraketugarh-Tamluk Region of Bengal" as the
source of RW. Schenk generalises this to "the Ganges valley" / "Ganges Plains".

**Firing technique is not fabric:** "At ﬁrst glance, the ﬁring in Black-and Red
technique, typical for the Southern regions, seems to be a contradiction. However, this is
merely a ﬁring technique, which can be carried out with each sort of clay (ﬁg. 8).
Classiﬁed as Fabric 4 of 'Fine Grey Pottery' (ﬁg. 10), this represents the adoption of
this technique" (pp. 113–114)

**Four fabrics of Fine Grey Pottery (p. 106 with fns 5–8; Fig. 10):**

| Fabric | Common name | First at Tissa | Earlier name (Schenk 2001: 72) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Plain Grey Ware" | phase a (5th/4th c. BCE) | Variant 5 (Type 1), Variant 6 (Type 2) |
| 2 | "Northern Black Polished Ware" (NBP) | late phase a | Variant 4 |
| 3 | "entirely ﬁred in red" | phase b | Variants 1–3 |
| 4 | "ﬁred in Black-and Red technique" | phase b | Variants 1–3 |

(Fn. 5: "Schenk 2001: 72: described as Variant 5." Fn. 6: "Variant 6." Fn. 7: "Variant
4." Fn. 8: "Variant 1–3." Fn. 4: "This modiﬁes and redeﬁnes earlier descriptions on
variants of 'Fine Grey Pottery' (Schenk 2001: 71–73; 128–130).")

**RPW critique.** Fine Grey Fabric 3 has the qualities usually called "Red Polished
Ware": "However, such a feature applies to fabric 3 of 'Fine Grey Pottery' as well (see
below and Schenk 2007: 82 ﬁg. 8–9), vessels that clearly belong to 3rd / 2nd century B. C.
E." (p. 99)

**The rouletted dish restricted to fine grey paste:** "To prevent confusion of ideas,
however, the well-known term 'Rouletted Ware' should be clearly restricted to those of the
ﬁne grey quality, which easily and without doubt separate those fabricated from the
South." (p. 105)

### 2.6 Trench 3B: the worked example

"Exempliﬁed is the ﬁnd situation at Trench 3B in restriction to ﬂat dishes of so-called
'Rouletted Ware' and 'NBP'. It starts from planum 26 on around 21,60 MSL (Compilation A)
reaching deeper from level to level (Compilations B-E). The upper level (phase c2) shows
many solitary pieces, some were recorded as very worn and re-used. At the time of their
disposal, they were certainly not anymore in use as complete vessels. Covered by debris on
level 21,45 MSL, which is the foundation level of succeeding phase c2, the compilations B
to D comprise the levels of Phase c1. Many fragments of 'Rouletted Ware' were mendable,
spread all over the layers horizontally. Underneath the foundation level of Phase c1 (c.
21,00 MSL), this scattering of complete vessels is conﬁned to dishes of 'Northern Black
Polished Ware' (NBP)." (p. 101)

## 4. Arguments

### 4.1 Pre-Periplus, pre-Roman maritime exchange

- [Int] Abstract: "Their ﬁnd spots trace Indian Ocean maritime routes as physical evidence
  in Pre-Roman times, which cover Southeast Asia differently to the picture drawn by the
  later Periplus." (p. 95)
- [Int] "Earliest ﬁnds of this pottery group conﬁrm connections along the East Coast of
  India already in the 5th / 4th century B. C. Trans-continental routing across the Indian
  Ocean is then evidenced by ﬁnds like the well-known 'Rouletted Ware' in the 2nd century
  B. C." (p. 95)
- [Int] "In particular it was the ﬁnal 'product line' of the 2nd century B. C. E. that was
  sent to the West and the East across the Indian Ocean (ﬁg. 3)." (p. 113). This agrees
  with Pavan & Schenk 2012's finding that Sumhuram's RW is the late variety.
- [Int] "'proto-global' sailing routes reaching from Egypt as far as Vietnam and Bali at
  least in the 2nd century B. C. E. (ﬁg. 3)." (p. 104)
- [Int] "Joint studies at the coastal sites of Sumhuram (Khor Rori) in Oman (Pavan /
  Schenk 2012) and recently at Sembiran / Pacung on Bali (Calo et al. in press) could
  exemplify this, so far restricted to the 1st / 2nd century B. C. E. according to the
  evaluation." (pp. 96–97)

### 4.2 Critique of Roman-centred interpretation and of Wheeler

- [Int] "However, this impressive seafarer's manual inﬂuenced the angle of research to
  focus on Roman Western trade, enhanced by the discovery of Terra Sigillata at the hence
  famous site Arikamedu on the Eastern Indian coast (Wheeler / Gosh / Krishna Deva 1946:
  34– 41). It was there that 'Rouletted Ware' (ﬁg. 1,1) was deﬁned for the ﬁrst time and
  considered as Roman (Wheeler / Gosh / Krishna Deva 1946: 45– 49 and pl. XXII). This
  interpretation was questioned in 1983, when V. Begley suggested a pre-Roman dating. The
  results from Tissamaharama conﬁrm this (for a summary: Schenk 2006)." (p. 97)
- [Int] "Subsequently, when Arretine Terra Sigillata turned up at Arikamedu, it was
  identiﬁed as the ancient Poduke. ... V. Begley already proved since the late 80ies,
  however, that Arikamedu had existed from pre-Roman up to medieval times and moreover was
  heavily disturbed by much later and even modern exploitations (Begley 1996: 30–37)."
  (p. 101)
- [Int] "Subsequently several material evidences were solely seen from the Roman
  perspective, among it 'Rouletted Ware' which was for the ﬁrst time identiﬁed at
  Arikamedu." (p. 101)
- [Int] "Due to the erroneous dating of 'Rouletted Ware', all the numerous ﬁnd spots were
  for a long time misinterpreted in the context of Roman Period of the early centuries C.
  E." (p. 105)
- On the Periplus author: "Interestingly, the eastern Indian Ocean region was not known to
  him." (p. 97); "This eastern direction is not mentioned in the Periplus. Apparently this
  area of the Indian Ocean was not part of the practical experience of the anonymous
  mariner who wrote the seafarer's manual." (p. 105)
- RPW as a second Roman-centred misreading [Int]: "High quality of red ﬁred fabric and
  extremely ﬁne paste of this 'ware' said to remind of Terra Sigillata led to the
  assumption to be either of Western origin or at least inﬂuenced. Therefore such ﬁnds
  served also as conﬁrmation of Periplus trade connections." (p. 98). Her counter: "I
  would like to argue that this might be part of a continuous regional pottery tradition at
  Western India (Gujarat / Maharasthra), where such high-quality clay was in use (see Orton
  1992)." (p. 99) and "Many other types in the compilation by Orton are medieval and it is
  them that are found at the sites at the Persian / Arabian Gulf (Schenk 2007: 65– 67)."
  (p. 99)

### 4.3 South Asian agency: Buddhism, Mauryas and Sungas

- [Int] "'Fine Grey Pottery' was transported either on already existing trading routes or
  it is connected to the dispersion of Buddhism, thus facilitating the formation of such
  paths. Interestingly, boats and ships are often part of illustrations on Buddhist sites
  since 2nd century B. C. E. (Ray 2007: 60)." (p. 113)
- [Int] "The ﬁnal production of 'Fine Grey Pottery' in the 2nd century B. C. E. might be
  explainable with the arising of the Sunga empire (185–73 B. C. E.), after the defeat of
  the last Mauryan king." (p. 113)
- [Int] "most ﬁnds were discovered only outside the supposed manufacture in the North, yet
  it seems they were custom-made particularly for Southern taste. This is again pointing to
  the above said Buddhist campaign, which is at least known from Mauryan King Asoka."
  (p. 114)
- Note: the Buddhist link is offered as one of two possibilities ("either ... or"), and the
  Sunga link is "might be explainable". Neither is argued from evidence in the article.
- South Arabian agency is not discussed.

### 4.4 Southern ports as the intermediary; the emporium

- [Int] Abstract: "It is suggested that they were transported from ports in the South of
  the Subcontinent. There, an emporium had formed in the course of time because favourable
  sailing conditions allow direct contacts of Western and Eastern Indian Ocean." (p. 95)
- [Int] "It is suggested here, that all the ﬁnds of 'Fine Grey Pottery' reached their ﬁnal
  destination via the southern tip of the Indian Subcontinent, where exchange routes along
  the east coast developed since at least the 5th / 4th centuries B. C. E. This region
  beneﬁted from the location in the centre of 'trade winds' that enabled and facilitated
  the trans-continental East-West sailing." (p. 114)
- Imitation as the tracer for ports [Int]: "The southern made 'imitation' of the dish with
  beaked rim (often mistaken for true 'Rouletted Ware') will be once more described and
  proposed as an important key type to prove the actual ports of embarkation." (p. 95)
- **Malabar [Doc/Int]:** "The numerous ﬁnd spots reﬂect a pathway of trading in
  North-South direction along the Eastern coast of India, where so far no ﬁnds are known
  east of Bangladesh15. Most probably this is not due to an academic void. Lack of ﬁnds
  applies also to most parts of the Western Indian coast, besides the southernmost region
  as demonstrated at sites like Pattanam (Cherian 2011)." (p. 113). Schenk does not name
  Muziris anywhere in the text (it appears only on the borrowed Fig. 2 map).
- Ship supplies [Int]: "The easily recognisable beaked rim belongs thereto, but also
  common potteries such as water – and storage jars and other dishes that certainly were
  needed on the ships on-route." (p. 114)

### 4.5 Overlapping networks: the eastern arm

- [Int] "The ﬁnds of 'Fine Grey Pottery' in Southeast Asia evidence an earlier formation of
  the Eastern maritime network." (p. 95)
- [Doc, from her own study] "Thanks to an invitation by Ambra Calo, I was able to study
  the 'Fine Grey Pottery' from a recent excavation project at Sembiran / Pacung, Bali14.
  This revealed also the earliest subtype A of Fine Grey Type 11 ('Rouletted Ware')
  besides some further early types." (p. 113)
- [Rep: Bouvet 2012] Khao Sam Kheo: "All the presented types are familiar at
  Tissamaharama (e.g. Bouvet 2012: 227 ﬁg. 84). Attention should be paid to the occurrence
  of Fine Grey fabric 1 featuring the Fine Grey types 1 (Bouvet 2012: 237 ﬁg. 87) and 2
  (Bouvet 2012: 236 ﬁg. 86). Exactly similar vessels arrived in the 4th and 3rd century B.
  C. E. at Tissamaharama." (p. 113)
- Local imitations in Southeast Asia [Rep: Calo et al.; Bouvet]: "At Sembiran / Pacung it
  was possible to identify beaked rim type of clear regional clay (Calo et al. in press: 8
  ﬁg. 5) ... And also at Batujaya on Java, local potters had been apparently inspired to
  produce an imitation (Bouvet 2012: 285 ﬁg. 107,6)." (p. 114). "However, at least one
  bottom fragment at Bali – of clear non-local, possibly South Asian paste – contained the
  'rouletted' decoration." (p. 114)
- Spices: "The distribution pattern of 'Fine Grey Pottery' includes Bali which is situated
  on the pathway towards the Molucca islands known for their endemic spices nutmeg, mace
  and cloves." (p. 105)
- Chinese source [Rep]: "Also Chinese written source seem to report of such an itinerary
  during the reign of Han Emperor Wu (141–87 B. C. E.)3." Fn. 3: "Gungwu 1958; 18–19;
  Borell 2011: 63– 65; Borell / Bellina / Chaisuwan 2014: 110." (p. 105)

### 4.6 Disagreements with named scholars

- **Wheeler, Ghosh and Krishna Deva 1946**: RW "considered as Roman"; corrected by Begley
  and Tissa (p. 97).
- **The "southern production" school (unnamed here)**: "However, a dispute is still going
  on whether 'Rouletted Ware' was actually fabricated in the South (summarized and
  commented in Pavan / Schenk 2012). Which is in fact the case as indeed a type of dish
  with identical shape was part of the range of products in Southern pottery workshops
  (ﬁg. 9,3; 15)." (p. 105). She concedes southern production of the *form* while
  restricting the *name* to the northern fine grey fabric.
- **Rao 1966 and Orton 1992 on RPW**: the category is "highly problematic"; Orton's
  compilation is "a conglomeration of varied forms" with medieval types (p. 99).
- **Hudson 2004**, fn. 15: "Hudson 2004: 210 with reference to Wheeler 1946: 50 plate
  30B; let alone the fabric also the rhombic decorated fragments on chart 4 (100–150 cm)
  are very different to the stamped leaves of Wheeler type 141." This rejects a claimed
  find east of Bangladesh (Burma).
- **Behera 2007**: fn. 13 notes Manikapatna pieces "(named as 'Rouletted Ware')", implying
  misnaming of Types 3–5.

## 6. Comparisons a historian could build on

1. **Sumhuram's late RW against Trench 3B.** Pavan & Schenk 2012 match Sumhuram Fig. 3/1–2
   to the late inward-curving RW group. Schenk 2014 Figs 6–7 show where RW sits in Trench
   3B, level by level: mendable in c1 (B–D, 21,60–20,80 MSL), isolated and worn in c2 (A,
   21,75–21,60). A sherd-by-sherd comparison of the Sumhuram profiles with Trench 3B
   nos. 11–27 would test whether "late" means late c1 (as Pavan & Schenk claim) or c2.

2. **Wheeler type 18 = Fine Grey Type 9.** Sumhuram Fig. 4/1 (red-fired, room A30, BF1,
   with Sumhuram and Yashuril coins) is a Type 9 in Fabric 3. Schenk 2014 puts Fabric 3 at
   phases b to c1, with dashes into c2, and Type 9 first in phase b. That gives a Tissa
   window of 3rd to 2nd century BCE for the Sumhuram base, which can be checked against the
   Yashuril coin date (2nd century BC, Sedov 2008c).

3. **The imitation dish as port tracer.** Tissa Form G1 rim type 9 (Fig. 15) with subtypes
   A–C that mirror Type 11 A–C. Pavan & Schenk record imitation RW at Sumhuram, one piece
   riveted. Schenk proposes the imitation "to prove the actual ports of embarkation"
   (p. 95). Pattanam's published "RW" could be sorted into true Type 11 versus G1/9 and
   into subtypes A–C. This is the most direct Malabar test either article makes possible.

4. **Pattanam as the one western-coast exception.** "Lack of ﬁnds applies also to most
   parts of the Western Indian coast, besides the southernmost region as demonstrated at
   sites like Pattanam (Cherian 2011)." (p. 113). Pavan & Schenk go further and name
   "Muziris (Pattanam)" as the nexus at the time of RW. Schenk 2014 does not repeat the
   Muziris claim and her Fig. 3 draws the westward route from the southern tip. The two
   articles by the same specialist differ in how far they commit on Malabar.

5. **Dressel 2–4 at Tissa against Pattanam.** One Dressel 2–4 fragment arrives at Tissa
   with Parthian glazed ware in 2nd-century-CE contexts (p. 98). Pattanam's amphora and
   torpedo-jar record could be set against Tissa's: at Tissa torpedo jars appear "later
   than 3rd century C.E., mainly after 500 C.E.". If Pattanam has torpedo jars in earlier
   contexts, that is a difference in how the two places were supplied.

6. **Incised owner's marks.** Figs 6/8, 9/2a, 9/4a, 15/2 and 15/7 show incised signs on
   RW and on southern imitations [Mine]; none is discussed here. Pavan & Schenk 2012: 200
   say marking vessels "was most popular during the BC periods at Tissamaharama" and cite
   Brahmi graffiti at Sumhuram (Bukharin 2002). Falk 2014 in the same ZAAK volume (p. 45)
   publishes the Tissa graffiti and would allow the Sumhuram inscription to be compared with
   dated Tissa marks.

7. **Repair by riveting.** Schenk 2014 Fig. 8/3 photographs a rivet in an RW sherd
   without comment; Pavan & Schenk 2012 record riveted RW at Tissa and a riveted imitation
   RW at Sumhuram. Repair is a measurable behaviour that bears on whether these were
   traded goods or personal possessions.
