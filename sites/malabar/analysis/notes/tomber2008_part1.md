## 2. Method material (ceramic archaeology for a Classics student)

### 2.1 Why pottery is harder than coins

- p. 38 (PDF 40), FACT/INTERP: "Pottery is only occasionally mentioned in Classical documents and then normally as a container for foodstuffs. Nevertheless it was sometimes a trade item in its own right, or at other times travelled as a personal possession or gift."
- p. 38: "Its identification is more problematic than that of coins since pottery is difficult to classify by description alone, and sometimes even from photographs or drawings, particularly in the case of body sherds."
- p. 37 (PDF 39), on coins for contrast: "their potential as the most precisely identified and largest single category of Roman finds in India makes them essential for interpreting trade."

### 2.2 Form + fabric; petrography; geological maps

- p. 38–39 (PDF 40–41), FACT (method statement): "Source identification of pottery can be determined in a number of ways, with the best results through the combination of vessel form and clay fabric. The study of clay fabrics is borrowed from geology and is based particularly on the identification of aplastic inclusions in the clay, either by eye or using a binocular or petrological microscope. The last enables the most precise classification of rocks and minerals and this technique was used extensively for identification of the fabrics described below."
- p. 39: "Sometimes sources can be assigned by matching the fabric with geological deposits, for which a geological map is invaluable. However, many mineral inclusions — such as quartz — are ubiquitous. In these cases typology is essential, for source areas can be suggested through distribution, with the greatest concentration likely to be in the home area."
- **Imitation vs import**, p. 39: "Nevertheless, pottery shapes are imitated outside their region for a variety of reasons, and detection of this relies on fabric analysis."
- Worked example of the import/imitation check (Aksumite pottery), p. 52 (PDF 54): "it was important to confirm that the vessels were actually from East Africa rather than an East African style made in Egypt. Invaluably, sherds and thin sections from Aksum itself were made available to me for comparison by David Phillipson and Jacke Phillips." The method is comparison against reference thin sections from the source site.
- Worked example of geological-map sourcing (Whole-mouth Cooking pot), p. 51 (PDF 53): "The fabric is basaltic and consultation with geological maps shows Aden, an important region for Indo-Roman trade, to have suitable natural resources." The same reasoning is applied to Red Sandy Ware, p. 54 (PDF 56): "Geological maps show a suitable area in the region surrounding Asmara and Massawa, north of the Periplus port of Adulis, where there is a juncture of sedimentary and volcanic resources."

### 2.3 Amphorae: form identifies the type, fabric identifies the source

- p. 43 (PDF 45), FACT: "Amphora shapes may or may not be exclusive to a single production area or fabric. One of the most popular Early Roman amphorae, Dressel's Types 2-4, was produced in most Roman provinces, but originated from an earlier Greek amphora made on Kos — the Koan amphora. Although it is a simple matter to identify the Dressel 2-4 when a handle or base is present, fabric is necessary to distinguish the specific source area."
- p. 43, the limits of petrography, REPORTED (Slane; Whitbread): "Even with visual inspection and petrographic analysis, it can be difficult to attribute some sherds to source area. Slane noted the problem of distinguishing the Koan fabric from some western Dressel 2-4s, while Whitbread's petrographic work indicated an overlap between Koan and other eastern fabrics (Slane 1992: 204; Whitbread 1995: 99). In the case of Koan amphorae, typology and stamps remain an important means for identification."
- p. 43, a reference collection: "Other fabrics can also be difficult to attribute to production area (particularly body sherds), but the establishment of an ongoing fabric database of samples from India provides a framework for classification as new sherds are recovered."
- p. 42–43, typology history: "Systematic classification of Roman amphorae began with Heinrich Dressel's typology of 1899, which still forms the basis for modern studies. As a result the dating, source and distribution of many types is well-known and need not be summarised." Terminology follows "Keay & Williams 2005", with "Late Roman (LR) and Dressel typologies consistently employed."
- p. 42 (PDF 44), the reassessment that made Indian amphorae identifiable, REPORTED: "Prior to the 1990s, and the reassessment of assemblages from Arikamedu (Will 1992; Slane 1992), no amphorae from India were precisely identified to source and could be understood only more generally from illustrations (particularly Wheeler et al. 1946: figs 9-10)."
- p. 46 (PDF 48), what can and cannot be identified from publication alone: "Unlike amphorae, it is fairly easy to identify RW and RPW from publication alone, and from this adjust the quantity of Roman pottery identified in India." **[Note]** Read the other way round, this says that amphora identifications from published drawings alone are not secure.

### 2.4 Linings: bitumen, beeswax, pitch

- Torpedo jars, p. 39 (PDF 41), REPORTED (Stern et al. 2008) + INTERP: "A bitumen lining (Stern et al. 2008), visible on most vessels, indicates that they were used for the transportation of liquid."
- p. 42 (PDF 44), diagnostic value: "The thick black lining helps to distinguish Torpedo sherds, for in India this is much better preserved than on Roman vessels."
- South Arabian Organic Storage Jars, p. 50 (PDF 52), FACT (analysis by Stacey): "Vessels are normally lined with a dark coating; using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry this coating has been identified by Rebecca Stacey as bitumen on some samples, beeswax on others. Their function as containers for liquid is reinforced by remnants of plaster seal around the mouth".
- Pitch on wood rather than pots, p. 73 (PDF 75): teak at Berenike "imported sometimes as finished objects, but also as planks and boards (occasionally pitched) that could represent remnants of ships or packing crates".
- **[Note]** Roman amphora pitch linings are not discussed in this range beyond the comparison quoted above.

### 2.5 Manufacturing traces (forming technique as a provenance marker)

This is the single most important method passage for a Malabar thesis.

- p. 46–47 (PDF 48–49), FACT: "The Wheeler Type 24s from the Red Sea were formed using at least two different techniques. The first displays internal wiping with an organic material considered to be a by-product of bamboo tools, still used today in India (Tomber & Begley 2000: 156 and plate 3-4)."
- p. 47: "Because of the distinctive nature of this wiping, it was hoped to source this pottery within India by locating parallels. With this aim, a search of collections from sites involved in Indo-Roman trade — such as Arikamedu, Nevasa and Nagara — was undertaken in 1998, but no matches were located. It was not until the site at Pattanam was discovered (see Chapter 5) and the pottery from it examined in late 2003 that a match was found."
- p. 47, ethnographic analogy, REPORTED (Saraswati & Behura 1966): "A return to the ethnographic literature was equally positive, describing a technique today known, only to the potters of North Kerala, as scooping. A ball of clay is roughly shaped, bamboo tools are then used to hollow the inside and define the rim and neck, followed by internal beating to achieve the correct thickness. Finally the vessel is burnished inside and out (Saraswati & Behura 1966: 81-3)."
- p. 47, INTERP: "It seems that the vessels found in Egypt and some seen in Kerala were less carefully finished, so that the bamboo marks are still visible. In this way a direct link between Kerala and the Red Sea was established."
- p. 47–48: "Different working techniques are visible on other CRSW sherds, but are less distinct than the organic marks and it has not yet been possible to locate their source within India. Nevertheless, petrographic examination of the Wheeler Type 24 and related forms found in Egypt confirms that numerous production centres were involved."
- Paddle-and-anvil, p. 48 (PDF 50): "A distinct subclass was produced using the paddle and anvil technique with a grooved paddle (Paddle Impressed Wares; Fig. 7, no. 6; Begley 2004c: 202-5; Selvakumar 2004: 616-17)."
- Torpedo interior, p. 39: the fabric is "generally fine and better sorted with finger impressions rather than wheelmarks on the inside."
- Handmade vs wheelmade, p. 48: Organic Black Ware is "A coarse black, handmade fabric with very thick walls".

### 2.6 Temper and fabric descriptions (as models for describing a sherd)

- Organic Black Ware, p. 48 (PDF 50): "Despite their thickness, the vessels are light in weight, so that when it was first encountered I nicknamed it 'light ware'. The fabric had been tracked previously in India by Sunil Gupta, whose own pet name for it was 'spongy ware'. The lightness results from its vesicular fabric caused by organic temper, particularly of rice (Fig. 8; Tomber et al. in preparation)." Fig. 8 is a thin-section photomicrograph, "field view 0.87 mm".
- Organic Storage Jars (South Arabia), p. 50: "high-footed storage jars in calcareous clay, frequently iron free, with organic tempering". "While the fabric is not dissimilar to some Egyptian ones, the form is distinct". **[Note]** This is a clean teaching case of form overriding an ambiguous fabric.
- Aksumite fabrics, p. 52–53: "The first has mudstone and trachytic igneous inclusions and compares well with samples from Aksum; the second fabric is micaceous with lightly metamorphosed rocks, and while not represented among the Aksum samples, is geologically compatible within the region (Tomber 2005b: 46)." Red Sandy Ware: "distinctive calcareous, granitic-tempered clay and is decorated with incised bosses known in the Aksumite repertoire".
- Torpedo fabric, p. 39: "The fabric is unlike typical Indian ones, but not dissimilar to the Late Roman Amphora 1 (LR1) fabric".
- Rouletted Ware, p. 44 (PDF 46): "fine quality black and/or red/orange slipped fabric and decoration". Coarse Red-slipped Wares, p. 46: "Coarse red wares, frequently with red slip that may be burnished".

### 2.7 Slip and surface as a source of misattribution

- RW, p. 44: "The most persistent of these is the Rouletted Ware dish (RW; Fig. 5, no. 1) or Wheeler Type 1 (Wheeler et al. 1946: fig. 12), whose fine quality black and/or red/orange slipped fabric and decoration has contributed to this misattribution."
- RPW, p. 46: "Another Indian fine ware historically considered Roman is Red Polished Ware (RPW; Fig. 5, nos 3-4) — here the confusion with Roman wares arose because of the similarity of its surface with Roman red-slipped wares. Nevertheless, RPW consists of a radically different set of forms, the most characteristic the sprinkler (Fig. 5, no. 3)."
- Conical amphorae, p. 42: "While in some of the older literature they are described as Roman, their Indian origin has been accepted for some time."
- The definition problem for slipped cooking pots, p. 107 (PDF 109): Sedov and Benvenuti "are cautious in attributing these vessels to India, as although slipped they do not belong to RPW, instead to CRSW. The problem here lies in the definition of RPW (Schenk in press). These cooking pots can be sourced to India although, unlike RPW, need not come from the North."

### 2.8 Use-wear and function

- Sooting identifies cooking pots, p. 46: "Vessels from the Red Sea are frequently sooted on the outside, establishing their use as cooking vessels." Also for Organic Black Ware ("frequently sooted", p. 48) and Red Sandy Ware ("frequently sooted from their use as cooking pots", p. 54).
- Sealing, p. 75 (PDF 77): "Although these vessels show no visible sign of having stoppers, in India Early Historic pots were sometimes covered on top and tied with a string that was then secured by a stamped, ceramic seal (Ray 1998: 42), none of which have been identified from Egypt."
- Unsooted jars are read as storage, p. 48: "Most common are a range of unsooted jars interpreted as storage containers".

### 2.9 Inscriptions on pots: stamps, graffiti, dipinti, painted marks

- p. 25–26 (PDF 27–28), general: "Graffiti comprise a much more opportunistic category. On pottery, for instance, they often provide information on who used the vessel and where, and in the Eastern Desert they provide insights into their ethnicity."
- A pre-firing mark proves the place of manufacture, p. 50: "Further confirmation for a Yemeni source ... comes from a vessel found in Egypt with a prefiring painted South Arabian graffito (Tomber 2004b: 353, fig. 6)." **[checked against render; the OCR reads "3538"]** **[Note]** The teaching point is that a mark made before firing was made where the pot was made, whereas a post-firing graffito only shows who handled it later.
- Primary vs secondary inscriptions: Fig. 13 captions call both Myos Hormos graffiti "secondary" (South Arabic monogram on an Indian jar; Tamil-Brahmi on an Indian storage jar), and p. 78 notes "a primary inscription in Epigraphic South Arabian of the proper name ydm (Gragg 1979)" at Berenike.
- The orientation of a graffito as evidence of the pot's state, p. 74 (PDF 76): "The writing is upside down, so the pot was inverted when it was inscribed suggesting either that it was full but stoppered, or that it was empty."
- Name inscribed on an amphora linked to an archive, p. 81–82 (PDF 83–84), REPORTED (Van Rengen 2003): "At Myos Hormos a certain Miresis is inscribed on a Dressel 2-4 from Cilicia. The name is rare, but belongs to one of Nicanor's sons, active between AD 41 and 62 (Van Rengen 2003: 43)." **[Note]** Whether this is a painted titulus or an incised graffito is not stated.
- Maker's stamp, p. 91–92 (PDF 93–94), REPORTED: "A rare find is a South Gaulish amphora with the maker's mark <MATUR> (Fattovich & Bard 1995: figs 2-4) of the potter Maturus (Laubenheimer 1985: 429, fig. 201, no. 30)."
- **Tituli picti** are not mentioned by that term in my range.

### 2.10 Residuality and curation

- Residual finds as evidence of an earlier phase, p. 60 (PDF 62), FACT (Southampton excavations): "Ptolemaic finds recovered residually by Southampton included three coins of the second and first centuries BC (D. Peacock, pers. comm.) and rare sherds of Ptolemaic pottery that reinforce the possibility of an earlier foundation for the site."
- Curation vs dating, p. 74: "Most of the Indian fine wares from the Red Sea were in contexts of the late first century BC or first century AD. This supports Schenk's revised dating schema, although if strictly applied some sherds were curated before being discarded. Certainly, two sherds from a late first-century BC/early first-century AD context were reworked (Begley & Tomber 1999: cat. nos 4 and 9)."
- Residual Indian wares later, p. 75: CRSW "were most common during the Early Roman period and may be residual thereafter." p. 77 (PDF 79): "Indian ceramics were less common in the Late Roman period than earlier and they may be residual. If coarse ware importation continued, it was on a much reduced scale."
- A single-period satellite site as a control for residuality, p. 76 (PDF 78), INTERP: "Although many of the deposits at Berenike had a residual element to them, importantly pepper, Indo-Pacific beads, teak and sapphire also occurred at its satellite site Shenshef ... Located 23 km south-south-west of Berenike, the lack of any Early Roman presence demonstrates the continuation of Indian imports into the fifth and early sixth centuries AD." **[Note]** This is an elegant method: a site occupied only late cannot contain early residual material, so it isolates the late signal.
- Residual coins, p. 76–78: the Kshatrapa coin and the Aphilas coin were each "found residually" in the Berenike church.
- Table 1 (pp. 83–87) marks most Late Roman Indian pottery at Berenike and Shenshef as "residual?" (see §3).

### 2.11 Dating methods mentioned

- Type-fossil dating of local pottery by imports, p. 14 (PDF 16): Wheeler "established a pottery typology for local pottery dated in reference to Roman ceramic type-fossils, still relied upon throughout South India." **[Note]** This is exactly the mechanism by which a mis-dated import contaminates a regional chronology (see Ravitchandirane 2007 cited in the same passage).
- Long-lived wares cannot date contexts, p. 51: Mesopotamian Glazed Ware: "With production continuing over a long period of time, possibly from as early as the third century BC into the Early Islamic period (Kennet 2004: 29-30), vessels are difficult to date unless specific shapes or decoration are present."
- Indian coarse wares, p. 46: "Indian coarse wares exported to the West are more difficult to provenance and date, since some were produced from the Early Historic period to the present day in both North and South India."
- Radiocarbon on shells associated with pottery, p. 90 (PDF 92), REPORTED (Peacock 2007a): "in the south-west corner of the site four 14C dates were taken from shells associated with some early local pottery. Calibrated, these dates range between 10 BC and AD 360, and indicate that this area of the site was active during the time of the Periplus (Peacock 2007a: 93)."
- Dated ostraca, p. 24 (PDF 26): Berenike customs dump "two precisely dated ostraca: 6 August 33 and 20 September 61" and "the rubbish was accumulated between c. 40/5 and 70/5 AD."
- Coins for site chronology (Marsa Nakari, p. 65; Qana, p. 104: "The site dating relies primarily on South Arabian coinage and imported pottery").

### 2.12 Reuse

- The Myos Hormos amphora jetty, p. 60: "an area over 60 m in length consisting of hundreds of complete to near-complete pots, many in upright positions ... they were laid to form a jetty (see front cover) and also to consolidate waterlogged land, as a 'hard' (an artificial foreshore or landing place) (Peacock & Blue 2006: 67-74). Most of the pots were Roman amphorae of Egyptian and Italian origin dating to the late Augustan period".
- Decanting, p. 81: "one ostracon that details the reuse of a foreign amphora for local (i.e. Egyptian) wine (ibid.: 18, fig. 1)" [ibid. = Bagnall et al. 2000].
- Aqaba amphorae at Matara "reused as water pipes or infant coffins" (p. 92); at Adulis "some were reused" (p. 91).

### 3.2 Catalogue by ware or object

#### A. Indian Rouletted Ware (RW) and related ware

- **Figure:** Fig. 5 nos 1–2 (PDF 47, p. 45). No. 1 is a Wheeler Type 1 dish rim; no. 2 is a "Related Ware" vessel in the same fabric that has a small stamped or impressed animal motif drawn on the interior (visible on the render; the motif is not described in the text).
- **Site:** Myos Hormos. **Context:** not stated. **Date of the sherds:** not stated.
- **Provenance attribution:** India, specifically the Ganges delta (Table 1: "India (NE)").
- **Evidence given:**
  - REPORTED (Begley 1988): "Apparent fabric similarities between the classic rouletted fabric and the earlier, Indian Northern Black Polished Ware (NBP), and dissimilarities in form with Roman types, lent force to Begley's argument." (p. 44)
  - REPORTED (Begley 2004b): "Begley considered RW a South Indian product, largely on distributional and typological grounds".
  - REPORTED (Ford et al. 2005; Gogte 1997, 2001): "A number of scientific analyses have confirmed that a single source region may be responsible for Rouletted and related wares (e.g. Ford et al. 2005). X-ray diffraction conducted by Gogte provided uniform results for RW samples from throughout India, Sri Lanka and Vietnam, NBP and clay from Chandraketugarh (1997, 2001). From this Gogte identified the Ganges Delta as the most likely source with the epicentre in the Chandraketugarh-Tamluk area."
  - FACT (Tomber's autopsy): "On personal examination, the large quantity of Rouletted and other fine grey wares from Chandraketugarh appear visually similar to those from Egypt, Arikamedu and other Indian sites."
- **Dating, as reported (p. 45):** "the widest bracket being 400 BC-AD 300 (Ford et al. 2005: 911). Commonly it is placed between the second century BC and the first century AD (Suresh 2004: 98) [checked against render; the OCR reads "58"], although based on Arikamedu Begley considered it to be first century BC to third century AD (2004b: 639-40). Most recently Schenk has argued that production ceased in the first century BC (2006: 136-8). In all dating schemes, the first occurrence of RW predates Roman sigillata in India."
- **Origin of the rouletting decoration:** unresolved. Slane links it with Hellenistic black wares (1992: 212); Salles suggests the Gulf (2002: 199).
- **Distribution, REPORTED (Schenk 2006):** "Curiously it has concentrations in Bangladesh and West Bengal, followed by an absence before resurfacing in peninsular India (2006: fig. 3). She suggests that Wheeler 1 and a few other forms (including Fig. 5, no. 2) in the same fabric were produced in the north specifically for the southern market".

## 4. Arguments

### 4.1 Wheeler and the Roman-centred reading of Arikamedu

- p. 13 (PDF 15), REPORTED + INTERP: Wheeler's publications "reinforced an existing orthodoxy of Indo-Roman trade clearly set out by E.H. Warmington, as being Roman controlled (1928: 1-2). In this schema, Arikamedu was a foreign city colonised by Roman traders during the first and second centuries AD (Wheeler 1951: 354-8)."
- p. 14, INTERP: "While Arikamedu has held a unique place in Indo-Roman trade, because of its concentration of Roman artefacts (Wheeler found 116 sherds of amphorae and 38 of terra sigillata), it has also maintained this position because of the influence of Wheeler. These two factors have together resulted in Arikamedu having a 'stranglehold on Indian historical writing' (Ray 1998: 49), as well as on Early Historic chronology (Ravitchandrane 2007)."
- p. 14, INTERP: "Wheeler's framework obviously owes much to his time and place, being unashamedly a member of the British Empire."
- p. 14, on the durability of his model: "Equally resilient is his interpretive model of terminal and transit trade. Transit involved Indian centres that served as entrepots between the West and East and where Western finds were relatively rare; terminal referred to centres where local products were in demand in exchange for Western goods (Wheeler 1951: 352-4)."
- p. 14: "Despite active scholarship in India, Wheeler's vision of Indo-Roman trade went essentially unchallenged until the re-excavation of Arikamedu in the late 1980s by Begley (et al. 1996, 2004). Begley's choice of Arikamedu is in itself a potent reminder of its role as the principal Indian site for Indo-Roman trade."
- p. 18, the book's programme: "A final chapter integrates the evidence from these different regions to achieve an overview of their interactions through time, thus departing from Wheeler's more colonial vision."
- p. 18, the parallel to Wheeler she draws herself: "It was the pottery especially that led Wheeler to Arikamedu and so too it is new pottery evidence that has provided the impetus for this review of Indo-Roman trade."
- Wheeler on coins as bullion (1954b), p. 34: "He based his argument largely on the lack of local coinage in precious metal, although this was somewhat overstated (Lal Gupta 1991: 125)." This is a mild disagreement, attributed to Lal Gupta.
- Misattributions descended from Wheeler-era thinking (RW, RPW, conical amphorae, torpedo jars called "Roman"), pp. 39–46.

### 4.2 Pre-Roman maritime exchange

- p. 15 (PDF 17), REPORTED (Salles; Ratnagar; Kitchen): "From the fifth millennium BC, small networks operated within the larger realm, exploiting the two main arteries of the Arabian/Persian Gulf (hereafter the Gulf) and the Red Sea (Salles 1998: 58, 66). Bronze Age trade between north-west India and the Gulf (Ratnagar 2006) and Old Kingdom links between Egypt and East Africa (Kitchen 2004) are but two examples. Under the Seleucids (312-60 BC), during the Hellenistic period, Indian trade reached the Eastern Mediterranean via the Gulf".
- The monsoon, p. 18 (PDF 20), REPORTED (Tchernia 1997b): "Although long harnessed by Indians, Arabs and Persians, and known by the Greeks from the time of Alexander, they were not systematically exploited by the Romans until the Augustan era (Tchernia 1997b: 250-61)."
- Material hints: the Red-and-Black Megalithic Ware at Myos Hormos "hints at pre-Roman contact" (p. 75). "In all dating schemes, the first occurrence of RW predates Roman sigillata in India." (p. 45)
- But at the Egyptian ports, FACT (p. 71): "Archaeological evidence for imported goods at the Egyptian sites during the Ptolemaic period is virtually non-existent." The only Ptolemaic elephant evidence is "Rare elephant tusks ... and a single tooth from a Ptolemaic deposit".
- PME 26 on Eudaimon Arabia (quoted p. 102): "since vessels from India did not go on to Egypt and those from Egypt did not dare sail to the places further on but came only this far, it used to receive the cargoes of both". Tomber calls it "an important trans-shipment point between India and the West prior to the Periplus".
- Khor Rori, REPORTED (Avanzini 2002; Avanzini & Sedov 2005), p. 106: "A more recent chronology tentatively suggests that it was founded before Qana in the third century BC to facilitate Hadrami trade with the Gulf and Oman".

### 4.3 Indigenous (Indian, Arabian, African) commercial agency

- The project's framing, p. 7 (Preface): contact "is a much broader experience, not only physically, but incorporating traders and merchants from outside the Roman world." p. 17 sets the questions: "Who controlled the trade? Who conducted the trade? Where did the traders live?"
- Indians at the Red Sea, INTERP (p. 73): "Graffiti in Indian languages strongly imply that Indians were living at Red Sea sites." p. 75: Indian coarse-ware distribution indicates "their usage was predominantly for Indians." p. 72: rice "may have been eaten mainly by resident Indians". Myos Hormos smithing area "seems, by its pottery, to be associated with East African and Indian foreigners (Thomas & Masser 2006: 133, 137-8; Thomas 2007: 154-6)" (p. 61).
- Indian ships, REPORTED (Whitewright 2007a), p. 63: "Whitewright argues that Indian skippered ships may have had more difficultly reaching Myos Hormos given their unfamiliarity with northerly winds and would have preferred stopping at Berenike." Sails and planks, p. 73: "Alternative explanations exist for the planks and sails: Roman-style ships were built in India, Roman ships were repaired in India, or Indian and Roman ships became technologically more similar through time." Tomber does not choose between these.
- Indian selection of coins, REPORTED (Turner 1989; MacDowall 1998; Burnett 1998), pp. 33, 36: Turner "thought these were selected by Indians and buried near to the centre of trade", and "certain issues of reliable weight were recognised by Indian traders and were specially selected for trade with India".
- Yavanas, REPORTED (Ray; Thapar; Champakalakshmi; De Romanis), p. 27: "Yavanas, an umbrella term that includes foreigners of diverse origin, were important in facilitating external trade, but they also assumed other roles."
- Arabian agency: "According to Cosmas, the Himyarites served as middlemen for the Romans in the sixth century" (p. 101). Aksumites "are generally considered to have been more important to overseas commerce than the Romans during this period, acting as their middlemen (Sidebotham 1986: 46-7)" (p. 88). Sedov sees Indian residents at Qana from the quantity of Indian cooking pots (p. 107).
- Ethnicity is not tied to pot use, INTERP (p. 78): the Greek graffito on a South Arabian jar and the South Arabic monogram on an Indian jar, "Like the Dressel 2-4, these two examples suggest that pottery usage was not restricted within ethnic groups."

### 4.4 Overlapping regional networks (Red Sea, Gulf, Bay of Bengal)

- p. 17, INTERP: "The Roman Red Sea, East Africa, the Gulf, South Arabia and India can be conceived as a series of interlocking regions, each with its own complex history, culture, social and political systems."
- The Red Sea as a funnel, p. 57: "In the complex network of regions it is the Red Sea that served as a funnel for goods from the East to the Roman Empire."
- Gulf vs Red Sea visible in pottery: Torpedo jars absent from the Red Sea (p. 42). Adulis's Mesopotamian Glazed Ware "points to a different sea route originating in the Gulf" (p. 91). Hafun, INTERP (p. 99): "The finds at Hafun can be seen to reflect the terminus for several different voyages to HW. In terms of quantity, direct trips from the Red Sea and another via the Gulf seem likely; a direct route from India is not ruled out but is less likely to account for the small number of Asian finds." Later "the route was dominated by the Gulf, reflecting the rise in Sasanian power (Smith & Wright 1988: 140)" (p. 100).
- Overland and seaborne networks in parallel: Mesopotamian finds at the Red Sea come "indirectly via the Palmyrenes" (p. 80). Nabataean pottery at Marib, Qaryat al-Faw, Khor Rori and Qana "attest to overland travel" (p. 102). Qana: "A sea itinerary would account for Indian and Mesopotamian pottery" (p. 104).
- Bay of Bengal: RW "followed both coastal and river paths" (Schenk) (p. 45). Gogte's Ganges-delta source (p. 44). Paddle Impressed Ware sources in "South India and the Chandraketugarh region" (p. 50). Schenk's gift-exchange explanation for RW distribution "related to the spread of Buddhism or Mauryan diplomatic activities, rather than through Indo-Roman trade" (p. 46), to which Tomber replies: "However, a different explanation may apply to vessels imported into Egypt (Chapter 3, p. 74)." Her alternative at p. 74 is that fine wares in Egypt "may also relate to Indian communities, rather than representing trade in ceramics or their contents."
- Regional sourcing as "geographical control", p. 50: "Although many of the Indian wares are difficult to source precisely, pottery from the north (RPW, Organic Black Ware), east (RW, Paddle Impressed Wares) and south-west (CRSW with organic impressions) are identified and provide some geographical control for assessing the embarkation point for Indian pottery."

### 4.5 Ports as intermediaries / entrepôts

- p. 56 (PDF 58), INTERP: "Not only were indirect routes frequently advantageous, but entrepots for the collection and redistribution of goods an important feature of the ancient economy. Within the Roman sphere, Alexandria can be readily identified as a regional entrepot through both historical sources and the richness of its artefact assemblages ... Sites containing a wide variety of goods are strong contenders for entrepots or emporia and this criterion can be used to define them in South Arabia and India."
- The criterion applied: "essentially everything that was available in Alexandria was found along the Egyptian Red Sea coast in varying amounts" (p. 81). At Qana imports form "up to 75% of the ceramics", and the "assemblage is rich and diverse with sources from the Roman world, East Africa, India and Mesopotamia all well represented" (p. 104).
- Khor Rori as a facilitator, INTERP building on Sedov & Benvenuti and Peacock et al., p. 107: the absence of Qana-region basalt is "further evidence for Khor Rori as a facilitator for Qana rather than an overseas port in its own right." This stands against Avanzini, who argues that the monumental architecture shows it was not "merely a satellite of Qana" (p. 106). Tomber reports both sides.
- Socotra as a meeting point, p. 108: text fragments "tell of Abyssinian (East African), West Indian, South Arabian, Armenian and Palmyrene visitors during the Early Roman period (De Geest 2006: 19)."
- Documents conflate the port with the product's source, p. 54: "The documents confused questions of source, with the provenance of goods equated with the port where they were acquired."
- Muziris as an entrepôt appears only in ch. 6 (outside my range). In my range, the Muziris papyrus is described on p. 25.

### 4.6 What pottery can and cannot say about merchants and routes

- p. 56, INTERP: "The ability to provenance archaeological remains is a critical first step in any attempt to reconstruct ancient trade ... Nevertheless, simple matching of find spots and sources does not necessarily trace the routes by which they travelled. Assessing the quantity of objects can go some way towards addressing this problem. However, the presence of Roman objects, for example, need not equate with the presence of Romans (e.g. Salles 1993: n. 46)."
- Meaning of imports, p. 56: "What, for example, was the meaning of Roman wine in India? Was it intended for locals or foreigners, were the contents enjoyed by a range of consumers? If not enjoyed, were they valued in another way, perhaps bestowing status on the owner?"
- The limit of a small assemblage, p. 99: "At Heis and Damo the preponderance of fine wares over amphorae, together with the glass, highlights a non-utilitarian strand of exchange. The quantity, however, could be explained by a single shipload."
- Mixed assemblages reflect provisioning, not ethnicity. REPORTED (Smith & Wright 1988: 138), p. 99: they "saw the mixed nature of both Hafun assemblages as resulting from ports recently visited for provisions, rather than reflecting the ethnicity of the traders".
- Pottery is not the commodity, p. 54: "As distinct from pottery, natural products such as spices and aromatics were the driving force of Indian Ocean commerce." p. 18: "Of course, pottery alone is not sufficient to achieve a balanced picture".
- Excavation effort as a bias, p. 71: "The more extensive list from Berenike would seem to result not from real differences but from the greater amount of excavation and publication to date."
- The crew and on-board use question, p. 75: "Were these wares generally used on-board ship by the crew, or was their primary function as containers for more important items".

## 5. Other observations a historian could build on

1. **The Kerala–Red Sea link rests on a forming technique, not on petrography, and the ethnographic parallel points to North Kerala, not the Periyar.** Tomber's "direct link between Kerala and the Red Sea" (p. 47) comes from internal bamboo wiping on Wheeler Type 24 *handi*. She matched it to Pattanam sherds seen in late 2003 and to an ethnographic technique "today known, only to the potters of North Kerala" (Saraswati & Behura 1966). Pattanam is in central Kerala. The match therefore establishes a Keralan technological tradition, not a Pattanam fabric. In my range she gives no petrographic match between the Egyptian sherds and Pattanam, and she notes that the Type 24s in Egypt come from "numerous production centres". The question it opens: did Malabar ports other than Pattanam (the northern candidates for Tyndis and Nelkynda) ship the same cooking pots? Answering it needs fabric comparison, which Tomber says depends on unpublished manufacturing data (in ch. 6, outside my range, she notes the technique is hard to trace "since manufacturing techniques are not published").

2. **The Adulis case is a direct methodological analogue for Muziris's "20 stadia".** Tomber reports PME 4: "The Periplus describes Adulis as being situated 20 stadia from the coast with the actual port facilities found on Oriene Island within a bay, furthermore stating that the port had previously been located at the furthest mainland point of the bay, at Didoros Island" (p. 89). The visible ruins at Adulis are Late Roman (5th–7th c.). The Periplus-period port was found only by survey (Peacock & Blue 2004–5) at probable island locations with Early Roman pottery, plus 14C dates of 10 BC–AD 360 from one corner of the main site (pp. 89–90). So the same short-distance figure ("20 stadia") was resolved at Adulis by recognising that the coastline and port facilities had shifted. This bears directly on the Pattanam shoreline question in the project.

3. **Two of the project's other ports appear in my range, both resting on thin evidence.**
   - **Leuke Kome**, p. 68–69: "Archaeologically Leuke Kome has been identified as a cluster of seven inter-related settlements in the region of Aynuna in Saudi Arabia, c. 5 km from the coast at the mouth of the Gulf of Aqaba (Ingraham et al. 1981: 76-8). Although known only through survey ... Surface pottery from Aynuna included Nabataean wares (Ingraham et al. 1981: pls 82, 86), but no Egyptian or Eastern ones can be identified from the literature." For a port whose PME 19 role is receiving Arabian small craft on the way to Petra, the absence of Red Sea imports in surface collection is notable, though it is survey evidence only. Tomber also reports the consensus that Ptolemy's western "Leukos Limen" is a confusion with Leuke Kome. **[Note]** The project's candidate table for Leukē Kōmē should record the Ingraham 1981 survey basis.
   - **Rhapta**, p. 98: Chami's Rufiji Delta location is "to date ... the most convincing", but "the finds from this region are difficult to source and date (Sinclair 2007) and at present add little to an overall account." Fig. 15 marks "Rhapta? (Rufiji Delta)" with a question mark.
   - **Barbarikon**, p. 35: only Casson's inference that coinage was used "as currency at Barbarikon (PME 39)". Table 1 lists Barbarikon as the Periplus export port for lapis lazuli and (with others) cotton.

4. **Tomber states the stadion as 185 m without argument.** p. 21: "The accuracy of the Periplus is being increasingly upheld for locating ancient sites and for descriptions of distances, in both stadia (each 185metres) and the travel time between them." The project's calibration gives about 155 m and treats the stadion as sailing time converted by rule. Tomber's line is an example of the conventional value being asserted, and her "and the travel time between them" pairs the two measures without noticing that they may be the same measurement.

5. **The torpedo-jar absence from the Red Sea against its presence in India, Qana, Khor Rori and Hafun separates two networks in a single artefact class.** Tomber also reattributes several published "Roman" amphorae in India to Mesopotamia. An argument could be built that the Indian amphora corpus, and hence any count of "Roman" amphorae used as an index of Roman contact at Pattanam or Arikamedu, needs re-sorting by fabric and lining before it is used. The diagnostic is cheap (a thick bitumen lining).

6. **Independent proxies agree: basalt ballast and pottery.** At Myos Hormos the basalt is 70% Qana and 30% Aden, which "matches remarkably well" the ratio of Hadrami Organic Storage Jars to Aden Whole-mouth Cooking pots (p. 79). This is a clean example of two independent noisy indicators converging on the same source mix, useful as a worked example in the thesis's statistical framework chapter.

7. **Excavation-effort bias is acknowledged.** Tomber explicitly attributes Berenike's longer import list to "the greater amount of excavation and publication to date" (p. 71). At Aila, Indian and Aksumite sources "may be under-represented" because about 40 non-Roman handmade sherds had few diagnostics, and only "a small percentage of the samples" were analysed for plants (p. 81). This parallels the project's finding that absence in Kerala is mostly absence of excavation.

8. **Indian coarse wares stay at the ports.** Except for one sherd at Vectus Hydreuma, Indian coarse wares in Egypt are "restricted to port sites" (p. 75). The mirror question for Malabar: are Roman amphorae in Kerala similarly restricted to the coast, or do they go inland like the coins? The project already finds the coins to be an interior phenomenon. Tomber's Fig. 21 (ch. 5, outside my range) maps amphorae in India.

9. **Customs passes against ship capacity.** The Berenike passes average about 8 amphorae (maximum 48), "not quantities one might associate with either Roman or Indian sea-going vessels with estimated capacities in the thousands" (p. 81). Tomber reports Bagnall et al.'s two explanations (small merchants, or broken-down shipments) without choosing.

10. **Aksum and Adulis are strangely free of Indian pottery.** "The lack of ceramic and botanical remains from India is in sharp contrast to that seen from sites on the Egyptian Red Sea. This is surprising if Aksumites or Indians were active in this leg of the trade." Yet 883 Indo-Pacific beads come from one Aksum tomb (p. 93). If Aksumite middlemen carried Indian goods in the Late Roman period, they did not carry Indian cooking pots. That is the reverse of the Early Roman Egyptian pattern, where cooking pots imply resident Indians.

11. **Harbour siltation decides which port is used.** Myos Hormos was located by "Comparison of ancient descriptions of Myos Hormos with modern satellite images" (Peacock 1993) (p. 58). Its harbour channel was traced by sedimentology in "over 100 2 x 2 m test pits" (p. 60), and "the silting up of the Myos Hormos harbour sometime during the third century reversed this trend" (p. 64). At Berenike the settlement migrated "eastwards and southwards ... in part a response to silting from the wadi and the shore" (p. 62). These are documented analogues for the project's coastline and channel questions: a Periplus port whose ancient waterfront is now inland and buried.

12. **The Periplus is dated by Malichus II.** "PME 19 refers to Malichus, king of the Nabataeans ... this one appears to be Malichus II who ruled between AD 40 and 70 (Casson 1989: 7)" (p. 21). Nahapana's revised reign (AD 48 or 52; Jha & Rajgor 1992; Turner & Cribb 1996) is also noted (pp. 21–22).

13. **Pliny and the Periplus on Malabar pepper.** "Both the writer of the Periplus (PME 56) and Pliny (NH 6.105) knew that some pepper came from Kerala, respectively described as Kottanarike/Cottonara" (p. 55). Over 95% of archaeological pepper in the Roman world comes from the Eastern Desert (p. 55, citing Cappers 2006).

14. **Inconsistencies noticed in the book.**
    - The East African ware is named "Sandy Red Ware" in the Fig. 11 caption and on p. 77, and "Red Sandy Ware" on pp. 53, 54, 78 and in Table 1.
    - The citation "Strabo (Geography 7.17.45)" for Berenike as a landing place (p. 62) is presumably a misprint for 17.1.45, which Tomber cites correctly on p. 63. This is my inference.
    - The OCR text layer misreads several citation numbers ("Tomber 2004b: 3538" for 353; "Suresh 2004: 58" for 98). Anyone quoting from `tomber2008.txt` should check numbers against the page image.
