## 2. Method material

### 2.1 Definition and identification of RW

**Form and decoration [S, with Wheeler cited for the form].**
- p. 126: "the eponymous decoration on the inside of the bottom of this flat dish with its characteristic “beaked” rim. The usually two concentric rows of indentations resemble closely the decoration of the Arretine ware."
- p. 129: "In addition to the fine and grey paste, the particular form of a flat dish with the so-called beaked rim is significant for RW. A simple featureless rim also occurs. Both shapes have been found in Tissamaharama without any chronological difference up to now. The decoration consists of two bands of rouletted or better-called chattered indentations applied on the inside the base of the wheel-thrown dish." (n. 17 cites Wheeler/Ghosh/Krishna 1946: 45–49, Wheeler type 1; n. 18 Wheeler type 2, simple rim; n. 19 "Variety 1 and 2 (Schenk 2001a: 72). – For Arikamedu see Begley 1996: 226"; n. 20 "For the technique of rouletting/chattering see Begley 1986.")
- Technique: she prefers "chattered" to "rouletted" (p. 129) but does not describe the tool mechanics herself; she refers to Begley 1986 (n. 20).
- Tool variation [S]: p. 135: "The only actual and indeed important variation to be seen is the evidence of different tools for making the decoration which could hopefully reveal different workshops (Fig. 1 a)."
- What is *not* RW [I]: decoration on both sides of the base and wavy-line rouletting. p. 129: "Decoration on both sides as well as wavy lines is unknown for “common” RW." n. 14: "Only two sites seem to yield this variety bearing wavy lines: Manigramam (Soundara Rajan/Raman 1994: pl. XIX: B); Wari-Bateshwar (Haque/Rahman/Ahsan 2001: 17, pl. 1: 10) there also called NBP."

**Fabric (paste) as the defining criterion [S].**
- p. 129: "Up to 2005 some 650 fragments of RW alone have occurred at the sites investigated in Tissamaharama. There, RW is categorised to “Fine Grey Pottery”, a group of different vessel forms that all have in common a very dense and extremely fine grey clay (Munsell 2YR N6). This high quality paste is eye-catching and completely different among contemporaneous southern Indian and Sri Lankan pottery assemblages and should be regarded as determinable for the classification (Fig. 2 k)."
- p. 127: bases "commonly is blackened with a grey core similar to NBP (Northern Black Polished Ware), whereas rim and wall sherds display a BRW-firing technique (Fig. 1 a, b). Yet their sections also reveal the grey-fired paste at least visible on the blackened interior zone (Fig. 2 k)." (n. 10: "Sarkar 1966: 63: “core is invariably grey”.")
- p. 135: "To date more than 600 pieces of RW have been registered from Tissamaharama and all are identical in terms of paste. No chronological differences are visible among worn, dull or polished samples or on thin or thick sherds. The only deviation is either a rather blackish or greyish colour on the interior or a mottled appearance instead of plain red on the outside of the sherd."
- Extended to Arikamedu by visual inspection [S]: p. 135: "This homogeneity also applies to RW sherds from Arikamedu as observed during a visit to the site. It also seems valid for all pieces published in a photo for southern India, Sri Lanka and abroad that are here acknowledged as RW." No date of the visit, no sample counts.
- The "Fine Grey Pottery" group (p. 129): RW, NBP, Wheeler type 10 and type 18 cups, "a plain grey ware with streak-polished surface and a flat, rather coarse dish with paddle-stamped bottom" (Fig. 2 a–k).

**Slip and colour [S].**
- p. 132: RW slip is hard to see; of 3rd/2nd c. BC BRW: "the slip was coalesced with the surface and hardly can be recognized, similar to RW."
- p. 135: worn sherds: "Some are worn to such an extent that only tiny speckles of the slip remain; sometimes even this completely gone."
- Sections: p. 131: "sections of RW fragments are almost entirely grey with a rather thin band of red towards the outside at the same areas (Fig. 2 k, l). Common to both is a sharp borderline on top of the rim dividing the blackish firing on the inside and the oxidised outer part. This feature differs from common BRW vessels with where the reduced kiln atmosphere usually has reached over the rim to the outside." (pp. 131–132)

**Firing [S, I].**
- pp. 130–131: "one can easily see that the potters of RW as well as of Wheeler types 10 and 18 intended to use the BRW firing technique, one which is typical for the local pottery tradition of peninsular India and Sri Lanka during the second half of the last millennium B.C. Rather often this seems to have failed. The potters somehow were not very familiar with this technique and therefore most sherds of RW yield a mottled appearance despite the otherwise high quality (Fig. 1 c). Some fragments even bring to mind Sigillata wares when “misfired” to an almost entire red (Fig. 2 b)."
- p. 130: "There seems to be no chronological difference regarding the thickness or the gloss."
- Contrast: "NBP, Plain grey ware or paddle-stamped dishes of the “Fine Grey Pottery”, on the other hand, have been well-fired, either plain grey or with a black glossy slip on the outside." (p. 131)

**"Fine" versus "coarse" RW is not import versus imitation [I].**
- p. 127: "Differing qualities, as evidenced at Tissamaharama (see below), probably led to the misunderstanding of an alleged local production in opposite to so-called “true Roman” RW, probably represented only by black bases of high quality. Such misconceptions persist."
- p. 141 (Summary): "RW as well as Wheeler types 10 and 18 can have a mottled or dull surface thus suggesting a “coarser” fabric."

**Misidentifications in published reports [S, from her reading of others' publications].**
- Banavasi (p. 127): "The recent excavation report of Banavasi describes even decorated bases of RW as “imitation Rouletted Ware”." Quoting Narasimha Murthy et al. 1997: 120: "it is a well known fact that during the early historic period almost all the cultural centres of South India, wherever Roman contact was in existence, produced imitation of the Roman rouletted ware".
- Nevasa (p. 127): "the excavation report for Nevasa lists only one base with rouletted decoration under “RW”. However, at least one of the two rim sherds listed for NBP should be categorised as RW as well because the published illustration shows the typical “beaked” rim." (n. 13: Sankalia/Deo/Ansari/Ehrhardt 1960: 278–79, fig. 140: T110, T111.)
- Brahmagiri (p. 129): "the one rim sherd of Brahmagiri defined as RW could well be a local imitation of BRW. It has been compared by Begley with those from Arikamedu and she found the latter “quite different” (1988: 428)."
- Mahasthan (p. 129): RW-decorated pieces labelled NBP by Salles/Boussac/Breuil 2002: 555, fig. 11, with indentations on both surfaces.
- Beikthano, Burma (p. 132): "The sherds referred to, however, are totally different from RW in decoration and especially in terms of shape".
- Bukit Tengku Lembu, Malaysia (p. 133): repeatedly cited as an RW findspot but the source (Sieveking 1962) describes a black cup with "“…a close resemblance to the highly characteristic Greek black glazed wares of the late 4th century B.C.”" and "No further reference to actual finds of RW at this site appears in her article."
- Quantities are small everywhere: p. 127: "In India alone more than 100 sites supposedly yield RW (Fig. 3 and appendix), but unfortunately those sherds are often only casually mentioned ... To judge from the published reports, the amount of sherds found on these sites is rather small (see also Ray 1994: 60–61)."
- Her demand (p. 129): "a secured definition of RW following a comprehensive study of this pottery from all known sites is a precondition for further historical interpretations based on this pottery." (n. 15 quotes Begley 1992: 181: "the Indian RW must be treated on a site-by-site basis and the sites should be dated independently".)

### 2.2 Local imitation versus import

**The BRW imitation at Tissamaharama [S].**
- p. 131: "RW-Imitations from Tissamaharama always show the potters’ experience with BRW manufacture (Fig. 2 l). Such dishes with beaked rim are classified among Form G of the local pottery typology of Tissamaharama. Form G, a carinated dish belongs to the basic vessel types of BRW (Schenk 2001a). The rim of Form G is usually featureless and simple. Therefore the appearance of that peculiar novelty as this characteristic beaked rim on form G was striking."
- Undecorated: p. 131: "this imitation strictly lacks the rouletted decoration"; p. 141: "These imitations are never decorated."
- Indistinguishable when whole: p. 132: "The ancient customer could not readily distinguish well-fired examples of RW and imitation BRW when still complete, aside from the missing decoration on the latter (Fig. 2 k, l)."
- Date: p. 132: "This BRW-imitation of RW belongs to the final development status of BRW in phase c2 (1st century B.C.) at Tissamaharama. A quite thick shiny slip is a common feature for this late stage of BRW. Additionally, the reddish colour is more distinctive than can be seen on the preceding BRW of the 3rd/2nd century B.C. ... On later specimens of BRW the slip often flakes off."
- Second-generation imitation in "Coarse Red Ware" [S]: p. 138: at Godavaya "some fragments of RW-imitation are recorded. They are not manufactured in BRW-technique but in plain “Coarse Red Ware”. ... Such specimens of imitated RW in “Coarse Red Ware” also occur in Tissamaharama, but not before phase d2."

**Arikamedu equivalents [R: Wheeler/Ghosh/Krishna 1946; I: Schenk's equation].**
- p. 131: "At Arikamedu, this dish with a beaked rim can be found among the local wares as well (Wheeler/Ghosh/Krishna 1946: 55 type 3 and 3e)." n. 21: "Wheeler type 3 is “like the majority of the local types of inferior and coarser fabric; . . . invariably devoid of rouletted decoration”." n. 43 (p. 138): "Wheeler type 3e “exclusively in red ware” and listed among local wares of “inferior and coarser fabric”."
- Appendix no. 96 (p. 144) sets out how Wheeler sorted the Arikamedu material: Wheeler type 1 "listed as imported"; Wheeler type 2 "listed as local" "but “identical in fabric” to Wheeler type 1"; Wheeler type 3c, d, f "of “invariably grey wares” and rim form like Wheeler type 1"; Wheeler type 3 "listed as local and of “inferior and coarser fabric” ... (= BRW-Imitation?)"; type 3e "“exclusively in red ware” = Imitation?". The question marks are Schenk's.

**Satanikota as supposed evidence of local manufacture [R: IAR 1978–79; Ghosh 1986; I: her rebuttal].**
- p. 127: "According to a laboratory analysis of two ceramic samples from this site, BRW and RW from Satanikota were “exactly similar in mineralogy and structure” (IAR 1978–79: 35)."
- p. 129: "As only two pottery samples have been analysed from Satanikota, it may well be possible that unfortunately both belonged to local BRW. Such local imitation of RW dishes definitely do occur at Tissamaharama and Arikamedu (see below). Therefore Satanikota cannot disprove with certainty the various material analyses of RW made in recent years always showing a uniformity of paste combined with an obvious difference to BRW."

Her methodological conclusions on provenance [I]:
- p. 134: "Besides Gogte’s study, all other material analyses are based solely on samples from peninsular India and Sri Lanka, despite the results that local clays do not match with RW and its relatives from a “single geological source”. Unfortunately, the scientists never considered NBP despite its easily visible closeness to RW and the other members of its pottery group. Its North Indian origin would have given a clue to the whereabouts of the “single geological source”."
- p. 135: "Astonishingly, XRD-analysis used by Gogte showed a similarity of RW and local clay. This indicates that the outcome of laboratory analyses depends on the quality of samples which are submitted and needs the suitable preparatory studies to verify the contemporaneousness of the samples."

### 2.4 How RW is dated

**Principle [S, I].** p. 125: "At Tissamaharama, the evaluation of pottery started from the principle that changes and improvements are always to be expected especially among day-to-day pottery. ... such novelties serve as key types and their first emergence within find assemblages in interrelation between their stratigraphical positions is significant in order to define phases." p. 136: "the evaluation of the day-to-day pottery, representing the main bulk of the pottery assemblage, with regard to the stratigraphy provides the basis for the pottery chronology. Radiocarbon dating substantiate the chronology. RW as well as other finds of comparatively rare occurrence are not used as evidence for dating, their positions are solely defined by the context in where they have been found (Schenk 2001a). Relevant and secured is only the date of their first appearance."

- Radiocarbon at Tissamaharama: only the sentence above. No dates, lab numbers or calibrations are given in this article; they are in Schenk 2001a / Weisshaar/Schenk/Wijeyapala 2001 (not stated which).
- Radiocarbon elsewhere [R]: Anuradhapura: "RW is said to have been found even as early as 400–300 B.C. However, this dating rests on the first emergence of RW in period I with a 14C-reference ranging from 380 to 190 B.C. (at latest Ford/Coningham 2005: 393–394)." (p. 136). Tra Kieu, Vietnam: "found in layer 6 at Tra Kieu, Vietnam, dated by 14C to cal. 380-0 B.C.: Glover/Yamagata 1994: 157; Glover 2005: 17, fig. 3a" (n. 24, p. 132). (The bibliography lists Glover/Yamagata 1995, not 1994.)
- Vaddamanu [R]: Wheeler type 10 "with a first appearance in period I (300 B.C. – 100 B.C.)" (n. 26, citing Sastri/Kasturibai/Veerender 1992: 109).
- Mahasthan [R]: "the pottery production of Mahasthan would have a dating-range from the early 3rd century B.C. to the late 1st century B.C. (Salles/Boussac/Breuil 2002: 542), as proposed here as well for RW." (p. 129)
- Red Sea [R: Tomber 2002]: "Berenike and Myos Hormos: Tomber 2002 within dated contexts of late-Augustan as the earliest." (n. 22, p. 132)

**Tissamaharama phase scheme as used here.**
- Phase b: 3rd c. BC (p. 137).
- Phase c1: date not stated in this article.
- Phase c2: 1st c. BC (p. 132).
- Phase d1: 1st c. AD; phase d2: 2nd c. AD (n. 6, p. 125: "phase d is now divided into the phases d1 (1st century A.D.) and d2 (2nd century A.D.)").
- Earliest settlement: "about the beginning of the 4th century B.C." (p. 137).
- n. 6: "An update of the pottery chronology of Tissamaharama is in preparation."

**End date argument [S, I].** Three strands, pp. 137–138:
1. Imitations appear in phase c2 alongside the latest BRW: "From phase d1 onwards strikingly few BRW sherds occur, mostly very worn just like the RW ones. In combination with the sudden emergence of RW imitations, this observation at Tissamaharama provides clear hints regarding the duration of production of both BRW and imitation RW." (p. 138)
2. Godavaya as a terminus: "Godavaya, situated at about 20 km south of Tissamaharama directly at the coast (Roth/Kessler/Recker/Wijeyapala 2001). Godavaya is reputedly a harbour. The study of its pottery yielded no evidence for settlement activities prior to phase d1 (Schenk 2001b). Beside a few worn pieces of BRW, no trace of RW or other kinds of “Fine Grey Pottery” have occurred." (p. 138)
3. Conclusion: "the transition of BRW to the usage of entirely red-fired pottery (“Coarse Red Ware”) and from RW to the occurrence of an imitation apparently took place within the 1st century B.C. This must be verified independently at other sites combined with a study of their pottery sequences." (p. 138)

Note that strand 2 dates Godavaya's founding by its pottery, whose sequence is the same Tissamaharama sequence. The terminus is therefore not independent of the chronology it is used to confirm, though Schenk calls such sites "independently dated on a site-by-site basis" (p. 138).

**Start date [R: Begley; S: confirmation].** p. 136: "Begley redated the appearance of RW from the 1st cent. A.D. back to the 3rd/2nd century B.C. (Begley 1992: 176 and 193 n. 58; Begley 1996: 12). The excavations at Tissamaharama confirm this earlier dating (Schenk 2001a)." First of the Fine Grey group to arrive: "vessels of “Plain grey ware” proved to be the first out of this group to reach Tissamaharama (Fig. 2 h)" (p. 136; n. 41: "Also at Anuradhapura: Ford/Coningham 2005: 394").

Summary statement (p. 141): "The manufacture of RW is restricted from the 3rd century to the 1st century B.C. at the latest. Imitations in local BRW emerge in the 1st century B.C. as evidenced in many contexts at Tissamaharama."

### 2.5 Residuality and curation

- p. 123: "finds in later contexts are taken to be residual heirlooms, which occur inevitably on sites with long settlement sequences" (n. 4: Schenk 2001a: 63; Salles/Boussac/Breuil 2002: 540; "See also at Arikamedu: Begley 1996: 30").
- Tissa 1 [S]: p. 137: "194 fragments of RW are known from Tissa 1. Thirty of them were found in contexts from phase b (3rd cent. B.C.) to phase d1 (1st cent. A.D.). The remaining sherds from the fillings of the later pits are in a worn condition and presumedly were dug up anciently from the earlier periods."
- Tissa 3 [S]: p. 138: "Largely undisturbed in-situ walking levels, acting as stratigraphic barriers, have been determined for all settlement periods excavated up to now. Therefore, as opposed to Tissa 1, heldover pottery from lower strata is restricted in upper levels and that which does persist is in a badly worn state."
- Reuse as tools [S]: p. 138: "these sherds often display abraded sections, obviously having been used as tools for rubbing (Fig. 2 f). This practice is confined to fragments with the very dense paste and of rather hard firing distinctive to “Fine Grey Pottery”, but moreover especially to pieces of foreign amphorae."
- Curation hypothesis [I]: p. 137: "This proposed high esteem for “Fine Grey Pottery” may owe to their distant origin. Therefore one may expect a careful handling of such vessels. They are most probably not used as day-to-day crockery ... Hence sherds in strata of phase c2 may belong to vessels produced and imported to Tissamaharama in earlier phases. One can imagine that it usually needs a few generations to change the attitude towards goods of some value." Used to explain "such a sudden increase of RW-finds at least in phase c2 compared to earlier phases."
- Riveted dish [S]: p. 137: "The already mentioned riveted vessel (Fig. 1 c), found in a structure of the early 1st century A.D. also points to a longer usage of RW dishes postdating their origin." n. 42 lists other riveted RW/NBP and notes the counter-view: "Salles/Boussac/Breuil 2002: 541 and n. 36 (Mahasthan): rejecting that such a repair could be a hint for high esteem."
- Consequence (p. 140): "The mere existence of a few, often worn sherds from a distant origin is at best an imponderable support for dating." and "due to the prolongued use of these sherds, as here supposed, they cannot be used for ascertained dating purposes on a long-lasting site. The emergence of local imitations in the 1st century B.C., on the other hand, can serve as key type for dating."
- Late-dating claims rejected: p. 123: "A continuity for it as late as the 4th century A.D. has often been suggested (Nagaswamy 1995: 78)."

### 2.6 Wheeler, Arretine ware and Arikamedu

What she says about Wheeler's dating is brief. She does not discuss the Arretine stamps, potters' names or their dates.
- p. 125: "This site became one of the most renowned sites of the early historical period in India due to the surprising discovery of Italic Arretine Sigillata, still unique in South Asia. Especially the finds of the well-known Sigillata seemed to provide an anchoring point to date the site’s stratigraphy. Thus, Wheeler considered Arikamedu to be one of the Indo-Roman trading centres described in the “Periplus Mare Erythraei” and as a single-period site which flourished largely within the first two centuries of the Christian era."
- p. 126: "The usually two concentric rows of indentations resemble closely the decoration of the Arretine ware. In addition, RW is made of an excellent quality of paste ... Therefore, most believed RW to be a Roman import. Samples of so-called coarser quality with a surface less glossy, or rather roughly made rouletting were taken to be local copies."
- Begley's reassessment [R]: p. 126: "Begley realized that the site “was exploited by generations of occupants”. It was reoccupied, intermittently, from the third century B.C. up to modern times (Begley 1996: 8; 30)." Vessel types used as Red Sea–India trade evidence were redated: "Begley determined especially those vessels to be medieval (1996: 33; 32, fig. 1: 19; 128, fig. 4: 13–14)." n. 8 adds: "at Tissamaharama also similar rim forms are not found before 9th century A.D.: Schenk 2001a: 124."
- Amphorae [R: Will]: p. 126: "A review of those vessels excavated at Arikamedu, however, points to a rather wide range of datings starting at the latest in the late 2nd century B.C. (Will 1992: 151; Will 1996: 317 f.; Begley 1996: 22 ff.). Therefore, finds of amphorae fragments alone do not authenticate the Roman trade of the Periplus era automatically."

## 4. Arguments

### 4.1 Redating RW [I, building on Begley]
- p. 125: "A dating for the manufacture and distribution of RW in the 3rd/2nd century B.C. ending at the latest in the 1st century B.C. would also resolve sore dating questions which are exacerbated by the absence of RW at some sites with supposedly evidenced “Roman” contact (Soundara Rajan/Raman 1994: 44; Suresh 2004: 97). Following this line of thought, such sites may not yet have existed in the 1st century B.C."
- p. 140: "The occurrence of such RW and “Fine Grey Ware” on the whole only indicates that the respective site has already existed within the last three centuries B.C."
- p. 141: "Sites lacking RW and derivatives may have been founded after these wares ceased to be produced."
- p. 140: "The review of the dating range of RW alone, as proposed here, shifts the settlement of sites such as Arikamedu back as early as the Asokan period. This changes the distribution maps of the early historical period considerably."
- Self-qualification (p. 125): "Evidence for an exclusively pre-Christian antiquity for RW exists, but it must be taken gingerly."

### 4.2 Critique of Wheeler and the Roman-centred chronology
- Opens with H. P. Ray: "“Old myths die hard”" (p. 123, Ray 1999: 317), and "all attempts being made to keep the linkage with Indo-Roman trade alive".
- p. 123: "to this day many scholars still regard Arikamedu and RW as well as proof of Indo-Roman trade during the early first centuries A.D., as reflected in the Periplus maris erythreae, despite new divergent results ... The uncritical reliance on half a century old publications and/or the non-observance of recent investigations often leads to a perpetuation of obsolete “classic” conceptions."
- p. 139: "This was not possible with the beginning of RW to 1st century Roman times. Thus RW has lost relevance as evidence for Indo-Roman trade."
- p. 141: "RW does not indicate Indo-Roman trade. Arikamedu is a site with a long sequence reaching from the 3rd century B.C. into the medieval period. It is by no means a single-period “Indo-Roman trading post”. Its find assemblages as a whole cannot be used as exemplary for the respective period."
- Named defenders of Wheeler's position: Soundara Rajan/Raman 1994 (n. 3: "in an appendix he very strictly defends Wheeler’s dating of RW and Arikamedu being an Indo-Roman site versus Begley"); Nagaswamy 1995 (4th c. AD continuity); Narasimha Murthy et al. 1997 (Banavasi).
- Amphorae too (p. 126): "finds of amphorae fragments alone do not authenticate the Roman trade of the Periplus era automatically."

### 4.3 Pre-Roman exchange and the Mauryan frame [I]
- p. 126: "Arikamedu should be associated at first with those settlement sites cited for the “Second Urbanization” especially as known in northern India." Contact with the West "at least since Alexander" (citing Karttunen, Ray, Thapar); "Begley published archaeological evidence for “pre-Periplus trade” following the redating of Arikamedu (1992)."
- p. 139: "the distributional pattern of these very distinctive vessels of RW and Wheeler types 10 and 18 is exceptional and obviously reflects Mauryan contacts."
- Asoka and Devanampiya Tissa (p. 139): "The relatively large frequency of RW-discoveries reaching as far as Sri Lanka can be explained hypothetically by a special relationship between Asoka and the contemporaneous king Devanampiya Tissa at Anuradhapura".
- Gift/Buddhist hypothesis (p. 139): "Pots of “Fine Grey Pottery” may have been distributed perhaps as presents or special trade good following the routes of Mauryan trade and as such also reaching places at Southeast Asia and the eastern trading centres of the Hellenistic world at the Red Sea region. This may be connected with the spread of Buddhism and may have been used as a present for conversion." Then: "Of course it remains highly speculative".
- Continuity after the Mauryas: "regarding a further supply beyond the decline of the Mauryan Empire, already established trade connections surely continued (Ray 1996: 354)." (p. 139)
- Counter-argument she acknowledges (p. 139): "At some sites, RW seems in evidence slightly later than NBP, which may be cited as a counter-argument." She answers it with curation.

### 4.4 Where it was made, and for whom [I]
- Not in south India or Sri Lanka: p. 140: "In the light of different material analyses, one indeed must accept that “Fine Grey Pottery” doubtless was not produced in southern India and Sri Lanka."
- Northern plains: p. 140: "Such vessels may have been produced either at different sites or at a single kiln site in the plains of northern India, be it Bengal (e. g. Chandraketugharh, Mahasthan) or the middle Ganga plains (e. g. Rajghat, Ayodhya) or both."
- Custom-made for southern markets: p. 139: "why should those vessels, this extraordinary kind of dish, not be a custom-made product, initially aimed for South Indian and Sri Lankan markets? ... There, the population were accustomed to vessels made of BRW-firing technique. This production method was shared in order to fabricate vessels of RW and Wheeler types 10 and 18, used by potters usually producing NBP." p. 141: "a combination of using clay similar to NBP and a firing technique especially popular in South India and Sri Lanka at that time."
- Absence in the north explained: p. 140: "the lack of RW in Northern India (besides Ayodhya and Rajghat) does not disprove its manufacture in this area."
- Imitation as response to cessation: p. 140: "The obvious need for a local substitute (imitation) of RW after a cessation of delivery or production would confirm this assessment."

### 4.5 Indigenous agency
She does not use the phrase. The relevant content: the ware is South Asian, not Roman; its producers adapted a southern firing technique to a southern market; local BRW potters at Tissamaharama and Arikamedu copied its rim form; the distribution follows Mauryan routes and diplomacy. The agents she names are northern Indian potters, the Mauryan state, Buddhist institutions (via Ray 1987), and the Sri Lankan king Devanampiya Tissa. Southern Indian or Sri Lankan seafarers are not discussed as carriers.

### 4.6 Regional networks
- Bay of Bengal: p. 132: "a concentration along the eastern coast of peninsular India and Bangladesh and includes sites alongside rivers such as Krishna, Godavari and Kaveri used as natural transportation routes (Fig. 3; Ray 1996: 352, fig. 1)."
- Sri Lanka: "Four such sites in Sri Lanka are published (Fig. 3 and appendix), easily to be reached from the southern tip of India (Begley 1996: fig. 1.1)." (p. 132). The appendix actually lists six Sri Lankan sites (119–124). The "four" appears to be retained from an earlier version (my inference; not explained).
- West coast: overland to Pattanam via Kodumanal and the Kaveri (p. 132). She does not propose a sea route round the Cape.
- Red Sea and Arabia: "Some fragments have been reported from the Red Sea coast of Egypt, Oman and Yemen" (p. 132); reached as "the eastern trading centres of the Hellenistic world" (p. 139).
- "this extraordinary distribution from the Red Sea in the West to Vietnam in the East reflects not only Mauryan expansion indicated by Asoka’s inscriptions, but also the range of the historically known diplomatic activities of the Mauryas." (p. 139)

### 4.7 Named disagreements and alignments
- **Wheeler** (Wheeler/Ghosh/Krishna 1946): single-period Indo-Roman site, RW as Roman import. Rejected.
- **Begley**: followed throughout (redating to 3rd/2nd c. BC; Arikamedu multi-period; site-by-site dating; Brahmagiri sherd "quite different"). No disagreement stated.
- **H. P. Ray**: framing quote; Ray 1994: 60–61 on small quantities; Ray 1987 on Buddhist monasteries and economic motives; Ray 1996 on Bay of Bengal. Aligned.
- **Gogte**: credited as the only analyst to sample a northern clay, and his 1997/2001 results are taken to "come close to the probable origin" (p. 135), but "it is indeed not imperative to connect the source of RW to Chandraketurgarh" (p. 135) and recent clays are doubtful evidence (p. 136).
- **Ardika et al. 1993; Krishnan/Coningham 1997**: single source accepted, south Indian (Arikamedu) location rejected.
- **Das/Panja/Mukhopadhyay/Chakrabarti 2002**: criticised for classification by surface quality, ignoring paste, and inconsistent terminology.
- **Suresh 2004**: cited for the data complaint (p. 123) and as the latest to claim Satanikota local manufacture (p. 127) and "Arikamedu was a major manufacturing centre" (n. 12). Disagreed with.
- **Nagaswamy 1995; Nath 1995a; Soundara Rajan/Raman 1994; Narasimha Murthy et al. 1997**: late dating or "imitation Roman" terminology. Disagreed with.
- **Krishna Mohan Reddy 2001**: clay "“completely different from the early South Indian pottery”" leading him back to a Red Sea origin (n. 12). Noted without direct rebuttal.
- **Salles/Boussac/Breuil 2002**: RW-type sherds called NBP at Mahasthan; their rejection of rivets as sign of esteem (n. 42). Partly disagreed with.
- **Deo/Joshi 1972; Subrahmanyam 1964**: early proponents of local origin/early dates (p. 126).
- **Glover; Smith 1999**: Smith quoted in support: "the possibility of it being traded much later than the date of manufacture, again cautions against seeing these items as definite proof of large-scale exchange in the early centuries C.E." (n. 45).

## 6. Observations a historian could build on

These are observations about the article and its evidence, not claims about where any port was.

1. **The Pattanam RW entered Schenk's corpus unseen.** Her definition rests on paste, which she checked by eye at Tissamaharama and Arikamedu and from photographs elsewhere (p. 135). Pattanam (no. 118) is included on a single citation (Shajan et al. 2004: 317) with no count, context or description. Whether the Pattanam sherds are RW in her sense, or BRW/Coarse Red Ware imitations of the kind she found at Tissamaharama, Arikamedu, Khor Rori and Satanikota, is an open question the article does not answer. Since imitations persist into phase d2 (2nd c. AD), the distinction changes the date bracket by up to three centuries.

2. **Her dating rule turns RW into a presence/absence variable with an asymmetric meaning.** Presence implies the site "has already existed within the last three centuries B.C." (p. 140); absence "may" mean founding afterwards (p. 141), but she also says quantities everywhere are small (p. 127), and at Tissa 3 phase b and c1 levels "only few sherds have been found" (p. 137). A site occupied in the 2nd c. BC could easily yield none in a small excavation. This is the same informative-missingness problem as the thesis's excavation-coverage issue, and Schenk's own numbers show it: at Tissa 1 only 30 of 194 RW sherds (15 per cent) were in primary phase b–d1 contexts.

3. **Her end-date and the Red Sea evidence pull against each other, and she does not reconcile them.** She puts manufacture ending "in the 1st century B.C. at the latest" (p. 141), yet reports (n. 22) that RW at Berenike and Myos Hormos is in contexts "of late-Augustan as the earliest". Her curation hypothesis could cover this, but the Red Sea finds are at the *receiving* end of a sea route, where a heirloom explanation is less natural than at a home site. Late-Augustan is also exactly the horizon of the Periplus world. This tension is a usable point for any argument about whether RW in a Malabar port context can be Periplus-era.

4. **Godavaya, the one harbour in her argument, is dated by the sequence it is used to confirm.** It has no RW and is dated to d1 onwards by its pottery (Schenk 2001b), and that absence is then used as an independent check on RW's end date (p. 138). A historian can point out that the harbour site, the class of site most relevant to the Periplus, is the weakest link in the chronology. Godavaya also offers a direct analogue for a Malabar harbour founded after RW stopped arriving.

5. **The count of Tissamaharama RW is internally inconsistent.** "Some 650" (p. 129) and "more than 600" (p. 135) sit alongside area totals of 194 + 7 + 234 (to 2004) + about 225 (2005) = about 660. About 150 imitations in 2005 alone, against about 225 RW, means imitations ran at roughly two for every three genuine pieces in that season, a ratio worth comparing with any Pattanam assemblage once published.

6. **Fig. 1a shows several distinct rouletting tools on one "reconstructed" dish.** The sherds are from different loci, and the impressions vary from fine triangular nicks to long comb-like gouges. Schenk suggests tool variation could reveal workshops (p. 135). A tool-mark typology is a non-destructive test that could be applied to Pattanam sherds and would bear on whether Malabar received RW from the same source as the east coast and Sri Lanka.

7. **The Kodumanal–Kaveri route is her only explanation of how RW reached Malabar** (p. 132), an overland link resting on Ray 1994 and Rajan 1998. No sea route round Cape Comorin or via Sri Lanka is considered, even though she maps six Sri Lankan sites and argues for a special Asokan–Sri Lankan link. For the thesis, this is an untested route assumption, and it connects with the finding that Roman coins in Kerala concentrate on the Palghat corridor.

8. **Fig. 3 numbering: no. 84 is missing and no. 85 is used twice**, so the "124" sites are 123. Thirty Indian entries rest only on "Suresh 2004: 91", and 41 are plotted only to district. Anyone reusing the map as a distribution should count it as about 123 reported findspots of very uneven reliability, not 124 confirmed ones.

9. **The Khor Rori RW date (3rd–1st c. BC) rests on a personal communication**, and the one published Khor Rori piece Schenk considers a probable BRW imitation because of visible inclusions (n. 23). That is a checkable claim against later Khor Rori publications.

10. **Fig. 2b, an RW sherd fired red with an incised graffito, is not discussed.** Graffiti on RW (cf. inscribed RW at Alagankulam, appendix no. 111, and Subrahmanyam's inscribed sherds at Salihundam dated to the 2nd c. BC, p. 126) would give a script-based date check independent of stratigraphy. The red-fired "Sigillata-like" appearance Schenk notes (p. 131; n. 35) also raises the question whether sherds reported elsewhere as Mediterranean red-slipped wares (Gogte's "African Red Ware" from Alagankulam; Sedov's "fine red slipped pottery" at Qana) are misfired RW.
