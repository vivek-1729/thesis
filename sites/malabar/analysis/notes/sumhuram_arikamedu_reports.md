# Sumhuram (Khor Rori) and Arikamedu: object-level reading of the supplementary reports

Reader's notes, 10 November 2026. Sources are the seven supplementary PDFs in
`sites/malabar/readings/supplementary/`, read from the page-marked text in
`analysis/text/supp_*.txt` and checked against renders in
`analysis/renders/sumhuram/`. "PDF n" is the page of the PDF file; "p." is the printed
page. Quotations are verbatim from the text layer (OCR ligature losses such as "ig."
for "fig." are left as they are). Anything not stated in the sources is marked "not
stated". Where I describe what a picture shows, that is my own observation and is
labelled as such.

Pavan and Schenk 2012 (AAE 23: 191-202) is being covered by another reader and is not
summarised here. Where these reports touch it, that is noted in §4.

## 2. Site phasing and dating as stated

### 2.1 Sumhuram

**Foundation**

- Pavan 2011: "The city's foundation is to be backdated by at least four centuries
  earlier than the heyday of Roman trade, in the 3rd century BC." (p. 101, PDF 51)
- Avanzini 2014: "Nous savons aujourd'hui que Sumhuram a été fondée à la fin du IIIe
  siècle av. J.-C., et qu'elle n'a pas été définitivement abandonnée avant le Ve siècle
  de notre ère environ." (pp. 486-487, PDF 3-4)
- Avanzini 2014 gives the basis in one sentence: "Sur la base de la stratigraphie, du
  matériel (monnaies, poterie), des datations au C14, les dates pour le début et la fin
  du port divergent de celles qui ont été avancées il y a quelques années par la AFSM"
  (p. 486, PDF 3, citing Avanzini and Sedov 2005, PSAS 35).
- **No radiocarbon determination, laboratory number or calibrated range appears in any
  of these documents.** The C14 basis is asserted, not shown.
- The earlier American dating, "Pour la mission américaine, le port a été actif entre le
  Ier et le IIIe siècle ap. J.-C.", rested on the gate inscriptions of Iliaz Yaluṭ,
  identified with Eleazos of the Periplus (p. 485, PDF 2).

**Avanzini's own caveat on the oldest layers (2014). This is important for object-level work.**

> "Or, les couches les plus anciennes contiennent du matériel chronologiquement
> incompatible : poterie indienne d'av. J.-C. et amphores du Ier siècle av. ou ap. J.-C."
> (p. 498, PDF 15)

Her explanation is a hypothesis: a small unwalled outpost in the 3rd century BC, then
"Entre la fin du Ier siècle av. J.-C. et le début du Ier siècle ap. J.-C., un énorme
travail de reconstruction de la ville aurait été entrepris … à l'époque du roi Yashhuril
… et du roi Eleazos. Ils pourraient donc justifier la confusion de matériaux dans les
couches plus anciennes." (pp. 498-499, PDF 15-16). The rebuilding would include the
raising of the well and the walls of the building around it. Avanzini and Pavan 2011
independently date the well lining and the raising of the rim by 3 m to "the vast
rebuilding in the course of the 1st cent. AD" (p. 50, PDF 26).

**Buffa 2019, as summarised by Vinogradov 2021 (pp. 448-450, PDF 4-6; my translation)**

- **Area A (three houses, about 350 m²):** five building phases grouped into three
  periods (Buffa p. 247).
  - **KR I** (phases 1-2): Monumental Building 1 (with the well) and other structures.
    Characterised by coarse Hadrami pottery, steatite cooking vessels and "imported
    Roman pottery: fragments of Dressel 2-4 amphorae and terra sigillata". Dated from
    "the middle to end of the 1st c. BC" to "the end of the 1st c. AD".
  - **KR II** (phase 3): major rebuilding. South Arabian cups and jugs, green-glazed
    ware, and "jugs and ceramic round-bottomed cooking pots of Indian production".
    Gulf pottery, green glaze and Indian pottery "including lids/lamps" continue into
    KR III. Dated to the end of the 1st to the beginning of the 3rd c. AD.
  - **KR III** (phases 4-5): minor alterations, then gradual abandonment. Green-glazed
    ware, "some Indian ceramic cooking pots", and South Arabian cooking and storage
    vessels. Dated from the mid to late 3rd century to the end of the 4th or beginning
    of the 5th c. AD. Sporadic 5th-century activity is possible.
  - Overall sequence in Area A: "from the middle of the 1st c. BC to the end of the 4th
    c. AD".
- **Vinogradov's caveat:** the epigraphic foundation date of the 3rd-2nd c. BC
  (Avanzini 2008, p. 637) "cannot yet be 'closed' by mass archaeological material"
  ("этот хронологический разрыв пока никак не удается «закрыть» массовым
  археологическим материалом", p. 448, PDF 4).
- **Area F (temple of Sin):** Buffa associates this area with the first Hadrami settlers.
  - A monumental building, 15 × 14 m with walls 3 m thick, is dated by "fragments of
    golden glazed ware" to "II (III?) c. BC" (Buffa p. 257). The review calls it
    "Monumental Building 2 (MB2)" in one sentence and "Monumental Building 1" in the
    next, so the building number is inconsistent in the review.
  - Room BF9 is dated 2nd c. BC to the end of the 1st c. BC.
  - Vinogradov (who excavated there in 2016-2017) reports that the temple platform cuts
    the BF9 staircase and sits on a destruction layer, so the visible temple is later
    than BF9.
- **Coins, 2004-2016** (Sedov, in Buffa 2019, as summarised by Vinogradov):
  - more than 700 finds: 624 Hadramawt and 68 Himyar
  - "Only two finds are attributed as imports, relating to the coinage of India and of
    Ptolemaic Egypt" (p. 448, PDF 4)
  - the earliest Hadrami issues date from the end of the 4th to the beginning of the
    1st c. BC (Buffa p. 155)
  - the latest Himyarite issues are 3rd-4th c. (Buffa p. 162)

**Inscriptions**

- The gate inscriptions name Eleazos (Iliaz Yaluṭ) and settlers from Shabwa (Tavolieri
  D'Andrea 2011, p. 114, PDF 58).
- Yashhuril, "roi de la deuxième moitié du Ier siècle av. J.-C.", is cited in a gate
  inscription (KR 10) (Avanzini 2014, p. 498, PDF 15).
- KR 11, a bronze plaque found in situ in 2013C on a floor associated with the square
  and the Eleazos inscriptions, is dated to the 1st c. AD by context and paleography
  (pp. 500-501, PDF 18).

**Discrepancy on Indian coins**

- Pavan 2011: "The coin of King Kaniskha I and one of Abhiraka, Satrap of Barygaza, are
  the only Indian coins discovered in whole Arabian peninsula." That is two Indian
  coins (p. 103, PDF 52).
- Vinogradov 2021 reports one Indian import among the 2004-2016 finds.
- These may refer to different excavation spans. The discrepancy is not resolved in the
  sources.

**End of occupation**

- Ayla/Aqaba amphorae, "Recently dated to the 4th century AD by discoveries made in
  Berenike", indicate trade "up to the 4th century when the city began to decline until
  its inal abandonment in the 5th century AD" (Pavan 2011, p. 107, PDF 54).

### 2.2 Arikamedu

These sources are all secondary for Arikamedu dating.

**Pillai (PDF 6-8):** a ten-phase scheme attributed to "the second volume of Arikamedu
ancient Port city" by Begley.

| Phase | Dates | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 300-200 BC | "settlement is identified primarily on the basis of pottery" (Casal). Includes a coarse-ware sherd with early Tamil-Brahmi |
| 2 | 200-100 BC | "marked by the appearance of 'roulette ware' in southern sector … Mediterranean wares or amphora were not associated with this phase" |
| 3 | 100-50 BC | "for the first time Amphora and other items of undoubtedly Mediterranean origins are encountered" |
| 4 | 50 BC-AD 50 | "Sigallata Period … Since Sigallata is the most precisely dated among all the imports in Arikamedu this phase is the most securely dated" |
| 5 | AD 50-200 | Post-sigillata. "In pottery fine ware was not much in demand except roulette wares" |
| 6 | AD 200-600 | |
| 7 | 600-1100 | |
| 8 | 1100-1500 | |
| 9 | 1500-1700 | No evidence |
| 10 | 1700-1800 | Jesuit seminary, 1771-3 to 1783 |

- **Internal inconsistency:** the Phase 5 text reads "Mediterranean amphora made its entry
  into Arikamedu during this period. Amphora related trade virtually came to an end."
  (PDF 7). This contradicts Phase 3 and is probably garbled. Do not quote it.
- Begley's conclusions, as summarised by Pillai: the southern sector was settled
  "perhaps from the 2nd century BC"; trade "seems to have been at its height between
  50BC to 50 AD" (PDF 5).
- Wheeler's view, as reported by Pillai: the site's history was "only 200 years old"
  (PDF 4), and the northern warehouse dates "from AD 25 or slightly later" (PDF 12).

**Walker and Santoso 1977, reporting Wheeler et al. 1946 (p. 231, PDF 4):** "At
Arikamedu, rouletted ware occurs in pre-Arretine (pre-20 A.D.), Arretine, and
post-Arretine levels, through to the end of the site c. 200 A.D." Arretine is dated
"perhaps c. 20-50 A.D." This is the 1946 chronology, before Begley's revisions.

**Pattanam, for comparison** (Selvakumar, Shajan and Tomber 2010, p. 34)

- Period II: "could be dated between the late 1st century BC and the 4th century AD.
  As of now, no foreign ceramic material clearly datable to before the late 1st century
  BC has been found at this site."
- No C-14 date was available for Period I at the time of writing.

## 3. Object catalogue

Each entry records what the source states. "Sufficiency" is my judgement of whether the
object can bear an object-level argument: identification, date, provenance and context
all tied to that individual piece.

### A. Sumhuram: Indian wares

**SUM-1. Rouletted ware: three pieces drawn and photographed (Avanzini 2014, Fig. 6)**

- **Location:** p. 489, PDF 6, bottom figure on the page.
- **Caption:** "FIG. 6. – Céramique indienne (*Rouletted Ware*) trouvée à Sumhuram
  (Italian Mission To Oman, dessins : 1, 2 Sergio Martelli ; 3 Vincenzo Labianca,
  échelle 1:3)."
- **What is shown** (my observation, zoom in `avanzini2014_p06_fig6_zoom.png`):
  - top left: a drawn half-profile of a shallow dish with an incurved, beaked rim
  - bottom left: a second dish profile of the same form, with a photograph of a dark
    sherd overlaid on both the exterior and the interior halves
  - top right: a drawing and a photograph of a small body fragment with bands of
    rouletting
  - bottom right: a photograph of a rim in section
  - scale bar 0-5 cm
  - **the numbers 1-3 named in the caption are not printed in the figure**, so the
    drawing-to-number correspondence is not given
- **Text reference** (p. 487, PDF 4): "Certains types de céramique sud-arabes (fig. 5),
  mais surtout deux bols de type indien (fig. 6), trouvés dans les couches les plus
  anciennes, ont non seulement démontré l'ancienneté de la date de fondation de la
  colonie…". The text says two bowls while the caption credits three drawings.
- **Inventory number:** not stated. **Area, locus or US:** not stated beyond "les couches
  les plus anciennes".
- **Date and basis:** "des poteries qui ont cessé d'être produites en Inde au Ier siècle
  av. J.-C." (p. 487, citing Pavan and Schenk 2012). That is a typological date for the
  ware, not for these pieces.
- **Provenance:** India. Not specified further. No fabric analysis is reported for these
  pieces.
- **Associated finds:** not stated. Avanzini herself says the oldest layers mix BC Indian
  pottery with 1st-century BC/AD amphorae (p. 498).
- **Sufficiency:** **partial.** Form and decoration are documented at scale, but there is
  no context number, the numbering is not printed, and the stratigraphic association is
  compromised by the author's own admission. The full context should be in Pavan and
  Schenk 2012 or the Khor Rori reports.

**SUM-2. Rouletted ware fragment (Pavan 2011, Fig. 3)**

- **Location:** p. 108, PDF 55, left spread, bottom row, the small photograph to the
  right of Fig. 4.
- **Caption:** "Fig. 3 Fragment of Rouletted Ware."
- **What is shown** (my observation, crop `avanzini2011_p55_fig3_rouletted.png`): an
  orange-red sherd photographed on a dark ground. Along the left edge is a thickened
  band, probably the rim. The rest of the surface is covered with closely spaced
  parallel impressed lines. No scale. The colour is notable because the text describes
  rouletted ware as "a variant of Fine Gray pottery"; orange examples of the ware do
  exist (Tomber 2010, p. 43).
- **Text** (p. 102, PDF 52): "The discovery of fragments of true Rouletted Ware (ig. 3),
  a variant of Fine Gray pottery, is to be considered exceptional, as being the only
  evidence of this kind to be found in all the Arabian peninsula. These are actually
  fragments of plates or shallow bowls that have a characteristic beaked rim and a
  typical decoration on the base … (rouletting)."
- **Context:** "in strata that can be dated to when the city was irst inhabited" (p. 102).
  This is said of the ware in general, not of this piece.
- **Inventory, area and US:** not stated. **Date basis:** "went out of production in the
  1st century BC (Schenk 2006)".
- **Whether this is one of the SUM-1 pieces:** not stated.
- **Sufficiency:** **insufficient** on its own. It is an illustration of the class.

**SUM-3. Paddle-impressed jar, rim and wall (Pavan 2011, Fig. 4)**

- **Location:** p. 108, PDF 55, left spread, bottom-left large photograph.
- **Caption:** "Fig. 4 Rim and part of wall of a paddle-impressed jar".
- **What is shown** (my observation, crop `avanzini2011_p55_fig4_paddle.png`): a grey
  sherd with a densely textured surface of small raised or impressed points. Most of the
  frame is background. The rim is not clearly legible at this resolution. No scale.
- **Text** (p. 102, PDF 52): "Sumhuram is the only one where fragments of the hand-made
  'paddle impressed ware' ceramic have been discovered … large in size, made to be used
  for storing or cooking purpose … decorated by means of a procedure of percussion,
  grooving paddles on the outside." Its source is attributed to "southern India and, in
  particular, to the area of Arikamedu which, therefore, may be considered the centre
  where these vessels were manufactured and shipped from". This attribution rests on
  technique, not on analysis.
- **Context, inventory and date:** not stated. The ware is grouped with rouletted ware
  as BC evidence (Avanzini 2014, p. 487).
- **Sufficiency:** **insufficient.**

**SUM-4. Indian cooking ware (Avanzini 2014, Fig. 8.6) and the rice-tempered thin section (Fig. 9)**

- **Location of Fig. 8.6:** p. 492, PDF 9, third row, left.
- **Caption:** "6 : Céramique indienne de cuisine". The full caption of Fig. 8 ends
  "(Italian Mission To Oman, dessins Sergio Martelli)".
- **What is shown** (my observation): a photograph of a large rim fragment of a cooking
  pot with a thick everted or rolled rim and a blackened exterior. No scale.
- **Fig. 9 location:** p. 493, PDF 10, top.
- **Fig. 9 caption:** "FIG. 9. – Lame mince de poterie indienne avec riz (avec
  l'autorisation de P. Pallecchi)." The micrograph shows elongated dark voids in a
  fine matrix (my observation). No scale bar is visible.
- **Text** (p. 491, PDF 8): "Des analyses archéométriques des échantillons de poterie de
  Sumhuram (fig. 9) confirment la présence de rice-tempered cooking ware du même type
  que celui qui a été trouvé à Bérénice et produit dans la région du Gujarat", citing
  Mariotti Lippi et al. 2011 (JAS 38: 1173-1179) and Tomber, Cartwright and Gupta
  ("JAS 38, 2010" as printed).
- **Whether the Fig. 8.6 pot is the sampled sherd:** not stated.
- **Context and inventory:** not stated. Dated only generally ("À partir du Ier siècle
  ap. J.-C., la variété et la quantité de poterie importée d'Inde augmente").
- **Sufficiency:** **insufficient** at object level, but useful for teaching
  petrography (§5).

**SUM-5. Lid or reused amphora sherd with a Tamil-Brahmi inscription (Pavan 2011, Fig. 5; probably Avanzini 2014, n. 10)**

- **Location:** p. 109, PDF 55, right spread, top row, first photograph.
- **Caption (2011):** "Fig. 5 Fragment of lid with Tamil-Brahmi inscription."
- **What is shown** (my observation, crop `avanzini2011_p55_fig5_tamilbrahmi_lid.png`):
  - a thick, flat, orange-buff sherd mended from at least two pieces
  - several incised signs on the larger piece, among them a cross-like sign and an
    angular sign
  - a rounded relief or depression on the smaller piece
  - no scale
  - I do not attempt a reading of the signs
- **Pavan 2011 text** (p. 102): this figure is cited among Indian vessels of the 1st c.
  AD onwards: "several samples of carinated pots, lids (ig. 5), oil lamps have been
  discovered in all the sites overlooking the Indian Ocean."
- **Avanzini 2014, n. 10** (p. 491, PDF 8), attached to "Sumhuram est le premier site en
  Arabie où l'on a trouvé une inscription en Tamil-Brahmi": "L'inscription est gravée
  sur un tesson d'amphore remployé comme couvercle (SUM06B ; US206,9). Elle a été
  traduite par K. Rajan et publiée par T. S. Subramanian, « Tamil-Brahmi potsherd in
  Oman », The Hindu, 25 October 2012."
- **Identity of the two:** that the 2011 figure and the 2014 note describe the same
  object is my inference. Both are a lid with a Tamil-Brahmi inscription from
  Sumhuram, and 2014 speaks of a single such inscription. Neither source states it.
- **Tension between the two descriptions:** 2011 presents it as an Indian lid. 2014 says
  it is an amphora sherd reused as a lid. The vessel's origin therefore differs between
  the two accounts.
- **Inventory and context:** "SUM06B ; US206,9" (2014). The format suggests the 2006
  season, campaign B, stratigraphic unit 206, find 9, but the format is not explained in
  the source. Area: not stated.
- **Date:** not stated for the object. **Reading of the text:** not given in these
  documents (only "traduite par K. Rajan").
- **Sufficiency:** **the best-contexted Indian-related object in these reports.** It has
  a season and unit, a published inscription, and a photograph. The vessel identity
  (Indian lid or reused amphora) and the date need the primary publication. The source
  given is a newspaper.

### B. Sumhuram: Gulf and Mesopotamian wares

**SUM-6. Glazed jug (Pavan 2011, Fig. 6; the same object appears to be Avanzini 2014, Fig. 8.7)**

- **Location (2011):** p. 109, PDF 55, top row, middle. **Location (2014):** p. 492,
  PDF 9, third row, centre.
- **Captions:** "Fig. 6 Glazed jug probably from Mesopotamia" (2011). "7 : Cruche à
  glaçure" (2014).
- **What is shown** (my observation): a near-complete single-handled jug.
  - The upper body and neck are covered in a green to blue-green glaze.
  - The glaze stops above an unglazed buff lower body and base.
  - The two photographs appear to show the same vessel, given the same profile, glaze
    edge and handle. Neither source says so.
- **Text** (p. 105, PDF 53): "Produced in southern Iraq and south-east Iran, the glazed
  pottery … can be divided into two main groups: that of a golden colour … dated to the
  centuries BC … and that of a green-bluish hue which can be dated to the early
  centuries AD which is the more numerous". The corpus is "numerically the biggest …
  in the whole of south-west Arabia" (Mouton 2009).
- **Date:** by class only (green-blue = early centuries AD). **Context and inventory:**
  not stated.
- **Sufficiency:** **insufficient** for a context argument. It is good for illustrating
  the class, as the only complete vessel in these figures.

## 4. The authors' arguments (interpretation, kept apart from §3)

**Indian presence at Sumhuram**

- Pavan 2011, p. 104: "There is an interesting point of discussion regarding whether
  or not there was an Indian community on the site." She cites Goetz on the bronze
  statuette (Fig. 2, from the American 1953 excavation) as "a private chapel" of an
  Indian merchant, and adds: "The great quantity of imported utilitarian vessels
  discovered here seems to be another evidence of a resident community". The parallel
  is Berenike: rice eaten "by the Indian merchants who resided there" (Cappers 2006).
- **Status:** the argument is an inference from the share of utilitarian wares. No
  quantification is given in these sources.

**Pre-Roman exchange and two routes**

- Pavan 2011, p. 102: rouletted ware establishes "a link among the three ports of
  Arikamedu, Sumhuram and Berenike". Paddle-impressed ware "is not attested in western
  India, in the Gulf or in Kane, but it is mentioned in sizeable amounts in Berenike".
- From the 1st c. AD, "the Indian forms and wares grew up and became predominant those
  from northern India", and routes shift to "India's north-western coast" (p. 103).
- Avanzini 2014, p. 491, citing Pavan and Schenk: "Au cours des siècles qui ont précédé
  l'ère chrétienne … les navires suivaient un chemin allant de Bérénice à Sumhuram, en
  traversant la mer jusqu'au sud de l'Inde, vers le port de Mouziris en Kerala." From
  the Christian era "l'itinéraire vers le sud de l'Inde ait été supplanté par la route
  décrite dans le Périple jusqu'à des destinations du nord de l'Inde."
- **For this thesis:** this names Muziris as the BC terminus. The basis in these
  documents is the distribution of the two wares, not any Muziris-specific find.
- **Distribution map** (Avanzini 2014, Fig. 7, p. 490, PDF 7). My reading of the
  symbols:
  - Rouletted ware: Berenike, Myos Hormos, Sumhuram, Muziris, Arikamedu, Anuradhapura
    and Tissamaharama.
  - Paddle-impressed ware: Sumhuram, Berenike, Muziris, Arikamedu and the Sri Lankan
    sites.
  - The text says the two co-occur only at "Arikamedu et dans quelques autres centres
    urbains du sud de l'Inde et du Sri Lanka, dans Sumhuram, dans le port de Myos Hormos
    et celui de Bérénice" (p. 487).
  - Where the map plots "Muziris" (Pattanam is implied) is not stated in the text.

**Circularity to flag**

- Rouletted ware is dated to before the end of the 1st c. BC by Schenk 2006. Its
  presence then "conirms the 'early' dating of Sumhuram" (Pavan 2011, p. 102).
- Walker and Santoso, following Wheeler et al. 1946, have rouletted ware at Arikamedu
  "through to the end of the site c. 200 A.D." (p. 231). Pillai's Phase 5 (AD 50-200)
  keeps "roulette wares" in demand.
- The early date of the Sumhuram pieces therefore depends on which rouletted-ware
  chronology is adopted, and Avanzini concedes that the oldest layers are mixed
  (p. 498).

**South Arabian agency**

- Avanzini 2011, p. 10: "I felt sure that trade in the kingdoms of south Arabia was run
  by the state". She calls the idea of Roman trade "transforming a people of
  camel-drivers into one of sea-goers" "completely erroneous".
- Avanzini 2014, p. 499: "avec l'arrivée des Romains, un avant-poste commercial
  deviendrait une ville". Rome intensified rather than created the network, which is her
  reading of Strabo 2.5.12 (p. 490).
- De Romanis 2011, pp. 119-121: PME 33 (emended) describes three businesses at Moscha.
  - Kane ships collecting autumn *cariathum*
  - "Greek" ships returning from India in January-February trading cotton, grain and oil
    for frankincense ("triangular commerce", Gupta 2007)
  - late ships wintering March-June for the spring *dathiathum* harvest
- Tomber 2012, p. 208: Egyptian coarse wares at Khor Rori (jugs, cooking pots and bowls)
  are "contrary to the documents and conventional interpretation" and are most simply
  read as on-board provisions of Egyptian sailors.

**Gulf**

- Pavan 2011, p. 106: links in the BC centuries were "sporadic trade which might even
  have been overland", and the Gulf trade "came to fullness after the birth of Christ".

**Mediterranean**

- Pavan 2011, p. 106: the largest amphora group is Italic Dressel 2-4. "The oldest
  strata of the city included some vessels from Kos and Rhodes earlier than 1st century
  AD … The same types have been found in Arikamedu (Will 1996, 2004)".
- Avanzini 2014, p. 498, by contrast, notes "l'absence presque totale d'amphores datant
  d'avant notre ère".

**Wheeler and Arikamedu**

- Pillai (PDF 4): Wheeler held that Arikamedu was "a sleepy fishing village before the
  arrival of Greco Romans" with a 200-year history. Casal and Begley extended the
  sequence.
- Rouletted ware was first classed by Wheeler with Mediterranean imports.
  - Walker (p. 231) quotes Krishna Deva: "This pattern is not an Indian feature and may
    be regarded as an importation from the Mediterranean region".
  - Tomber 2010, pp. 42-43, records the revision: "Begley has, however, convincingly
    demonstrated an indigenous source for both the well-made ineware and the coarser
    examples". Gogte's XRD points to the Ganges basin near Chandraketugarh and Tamluk.
    "Salles … has instead suggested a Seleucid inspiration".
- Pillai (PDF 15): "The imported pottery, including Amphora forms a mere fraction of one
  percent of the pottery at the site". No source is given.

**Rouletted ware beyond India**

- Walker and Santoso 1977, pp. 232-234: the ware was "made in India rather than farther
  west". "Whether they were made by Mediterranean or local craftsmen we cannot tell".
  Its distribution suggests "dispersal from a center in southern India … trading up the
  coast as far as the Ganges delta". Its presence in Java "demands a reconsideration" of
  sea routes to Southeast Asia.
- The authors' term "Romano-Indian" and their suggestion that local potters were
  "instructed by foreign potters" are superseded by Begley and Gogte, as summarised in
  Tomber 2010.

## 5. Methods material useful for teaching

1. **Fabric plus form, with petrography as a referee** (Tomber 2010, pp. 46-47).
   - "In many cases, when dealing with fragmentary sherds, variations in clay or fabric
     are a more reliable means of differentiation."
   - Campanian Dressel 2-4: "red or orange … well-sorted inclusions of what looks to be
     black sand, but is identiiable in thin section as volcanic minerals (particularly
     pyroxenes)".
   - Koan: "red to pale or buff, relatively ine-textured … typically having a greenish
     surface".
   - Pavan 2011 (p. 106) uses the same Campanian criterion at Sumhuram.
2. **Imitation versus import:**
   - **Yemeni amphorae copying Roman shapes:** "amphorae used for transportation that
     copied the shape of the amphorae produced in Roman times but of a fabric that is
     undoubtedly Yemeni in origin" (Pavan 2011, p. 111; Pavan and Pallecchi 2009 on a
     talc-based fabric).
   - **Local stone imitations:** "a calcarenite tray in the same shape of the stone
     vessels made out of talc-schist coming from Yemen" (Pallecchi and Pavan 2011,
     p. 91).
   - **Rouletted Type 1 in fine versus coarse fabric at Pattanam:** "The fabric of the
     coarse varieties is quite distinct from the iner one and is very similar to the
     thousands of coarse ware sherds found at Pattanam" (Selvakumar et al. 2010, p. 36).
   - **Look-alikes:** orange Alagankulam rouletted ware was once "Mistakenly allied with
     … African Red Slip ware", and Red Polished Ware was mistaken for sigillata (Tomber
     2010, p. 43). Conical "Wheeler Type 74/75" jars are Indian, not amphorae (p. 43).
     Torpedo jars were long published as Roman: "many vessels published as Roman
     instead belong to … the torpedo jar … over half of them yielded Mesopotamian sherds"
     (p. 50).
3. **A local reference fabric from the geology** (Pallecchi and Pavan 2011, p. 83):
   "The presence of the carbonatic component both in the plateau rocks and in the
   sediments along the coast … allows the distinction between the objects produced with
   local materials and those imported". Their fabric groups appear in Figs 7-8 (p. 89).
   The ethnographic box (pp. 92-94) shows rouletting with "metal wires, twisted around a
   ring" in modern Dhofari ware. This is a useful warning that rouletting alone is not
   diagnostic of Indian rouletted ware.
4. **Rice temper as a provenance marker** (Avanzini 2014, p. 491 and Fig. 9; Tomber,
   Cartwright and Gupta). It ties Sumhuram and Berenike cooking ware to Gujarat.
5. **Manufacturing traces** (Tomber 2012, pp. 205-206): internal wiping marks on Indian
   cooking pots at Quseir and Berenike, matched ethnographically to "scooping" in
   northern Kerala and archaeologically at Pattanam but not at Arikamedu. This is a
   technique-based link between the Red Sea and southwest India.
6. **No analysis is possible without permission**, at Arikamedu (Pillai, PDF 22):
   "Unfortunately no permission been granted by ASI to send samples for laboratory
   analysis no petrological or chemical analysis of the clay or clips could be
   undertaken." The Arikamedu fabric classes (Coarse 1-3, Fine 1-5, PDF 23-24) are
   therefore macroscopic only.
7. **Contexts can be lost entirely** (Pillai, PDF 22): "The pottery from most loci …
   has lost much of its contextual significance … because of robber trenches, dumps and
   secondary deposits."
