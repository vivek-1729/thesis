# Were Westerners living at Muziris? Text against archaeology

*Part 4, argument 2, of `ceramics_and_ports.md`, rebuilt. Prepared 10 November 2026.*

---

## The question, and why it matters

What made Muziris commercially important? Two answers sit behind the literature:

- **A foreign community.** Western merchants lived there, and the port's importance came from their presence. This is Casson's position, and Wheeler's for Arikamedu.
- **A local port serving seasonal visitors.** Local people ran the port: they lightered cargo, stored pepper, and supplied and financed ships. Western ships came and went with the monsoon. The port's importance came from local organisation and from its position.

The difference matters for the thesis question. It also matters for the locating problem. If Western presence at Muziris was seasonal and confined to the waterfront, its traces should concentrate where ships were handled. That makes the position of the wharf relative to the first-century channel something the coastline and river work can test.

**What is not new.** That Indians are better attested in Egypt than Westerners in India has already been said: "there is currently firmer evidence for the presence of Arabian and Indian merchant communities in Egypt than vice versa, although evidence for the latter is not absent" (Cobb 2018: 8). That Western traders stayed only while trade was done has also been said: they "would have had to stay, even if only while trade was conducted and ships were unloaded and provisioned" (Tomber 2008: 148, following Seland). This document does not claim either point.

**What is added.** Three things:

1. **The same test in both directions.** The test that establishes Indian residents in Egypt is applied, item by item, to Westerners at Pattanam.
2. **A match between text and archaeology.** Wheat and grape pips in the Pattanam wharf deposit meet Periplus 56's grain imported for "those involved with shipping". None of the sources read for this document makes that connection.
3. **The Tamil evidence split by coast.** On the Chera coast the Yavanas are only ever arriving and leaving.

- **[Read]**: checked in the source.

## 1. The textual case for a resident colony

Casson's case rests on three texts: "At Muziris/Nelkynda there are unmistakable indications of a foreign colony" (Casson 1989: 24). **[Read]** Each one has since been weakened.

### 1a. The Temple of Augustus on the Peutinger Table

- **Casson's reading.** "The clearest evidence comes from the Tabula Peutingeriana ... a building identified as Templ(um) Augusti ... such a building could only have been put up by Roman subjects living there" (Casson 1989: 24). **[Read]**
- **The map's date.** The map is a medieval copy of a late Roman original, about AD 300 by Talbert's reckoning (reported in the BMCR review of Talbert 2010) or the 4th–5th century by others. It may incorporate itineraries "going back perhaps to the 2nd c." (Gurukkal and Whittaker 2001: 337 n. 12). **[Agent-read]**
- **Its location is uncertain.** "The accuracy of the map is not enough for us to be sure that the temple of Augustus was actually at Muziris" (Gurukkal and Whittaker 2001: 337–338). **[Agent-read]**
- **Other explanations.** "A Roman temple could have been established by local chieftains as a diplomatic gesture ... It is equally plausible that a religious structure existed on the spot, which the Romans merely represented as a monument familiar to them" (Tomber 2008: 30). Tomber then writes that the temple "as a real edifice has already been largely discounted" (2008: 148). **[Read]**

**Assessment.** The temple is a late, schematic icon on a map, and at best it is consistent with a Roman cult place near Muziris at some date. It cannot carry residence.

### 1b. The Muziris papyrus (P. Vindob. G 40822)

- **Casson's two readings.** In 1989 Casson read it as "a loan agreement drawn up at Muziris" (1989: 24, 33 n. 52). In 1990 his new reading made it "a virtual certainty" that the surviving document was drawn up at a Red Sea port, and only the original loan "at Muziris" (Casson 1990: 203, 206). **[Agent-read]**
- **Gurukkal and Whittaker.** "What the document does not say, however, is that a contract was made in Muziris, as has been asserted. It therefore tells us nothing about the signatories to the agreements nor where the contracts were drawn up — much less whether there was a Roman agent 'resident at Muziris'" (2001: 337). **[Read]**
- **De Romanis.** The phrase κατὰ Μουζεῖριν refers to "a traditionally established deadline, in which the loans for commercial enterprises to Muziris were repaid", and the recto "is the loan contract itself" (De Romanis 2014: 79–83). **[Agent-read]** His 2020 book places the contract at Alexandria (reported by Moioli, BMCR 2020.11.32).
- **Where the agents are.** Every agent of the lender mentioned in the surviving text acts in Egypt: at the Red Sea port, at Koptos and at Alexandria. None is placed at Muziris. **[Agent-read]**

**Assessment.** On the current reading the papyrus says nothing about Westerners at Muziris.

### 1c. Periplus 56: grain for "those involved with shipping"

This is the one text that still stands.

- **The text.** Among the imports to Muziris and Nelkynda: "grain in sufficient amount for those involved with shipping, because the [sc. local] merchants do not use it" (PME 56; Casson 1989: 85). **[Read]**
- **Casson's resident reading.** He reads it as resident Westerners: the locals "eat the local rice, whereas 'those involved with shipping' are Westerners who prefer to eat what they have been accustomed to ... These Westerners, permanently established, served as middlemen" (Casson 1989: 24). **[Read]**
- **Casson's own admission.** "All translators have given it" the sense of the crew (1989: 264). His objection is that crews' provisions do not belong in a list of goods for sale. **[Read]**
- **The seasonal reading.** "Presumably, therefore, the shippers and their crews disembarked and lived in the ports while waiting several months for the return monsoon winds, while the trading organisations and warehouses must have been local" (Gurukkal and Whittaker 2001: 336). **[Read]**

Either way, the passage says someone at Muziris was eating imported grain because the local diet was rice. Casson's own note cites Marco Polo for the Malabar coast: "No wheat grows in this province, but rice only" (1989: 24 n. 24). **[Read]** That gives a prediction that archaeology can test.

### 1d. The Tamil poems, split by coast

The Sangam corpus is dated broadly between 300 BC and AD 300, and the *Cilappatikāram* to about the 5th century (Tomber 2008: 26–27). **[Read]** In the passages located, the coasts look different.

| Text | Place | What the Yavanas do |
|---|---|---|
| *Akanāṉūṟu* 149 | **Muciṟi, the Chera coast** | Their ships "come with gold and return with pepper" (trans. Hart). They are arriving and leaving. |
| *Patiṟṟuppattu* II | **The Chera court** | Yavanas "of harsh speech" are captured and bound. Hostile, with no settlement. |
| *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 56 | Madurai (Pandya) | Their ships bring wine. |
| *Mullaippāṭṭu* 59–62 | A royal war camp | Yavana guards. |
| *Neṭunalvāṭai* 101–2 | Madurai palace | A lamp made by Yavanas. |
| *Paṭṭiṉappālai* 211–18 | Puhar (east coast) | Foreigners of many languages living together. |
| *Cilappatikāram* 5.10–12 | Puhar (east coast; later text) | "Yavanar irukkai", the dwellings of the Yavanas. |

All entries are **[Agent-read]** from the Tamil texts in `library/sangam/` and published translations.

**Assessment.** Yavanas are described as *residing* only at Puhar on the east coast, in a later text, and as guards or craftsmen in Madurai. On the Chera coast, the coast of Muziris, they arrive and leave. The search covered 38 locally held Tamil texts, not the whole corpus, so this is a pattern in what was searched, not a proof of absence.

## 2. The archaeological test, applied in both directions

**The test.** Cobb sets out the criterion. Fine wares travel through many hands. Coarse and cooking wares "are more likely to be culturally specific and narrowly utilized" and so are better evidence of people (Cobb 2023: 369–370). **[Read]**

### Indians in the Roman Red Sea ports pass it

| Category | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Cooking pots** | Coarse Red-Slipped Ware, sooted and wiped with bamboo, at Berenike, Myos Hormos and Koptos, and at Sumhuram, Qana and Ras al-Khaimah. Part of it is attributed to south-west India (Cobb 2023: 376; Tomber 2008: 46–47). **[Read]** |
| **Food** | Rice, including de-husked rice, mung beans, coconuts and gooseberries at Myos Hormos and Berenike (Cobb 2023: 376). **[Read]** |
| **Names** | Tamil-Brahmi personal names on an Indian jar at Myos Hormos, on a Dressel 2-4 at Berenike, and on a reused amphora sherd at Sumhuram (Cobb 2023: 375–376). **[Read]** |
| **Cult** | Buddha statuettes and a Sanskrit–Greek dedication of AD 248 at Berenike. This is later than the Periplus (Sidebotham et al. 2025, abstract only). **[Web, abstract only]** |
| **The verdict** | "Indian merchants or their agents visited and lived in Berenike and Myos Hormos" (Sidebotham 2011: 75). **[Agent-read]** |

### Mediterranean people at Pattanam do not

| Category | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Cooking pots** | None reported. Two jug handles from 2011 "may be from Egyptian vessels". Tomber suggests the Egyptian coarse wares at Qana "and perhaps Pattanam, could easily be on-board provisions for Egyptian sailors" (Tomber 2012: 206, 208). **[Read]** | Ship's stores, not a household |
| **Lamps** | 97 by 2015, terracotta, listed among the local terracotta objects (Cherian 2015: 754; Cherian 2011: 4). **[Read]** At Arikamedu, about five Roman lamp fragments in all, one unstratified (Wheeler 1946: 101; Autiero 2019: 579, reporting Begley 1996: 366). **[Agent-read]** | Local at Pattanam |
| **Names** | No Greek or Latin owner-graffiti reported. Latin letters occur only as potters' stamps ('VIL'). At Arikamedu a sigillata sherd carries a *Tamil-Brahmi* graffito, meaning an Indian owner of a Roman dish (Tomber 2008: 150). **[Read]** | Absent |
| **Cult** | A Greco-Roman miniature head and a Sphinx intaglio (2020). The excavators present them as products of a local lapidary workshop (PAMA 2020: 2–3). **[Read]** | Ambiguous |
| **Consumption goods** | 9,125 amphora sherds, 179 sigillata, Roman glass, three glass gaming counters (Cherian 2015: 750–751). **[Read]** | Equally consistent with local elites (Tomber 2010: 48, the Welwyn analogy) |

**Result.** On the test that establishes Indians as residents in Egypt, Westerners at Pattanam do not register as residents. The one hint of Western kitchen equipment, the Egyptian jug handles, is read by the specialist as on-board provisions.

## 3. The new point: imported food at the waterfront

The Pattanam wharf has a waterlogged deposit with preserved plants. The catalogue says the botanical assemblage from the wharf context includes "black pepper, cardamom, rice, **wheat**, bread fruit seeds, **grape pips**, teak, ... green gram, lentil, mango seeds" and more (PAMA: 120; also Cherian 2012: 28, citing Kajale's reports of 2007 and 2008). **[Read]**

Set this beside the texts:

- **What the Periplus predicts.** Grain is imported to Muziris for "those involved with shipping, because the [local] merchants do not use it" (PME 56).
- **What the place grows.** Rice is the crop, not wheat (Casson 1989: 24 n. 24, citing Marco Polo).
- **What the wharf shows.** Wheat, which is not a local crop, sits beside the local rice in the waterfront deposit. So do grape pips, from grapes that do not grow on the wet Malabar coast.

**My interpretation.** This is the text–archaeology match the residence debate has lacked. It is the reverse of the rice at Berenike, which is read as food for Indians living there. At Pattanam, foreign food turns up where ships were handled, not in a domestic quarter. That fits Periplus 56 read as provisions for ships' people, and Gurukkal and Whittaker's crews "waiting several months for the return monsoon winds", better than it fits a settled colony. In the sources read here, nobody reports the wheat and grape pips in this connection. The research search turned up no mention of grape remains at Pattanam at all.

**The weaknesses, stated plainly.**

1. **Date.** The plant deposit lies beneath the ceramic layer of the wharf (Cherian 2012: 28), and the wharf's dates are the weakest in the Pattanam record. The canoe there gives a calibrated range of 1300–100 BC. So the wheat may not be first-century AD. *Test: radiocarbon-date the wheat grains themselves.*
2. **Whose wheat.** North-western India grew wheat, and the Periplus has Barygaza traders at Muziris (Tomber 2008: 148, citing PME 54 and De Romanis). The wheat may have fed Indian, not Western, crews. Either way it fed non-local people at the waterfront, which is the seasonal-port picture. It only weakens the claim that the people were Western.
3. **Grapes.** The grape pips could come from raisins or from wine lees. Grapes grow in the Deccan. They point to imported food, not specifically to Mediterranean food.
4. **Quantity.** The published lists are presence-only. The specialist reports by Kajale, and by the Oxford and Durham teams, have not been read.

## 4. The timetable makes a seasonal stay unavoidable

- **Pliny.** Ships leave Berenike in midsummer, reach Muziris about seventy days later, and must leave India in the Egyptian month Tybi or "within the Ides of January" to return the same year (Pliny, *NH* 6.104–106).
- **Casson's reading.** Ships reach India "no earlier than September" and leave in December–January (Casson 1989: 289–290). **[Agent-read]**
- **De Romanis.** He has them leave Egypt around 20 July and India by 13 January, with the deadline probably written into the loan contracts (De Romanis 2014: 81–83). **[Agent-read]**

That is a stay of about three to four months at Muziris every season. It is enough to make crews eat and lodge ashore, and it needs no permanent colony. Seland draws the opposite inference: the monsoon would leave visiting crews in port for months, and "some of them stayed behind" (Seland 2013: 374, 377–378). **[Agent-read]** The archaeology above is the test between Seland's inference and the seasonal reading. So far it favours the seasonal one.

**Why the asymmetry? A question for the voyage model.** Indians in Egypt left kitchens, names and shrines. Westerners at Muziris left provisions at the wharf. One possible cause is simply the timetable: how long each party was forced to wait at the far end before the wind turned. The project's voyage simulator can compute the minimum forced stay at each end. If the stays are similar, the asymmetry was a choice of institutions, not something the wind imposed. That would be a finding in itself.

## 5. The argument, stated

> **The texts that were thought to prove a Western colony at Muziris no longer do. The Temple of Augustus is a late map icon, and the Muziris papyrus places the lender's agents only in Egypt. The one text that survives, Periplus 56, describes grain imported for people connected with ships. The Pattanam wharf has the matching evidence: wheat and grape pips, foreign food, at the waterfront. Nothing in the Pattanam record passes the domestic test that shows Indians living in Egypt, and on the Chera coast the Tamil poems show Yavanas only arriving and leaving. The best-supported picture is a port run by local people for seasonal foreign crews, who ate imported food while they waited for the wind. On this reading Muziris was important because of how it handled the turnaround, not because foreigners settled there.**

**What would overturn it:**

- a domestic Mediterranean assemblage from any Pattanam trench, meaning cooking ware, lamps or owner-graffiti;
- a date for the wharf wheat that falls outside the trading centuries;
- Kajale's reports showing the wheat is widespread across the site rather than concentrated at the wharf.

**What it means for locating Muziris.** If Western presence is a waterfront phenomenon, then the Muziris of the Periplus is where ships were handled. The question becomes where the first-century waterfront was. That is the shoreline and channel question in `quantitative/`. Pattanam's wharf sits about 220 m from the undated ancient shoreline in Narayana et al. 2001. The bathymetry pilot, `quantitative/coastline/bathymetry_1857.ipynb`, shows that in 1851–53 the Kodungallur bar admitted only small craft at low water. Ships of the Periplus anchored off and lightered, as Pliny says for Muziris: "longe a terra ... lintribusque adferuntur onera et egeruntur" (cargo is carried to and from the ships in boats, because they lie far from land). That is consistent with this picture.
