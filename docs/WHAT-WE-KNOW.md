# Where were these ports? A survey of the evidence, site by site

## The problem

The *Periplus Maris Erythraei* is a merchant's handbook to the Indian Ocean,
written in Greek around AD 50 by an anonymous trader working out of Roman Egypt.
It names dozens of harbours between the Red Sea and the Bay of Bengal, gives the
distances between them, and says what could be bought and sold at each. It is
the single most detailed account of Indian Ocean commerce to survive from
antiquity.

For a great many of the places it names, nobody knows where they were.

There are three reasons the question is hard. The text comes down to us in one
manuscript, a tenth-century copy in Heidelberg, with a single later apograph, so
there is no independent witness against which to check a corrupted place name or
a mistaken number. The distances are given in stadia and in days' sail, neither
of which converts cleanly into kilometres. And the coastlines have moved in the
two thousand years since: rivers have switched channels, deltas have grown
several kilometres seaward, and harbours that were open water in antiquity are
now silted lagoon or dry land.

Six ports are seriously disputed, and they are the subject of this project:
Leukē Kōmē on the Red Sea coast of Arabia, Barbarikon in the Indus delta,
Tyndis, Nelkynda and Bakarē on the Malabar coast of Kerala, and Rhapta somewhere
on the coast of Tanzania.

## What evidence can be brought to bear

Four kinds, and they are not equally useful.

The first is the distances in the text itself. The Periplus measures in stadia,
and the value of the stadion is not fixed; scholars have generally assumed about
185 metres. We calibrated it empirically instead, by taking the legs of the
voyage where both the departure point and the destination are securely
identified, and dividing the stated stadia by the real distance. The median of
those legs gives roughly **155 metres**. This is not a trivial correction. It
moves every position derived from a stated distance by about sixteen per cent,
and as the Tyndis section below shows, that is enough to change which candidate
fits.

The second is material evidence: imported pottery, coins, glass and beads
recovered from excavation. Where it exists it is decisive. The central finding
of this survey is that for most of the disputed ports it does not exist, and the
reason it does not exist is not what one might assume.

The third is coins specifically, which we have now assembled at scale. The
Coin Hoards of the Roman Empire database, maintained by the Ashmolean Museum and
the Oxford Roman Economy Project, yielded 627 hoards and 10,780 individually
recorded coins for the Indian Ocean and Red Sea region. To this we added coins
recovered from occupation layers at excavated sites, which the hoard database
does not cover and which are the more informative category for locating a
harbour.

The fourth is later settlement: whether a place was still functioning as a port
in AD 900 or AD 1500. On its own this proves nothing about the first century,
but it turns out to explain a great deal about how the candidate lists were
assembled.

## Leukē Kōmē, the Nabataean port on the Red Sea

### The history of scholarship

The Periplus describes Leukē Kōmē as a harbour in the territory of the
Nabataeans, the Arab kingdom centred on Petra, with a customs post and a Roman
garrison. Two locations on the north-western coast of Saudi Arabia have been
proposed. The first is **Aynuna**, near the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, where a
joint Saudi and Polish expedition excavated between 2014 and 2018. The second is
**al-Wajh**, roughly two hundred kilometres to the south, argued for on the
grounds of sailing distance from Egypt.

The standard modern treatment is Dario Nappo's 2010 article in the *Journal of
Roman Archaeology*, which argues for Aynuna on the basis of the distance the
Periplus gives from the Egyptian port of Myos Hormos. We found a problem with
that argument. Nappo measured from Abu Sha'ar, which is now generally rejected
as the site of Myos Hormos; the port is instead placed at Quseir al-Qadim,
further south. When the measurement is taken from Quseir al-Qadim, Aynuna lies
236 kilometres away and al-Wajh 222 kilometres, and both fall comfortably inside
the 185 to 278 kilometre window that Nappo himself derives. The distance
argument, in other words, does not distinguish between the two candidates at
all.

### The material evidence

Here the two candidates part company decisively. The excavations at Aynuna
uncovered harbour installations, a large storehouse, and two cemeteries, all of
Nabataean date. They also produced a catalogue of coins, which we extracted:
**twenty-five specimens, of which twenty-four are bronze**, attributable to
Nabataean rulers from Obodas III and Aretas IV through to the emperor Tiberius,
and dating between the second century BC and about AD 40.

The metal matters. Bronze is low-value coinage, and bronze lost in occupation
layers means small change circulating in daily use. That is the signature of a
working harbour where people bought and sold, and it falls exactly in the period
the Periplus describes.

Al-Wajh has never been excavated. There is a coin findspot about a kilometre
from the proposed site, which at first looked like a serious rival, but on
inspection it consists of three late Roman *nummi* of the emperor Maximianus,
struck in AD 295 or 296, with a second findspot nearby holding three of
Constantine I from the AD 320s. These are two and a half centuries later than
the Periplus and tell us nothing about a first-century harbour.

### Later settlement

Aynuna has only limited early Islamic material overlying the Nabataean port.
The fact that it ceased to be a harbour is precisely why archaeologists have
been able to excavate it at all.

### Where this leaves the question

Leukē Kōmē is the one disputed port where material evidence genuinely settles
something. It favours Aynuna, and it does so for a better reason than the
distance argument usually cited.

## Barbarikon, the port of the Indus delta

### The history of scholarship

The Periplus places Barbarikon on the middle of seven mouths of the Indus,
serving the inland city of Minnagar. Alexander Cunningham identified it with
**Banbhore**, about sixty kilometres east of Karachi, in the nineteenth century,
and that identification remains the majority view. It has never been
demonstrated. The two modern gazetteers we compared disagree about where
Barbarikon was by 203 kilometres, which is a fair measure of how much genuine
uncertainty remains.

There is a long-standing problem with the distance given in the text, though it
is less useful than it first appears. The Periplus gives 3,000 stadia from
Barbarikon to a promontory called Papica, which Schoff identified with Gopinath
Point on the Saurashtra coast, at the mouth of the Gulf of Khambhat opposite
Barygaza. The text describes this explicitly as a sailing course along the
coast. Any plausible route from Banbhore to Gopinath Point, even one that cuts
across the mouths of the Gulf of Kutch and the Gulf of Khambhat as the Periplus
says ships did, runs to something like 700 kilometres or more, whereas 3,000
stadia is 465 kilometres at 155 metres and 555 at 185. The stated figure is
therefore too small for the journey it describes.

This is a genuine difficulty, but it is a difficulty about the number or the
route rather than about the identification. Moving Barbarikon eastward would
shorten the distance, but as the next section shows, the material evidence
anchors it at Banbhore.

### The material evidence

Banbhore has been excavated for something like thirty seasons, first by Fazal
Ahmad Khan for the Pakistani Department of Archaeology between 1958 and 1966,
and more recently by a joint Italian and Pakistani mission. The most visible
remains belong to the Islamic-period harbour of **Daybul**, occupied from
roughly the eighth to the thirteenth century, and the later town obscures what
lies beneath it.

Beneath the Islamic and Sasanian phases, however, Khan identified a
Scytho-Parthian level, conventionally dated to between the first century BC and
the second century AD, which is precisely the horizon of the Periplus. Mohammad
Rafique Mughal published the pottery from these earliest levels in *Pakistan
Archaeology* 33 (2018): Red Polished Ware of the first century BC to second
century AD, a ware whose source is Gujarat and which is found at sites across
the Indo-Roman trade network. On that basis Mughal identifies Banbhore with
Barbarikon directly.

The site has never been excavated to virgin soil. Khan's trenches were stopped
by heavy water infiltration, and the Italian mission has not reported reaching
the bottom either. The earliest levels at Banbhore are therefore not absent;
they are below the water table and largely undug. That is an engineering
obstacle rather than an archaeological finding, and it is the reason the
first-century evidence is thinner than the site's importance would suggest.

### Later settlement

Daybul was a major harbour of the Islamic period, and it sits directly on top of
whatever preceded it. This is the ordinary difficulty of Indian Ocean port
archaeology in a compact form: the places that were good harbours stayed good
harbours, and the later town buries the earlier one.

## Tyndis, five hundred stadia north of Muziris

### The history of scholarship

The Periplus locates Tyndis 500 stadia north of Muziris, the great pepper
emporium of the Malabar coast. Four modern villages have been proposed:
**Ponnani**, which is the common identification, **Tanur**, the cluster of
**Kadalundi, Beypore and Chaliyam**, and **Koyilandy**. None of these proposals
rests on an excavation. All were arrived at by combining the distance in the
text with the later history of each place as a harbour.

### The distance, which is the most striking result we have

Muziris is now generally identified with the excavated site at **Pattanam**, in
the Periyar delta, for reasons set out below. Measuring north from Pattanam
along the coast gives the following:

| Candidate | Distance from Pattanam |
|---|---|
| Ponnani | 75 km |
| Tanur | 99 km |
| Kadalundi / Beypore / Chaliyam | 116 km |
| Koyilandy | 154 km |

Five hundred stadia at the calibrated value of 155 metres is 77.5 kilometres,
and Ponnani lies 75 kilometres from Pattanam in a straight line.

That looks like an extremely close fit, and it is worth being careful about how
close it really is. The figures above are straight-line distances, whereas the
Periplus is recording a distance sailed along a coast. A ship does not travel in
a straight line, so the true sailed distance to each candidate is somewhat
greater than the figure given. It is not twice as great: measuring along every
vertex of the modern coastline gives 155 kilometres to Ponnani, but that path
follows each estuary and backwater inlet, which no vessel standing off the coast
would do. The real figure lies between the two and nearer the lower end, but we
cannot currently say where.

**What is defensible is the ordering rather than the precision.** For any value
of the stadion between about 148 and 170 metres, the target distance falls
closer to Ponnani than to any other candidate, and Tanur, Kadalundi and
Koyilandy are progressively too far north. Only at the conventional 185 metres,
giving 92.5 kilometres, does Tanur become competitive. So the empirical
calibration does real work here, and it selects Ponnani, but the claim should be
stated as a ranking and not as a match to within a couple of kilometres.

One further notice supports the underlying anchor. The Periplus says Muziris
itself lies 20 stadia up its river, which at 155 metres is about 3.1 kilometres.
Pattanam sits roughly 4 kilometres from the sea. That is an independent check on
the Muziris identification that does not depend on the Tyndis argument at all.

### The material evidence

There is none, and this is not for want of looking. In the field season of 1978
to 1979, officers of the Southern Circle of the Archaeological Survey of India
explored the districts of Malappuram and Palghat, in the published words of the
report, "while investigating for the sites showing Roman contact in the Ponnani
valley." What they found was a series of megalithic burials of the *topikal*
type, a Kerala form of Iron Age tomb capped with a hemispherical stone, together
with standing stones and rock-cut graves. They recorded no Roman material.

This is worth distinguishing from ordinary absence. Archaeologists went to the
right place, looking for exactly the thing the Tyndis identification predicts,
and did not find it. That is a negative result rather than a gap in the record.

### Later settlement

All four candidates are well documented as harbours in later centuries.
Koyilandy is the medieval port of **Panthalayani Kollam**, visited by Ibn
Battuta in the fourteenth century, called Flandarina by the Franciscan traveller
Odoric of Pordenone and Pandarani by the Portuguese. Ponnani became a major
Mappila commercial and religious centre. Beypore was a shipbuilding town whose
yards still construct ocean-going vessels of the traditional *uru* type.
Chaliyam held a Portuguese fort from 1531.

## Nelkynda and Bakarē, the river port and its outlet

### The history of scholarship

The Periplus treats these two together. Nelkynda lies 500 stadia from Muziris
and a further 120 stadia up a river from the sea; Bakarē is the village at that
river's mouth, where ships took on cargo before sailing. Lionel Casson, whose
1989 edition is the standard one, placed Nelkynda at **Niranam**. Wilfred Schoff,
in his 1912 translation, preferred **Kottayam** on the Minachal river. Older
scholarship favoured **Kollam**, also called Quilon, and a minority has argued
for **Neendakara**. For Bakarē, Schoff proposed **Purakkad**, McCrindle
**Kallada**, and a minority **Thevalakara**.

### The distance

Measuring south from Pattanam gives:

| Candidate | Distance from Pattanam |
|---|---|
| Kottayam | 71 km |
| Purakkad | 90 km |
| Niranam | 106 km |
| Thevalakara | 135 km |
| Kallada | 138 km |
| Neendakara | 140 km |
| Kollam / Quilon | 147 km |

Against the same 77.5 kilometre target, Kollam and Neendakara are far too
distant to be considered. They are independently excluded by the second notice
in the text, since 120 stadia upriver is about 18.6 kilometres and both sit
roughly four kilometres from the sea. More awkwardly, **Casson's Niranam, at 106
kilometres, fits poorly**, and Schoff's Kottayam fits the coastal distance best
of the four even though it is an inland town on a backwater rather than a
coastal harbour. No candidate for either port is well supported, and the fit is
worse than the literature generally acknowledges.

### The material evidence

There is none at all. *Indian Archaeology: A Review*, the annual record of every
excavation and exploration reported in India, has been published since 1953. We
assembled 78 volumes covering 59 distinct years, from 1953 to 2014, and searched
the whole run. Niranam, Neendakara, Purakkad, Pirakkad and Thevalakara appear
**zero times**. Kallada appears once, in connection with a neolithic stone axe
found in the river basin. Not one of the seven candidates for these two ports
has ever been excavated.

### Later settlement

All of the Nelkynda candidates were significant medieval places. Kollam was a
major port visited by Arab, Chinese and Jewish merchants and described by Marco
Polo; it is important enough that the Malayalam calendar era, still in use, is
named the Kollam era and begins in AD 825. Purakkad was an early modern pepper
port with a Dutch factory in the seventeenth century. Kallada and Thevalakara,
by contrast, have no attested later occupation, and rest on topographic argument
alone.

## Rhapta, the metropolis of Azania

### The history of scholarship

The Periplus describes Rhapta as the last market town on the African coast, two
days' sail beyond an island called Menuthias, and calls it a metropolis. Ptolemy
later repeats the name. Nothing else survives.

The modern debate begins with Bassett Datoo's 1970 article in *Azania*, which
narrowed the possible location to the stretch of coast between the mouths of the
Pangani and Rufiji rivers, favouring the northern part of that range. Every
later argument inherits this bracket. Gervase Mathew argued specifically for the
Pangani mouth. Neville Chittick and later Felix Chami argued for the Rufiji
delta, and Chami additionally reported submerged structures off Mafia Island.
More recently Hughes attempted a geographic information systems approach.

The textual route runs through Menuthias. The Periplus places that island 300
stadia off the mainland, which at 155 metres is about 46.5 kilometres. Measured
from island to nearest mainland shore, that notice fits Zanzibar better than
Mafia or Pemba, and if Menuthias is Zanzibar then the two days' sail beyond
points south towards the Rufiji, which is where Chami's material comes from.

### The material evidence

There are no Roman coins anywhere on this coast. This is not a failure of
searching: the Coin Hoards of the Roman Empire database has no country entry for
Tanzania, Kenya, Somalia or Ethiopia at all, so there is nothing to query.

Every coin recorded at any of the candidate sites belongs to the Islamic period
or later. Unguja Ukuu on Zanzibar produced eighth-century Muslim gold coins found
in 1866, silver of a later phase, and a Chinese bronze of the Northern Song
dynasty. Kilwa has the coinage of its own sultanate, struck between about 1200
and 1400. Mafia has medieval coins from Kisimani Mafia.

The one claim of Periplus-period material is Chami's report of Roman glass beads
from the Rufiji delta, published in *Current Anthropology* in 1999 under the
title "First Incontrovertible Archaeological Link with the Periplus." It is the
most cited archaeological claim about Rhapta, and its stratigraphy has been
questioned. We have not been able to obtain it and are currently relying on
secondary accounts, which is unsatisfactory for a claim this load-bearing.

### Later settlement

Unguja Ukuu was occupied from the sixth or seventh century, which is already too
late for the Periplus. Mafia and Kilwa are medieval. Pangani was a Swahili and
later Omani port. Dar es Salaam was founded in the 1860s.

## Five things that hold across all six ports

**Absence of evidence is mostly absence of excavation.** Thirteen of the
twenty-two candidate locations have never been dug at all. Any map that plots
find counts without accounting for how much archaeology has been done at each
place will argue the opposite of the truth, showing a concentration of trade
where there is really only a concentration of fieldwork.

**Roman coins in India do not mark ports.** Of 121 Indian hoards in the CHRE
database, 98 lie more than 25 kilometres from the coast, and they account for 73
per cent of all recorded coins. The largest concentration sits between 100 and
200 kilometres inland, in the Coimbatore district and the Palghat gap. Roman
coins in India travelled inland towards the sources of pepper and beryl and were
buried there. They record the movement of bullion and the habit of hoarding, not
the location of harbours.

**Hoards and site finds are different kinds of evidence and should not be
pooled.** The Indian material is overwhelmingly hoarded gold, 790 gold pieces
against 184 silver and almost no bronze. The Aynuna material is bronze lost in
occupation layers. The first is stored wealth; the second is money being spent.
For the purpose of locating a harbour the second is far more informative, and it
is precisely the category that the hoard databases omit.

**Every Kerala candidate is a documented later port with no first-century
evidence whatsoever.** This is a fact about how the candidate lists were built.
These villages were not proposed because Roman material was found at them. They
were proposed because they are known harbours in medieval and early modern
sources, and the identification was then projected backwards onto a
first-century text. The reasoning is not worthless, since a river mouth that
supported a port for a thousand years was probably usable in the first century
as well. But it is circular if left unexamined. **Kodungallur is the warning
case**: it was excavated in 1969 and 1970 precisely because it was the
traditional identification of Muziris, and everything recovered dated to the
ninth to eleventh centuries. The traditional identification was tested and it
failed.

**Muziris itself is less settled than it appears.** Pattanam has yielded 6,029
Roman amphora sherds, against 116 from Arikamedu, the type site for Indo-Roman
contact on the opposite coast. That is a ratio of about fifty to one and it is
why the identification has been widely accepted. But two qualifications belong
beside it. Roman wares amount to 0.17 per cent of the total ceramic assemblage,
the rest being local pottery. And Mesopotamian wares, chiefly bitumen-lined
torpedo jars, come to 4,625 sherds, about three-quarters of the Roman total.
Torpedo jars are largely of Sasanian date and are not found on Red Sea sites, so
a substantial part of Pattanam's imported pottery records traffic through the
Persian Gulf in the third to seventh centuries rather than Mediterranean trade
in the first. Pattanam was a port for far longer than it was Muziris.

## Where the six ports now stand

For **Leukē Kōmē** the material evidence decides the question, and it favours
Aynuna.

For **Tyndis** the empirically calibrated stadion selects Ponnani over the other
three candidates across the whole plausible range of stadion values, though the
comparison is between a straight-line measurement and a sailed distance and so
should be read as a ranking rather than a precise fit. The survey of 1978 to
1979 separately establishes that the absence of Roman material at Ponnani has
been tested rather than merely assumed.

For **Nelkynda and Bakarē** no candidate is well supported. The distances fit
both Casson's and Schoff's proposals less well than is generally acknowledged,
and no candidate for either port has ever been excavated.

For **Barbarikon and Rhapta** the material layer cannot help at all, and any
argument will have to be carried by the distances in the text and by the
reconstruction of ancient coastlines.

## What would change the picture

Four publications would materially improve the argument, and we do not currently
have any of them: Paula Turner's 1989 catalogue of Roman coin finds in India,
which includes the single and stray finds that the hoard databases omit; Datoo's
1970 article, which we have been citing at second hand; Chami's 1999 paper on
the Rufiji beads; and the Salles and Sedov monograph on the excavations at Qāni'
in Yemen, which would give a second quantified assemblage to set beside Aynuna.

More than any of these, though, the thing that would settle Tyndis is a trench
at Ponnani.
