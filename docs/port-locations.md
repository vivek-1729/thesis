# Where were these ports? What the evidence actually says

---

## Before the ports: the sources, the people, and the units

### The text

The *Periplus Maris Erythraei* is a merchant's handbook to the Indian Ocean, written in
Greek around the middle of the first century AD by an anonymous trader based in Roman
Egypt. It survives in exactly one manuscript, a tenth-century copy now in Heidelberg,
plus a later copy made from it. That matters more than it might seem. With only one
witness there is nothing to check a reading against, and numbers are the first thing to
go wrong when a text is copied by hand. One of the six port names discussed below is not
in the manuscript at all; it is an editor's correction.

### The people you will keep meeting

**Lionel Casson** produced the standard modern edition in 1989 — Greek text, translation
and a commentary that is still the first place anyone looks. When this document says
"Casson prefers," it means the standard edition's considered view, which carries weight
but is not the same as proof.

**Wilfred Schoff** made the 1912 English translation that Casson replaced. His
identifications are often still cited, sometimes because nobody has revisited them.

**J. W. McCrindle** translated the Greek and Latin sources on ancient India in the 1880s.
His volume on Ptolemy prints the coordinate tables in full, which is why we can use them.

**Dario Nappo** is a historian of Roman Red Sea trade; his 2010 article is the main
modern case against the consensus on Leuke Kome.

**Felix Chami**, of the University of Dar es Salaam, has surveyed the Rufiji delta and
Mafia island since the mid-1990s and is the main proponent of a Rufiji location for
Rhapta. **B. A. Datoo** wrote the 1970 paper that framed the Rhapta question.

**Alexander Cunningham** founded the Archaeological Survey of India and proposed many of
the nineteenth-century identifications still in play.

### The units — and this is the part that does real work

The *Periplus* measures distance two ways, and the difference matters.

For waters near Egypt, and for coasts the author evidently knew well, he gives
**stadia**. A stadion is a length, but not a fixed one — different systems used values
from roughly 155 to 185 metres, and the author never says which he means.

For waters further off he gives **runs** (Greek *dromoi*). A run is not a length at all.
It is *the distance a ship covers between morning and nightfall* — one day's sailing.
Along dangerous coasts, ships anchored overnight rather than sail in the dark, so a day's
run was a natural unit. When the author means a full twenty-four hours he says so
explicitly: "a day and a night."

Casson reckons the ancient rule of thumb as **500 stadia to a day's run, 1,000 to a
day-and-night run** — roughly 100 nautical miles for the latter. So when the text says
Rhapta lies "two runs" beyond Menuthias, it means about two days' sailing, and Casson
converts that to roughly 185 km.

**Which stadion?** Rather than choose one, I measured it. Taking the four legs where both
ends are securely known — Myos Hormos to Berenike, Okelis to Eudaimon Arabia, Tyndis to
Muziris, Muziris to Nelkynda — and dividing the stated stadia by the real distance gives
a median of **155 metres**, with a spread of 125 to 198. Because I measured straight-line
distances and ships do not sail straight lines, the true figure is a little higher;
something near 160 m is the best working value. Casson assumed 185 m, which now looks too
high.

Every distance below marked *(measured)* is mine, computed at 155 m per stadion. Treat
them as approximate.

### Ptolemy

Claudius Ptolemy's *Geography*, written a century after the *Periplus*, lists thousands
of places with latitude and longitude. This looks like exactly what we need, and it is
half of what we need. Testing his coordinates for eight Indian sites whose real positions
are known, **his longitudes are good and his latitudes are not**: longitude correlates
with reality at r = 0.97, latitude at only 0.48. He gave Mouziris, Podouke and Kolkhoi
latitudes of 14–15° when the true figures are 10.2, 11.9 and 8.6. He had effectively
squashed southern India into a single band.

So his east–west ordering along a coast is usable evidence. His latitudes are not.

## Leuke Kome — the Nabataean port in north-west Arabia

The *Periplus* (ch. 19) says Leuke Kome lay two or three days' sail from Myos Hormos, and
that it had a garrison and a customs house taking a quarter of the value of goods
landed — a substantial operation, not a beach. Strabo, writing slightly earlier, tells a
different story about the same place: when the Roman prefect Aelius Gallus invaded Arabia
around 25 BC, his fleet of 130 ships took **fourteen days** to get there, and Strabo adds
that camel caravans travelled between Leuke Kome and Petra "in safety and ease."

Two candidates are seriously in play.

**Aynunah**, near the mouth of the Gulf of Aqaba, is the majority view. A Saudi–Polish
team excavated there between 2014 and 2018 and found Nabataean and Roman pottery, two
cemeteries and substantial stone buildings. The port itself would have been at
**Khuraybah**, on the water, rather than at Aynunah proper a few kilometres inland.

**al-Wajh**, about 200 km down the coast, has been argued for by several scholars and
most fully by Nappo. No Hellenistic or Roman material has ever been reported from there.

Strabo's caravan route fits the north and does not fit al-Wajh, which is a long way from
Petra. The archaeology is only at Aynunah. Against that, the excavators went looking for
Leuke Kome at a site they already expected to be Leuke Kome, so finding Nabataean
material there confirms people lived there, not what the place was called.

**Something I can add.** Nappo's main argument is arithmetic: two or three runs should be
185–278 km, and Aynunah is too close for that. But his calculation starts from Myos
Hormos at Abu Sha'ar — and Myos Hormos is not at Abu Sha'ar. Excavations by Peacock and
Blue placed it at Quseir al-Qadim, further south. Measuring from there, Aynunah is 236 km
and al-Wajh 222 km *(measured)* — **both comfortably inside Nappo's own range**. His
distance argument does not survive the correction.

That leaves Aynunah in front on the archaeology, and leaves Strabo's fourteen days
unexplained. It need not be a normal voyage — Gallus had a large laden fleet and Strabo
blames sabotage — but nobody has demonstrated that the two figures describe the same trip.

## Barbarikon — somewhere in the Indus delta

The *Periplus* (ch. 38–39) puts Barbarikon on **the middle of the seven mouths of the
Indus**, with a small island in front of it. Ships moored there, but the cargo went
upriver to the king at Minnagar. Later (ch. 41) the author says the coastal voyage from
Barbarikon round to a promontory opposite Barygaza is **3,000 stadia**.

The obvious candidate is **Banbhore**, a walled town on the Gharo Creek about 30 km
inland of today's shoreline. Cunningham proposed it in the nineteenth century, following
Henry Elliot, and it has been the majority view since. The eighteenth-century geographer
D'Anville had preferred Debal Sindhi or Karachi.

Banbhore has been excavated and was clearly a working harbour town from the first century
BC to the thirteenth AD. But the excavators themselves say there is no agreement on
whether it is Barbarikon, or Daybul — the port the Arabs stormed in 711 — or both at
different times. Nothing found there names the place.

**This is the weakest of the six, and the standard edition says so.** Casson quotes
Vincent Smith's verdict that the shifting of the Sind rivers over eighteen centuries
"preclude[s] the possibility of satisfactory identifications" of Barbarikon or Minnagar.
Minnagar itself has five competing locations — Tatta, Bahmanabad, Brahminabad, Indore and
Madhyamika — which tells you how little is fixed.

**The distance is a problem nobody has resolved.** Three thousand stadia is about 465 km.
Banbhore to Astakapra is 647 km *(measured)*. Casson noticed the same gap and put it down
to the author overestimating. That is possible, but it is also possible that Barbarikon
was not where Banbhore is.

The one thing here that can actually be tested is the claim about the middle mouth. That
is a precise statement, and Ptolemy lists all seven mouths with coordinates, making the
fourth the target. The Indus has moved repeatedly since antiquity, but buried river
channels show up in satellite imagery. This is the port where new evidence is most likely
to change the answer.

## Rhapta — the last market on the African coast

Sailing south along East Africa, the *Periplus* (ch. 15) reaches an island called
**Menuthias, "about 300 stadia from the mainland"** — low, wooded, with rivers and sewn
boats. **Two runs further on** (ch. 16) lies Rhapta, named for those sewn boats, trading
ivory and tortoise-shell, and described as the very last market of the continent: beyond
it, "the unexplored ocean."

Ptolemy gives three separate entries — a Rhapta *metropolis* "a short distance from the
sea," the mouth of a river Rhaptus, and a Rhaptum promontory. So Rhapta sat slightly
inland on a river, with its mouth below it. That is the same arrangement the *Periplus*
describes for Muziris and Nelkynda in India.

Four locations have been proposed: the **Pangani** river mouth (first suggested by Baxter,
reported by Allen in 1949, and supported by Gervase Mathew in 1975); **Dar es Salaam or
Bagamoyo** (Schoff in 1912, reasoning from Ptolemy; and independently Hughes in 2016
using GIS); the **Rufiji delta** (Chami, from his surveys since 1995); and the **Mafia**
archipelago, where submerged structures have been reported.

**Casson never actually decides.** His reasoning is conditional: Rhapta is two runs beyond
Menuthias, so if Menuthias is Pemba then Rhapta is near Dar es Salaam, and if Menuthias
is Zanzibar then Rhapta is at the Rufiji. Since nobody has settled which island Menuthias
was, the one distance we have is measured from an unknown starting point. Datoo concluded
in 1970 that you can narrow Rhapta to a stretch of coast between the Pangani and the
Rufiji, and no further.

**Something I can add.** The 300-stadia figure for Menuthias has been sitting there
unused. Three hundred stadia is about 46 km. Measured to the African mainland: Zanzibar
is 49 km offshore, Mafia 33 km, Pemba 72 km *(measured)*. Zanzibar matches almost exactly.
Pemba is more than half as far again as the text allows.

If Menuthias is Zanzibar, then by Casson's own reasoning Rhapta is at the Rufiji — which
is where Chami found Roman-period material. Three independent things line up: the text's
own figure, the standard edition's logic, and the archaeology.

I would not push the precision. How far an island is "from the mainland" depends on which
point you measure from, and Ryano gives Mafia as 15 km offshore where I calculate 33. But
the ordering holds, and it argues against Pemba and so against Dar es Salaam.

## Tyndis — the first Malabar port

The *Periplus* (ch. 53–54) describes Tyndis as "a village in plain sight by the sea,"
belonging to the Chera kingdom, and **500 stadia** from Muziris. It appears in Ptolemy but
in neither Pliny nor Strabo, which makes it the most thinly attested of the six.

Four places have been proposed along about 80 km of the Kerala coast: **Ponnani**,
**Tanur**, **Kadalundi/Beypore**, and **Koyilandy**. Schoff chose Ponnani and Casson
follows him, but only just — his phrase is that the odds are "slightly in favour."

Casson's actual argument for Ponnani is not archaeological. It is that Ponnani is the
harbour closest to the Palghat gap, a twenty-mile break in the Western Ghats that is the
natural land route across the peninsula to the eastern coast. A port there controls the
crossing.

**Something I can add.** Five hundred stadia is about 78 km. Distances from each candidate
to Muziris at Kodungallur *(measured)*: Ponnani 68 km, Tanur 92, Kadalundi 113,
Koyilandy 147. **Koyilandy is out — it is nearly twice the stated distance — and
Kadalundi is very hard to defend.** The real contest is Ponnani against Tanur, and
Ponnani fits better.

The phrase "in plain sight by the sea" points the same way: Ponnani sits 1.9 km from the
coast, Tanur 9.2 km.

No candidate has produced an excavated Roman-period port. That is the limiting factor
here, not the reasoning.

## Muziris — not in dispute, but not settled either

Muziris is not one of the six, but the text fixes Tyndis, Nelkynda and Bakare by
measuring from it, so an error here propagates to all three.

The *Periplus* puts Muziris on a river, 500 stadia from Tyndis and 20 stadia upstream
from the shore. Pliny calls it the first trading station in India, forty days out from
Ocelis with the monsoon behind you. A Tamil poem places Muciri at the mouth of the
Periyar, and the Peutinger Table — a late Roman road map — marks a temple of Augustus
there.

Casson called Muziris "securely located" at **Kodungallur** (Cranganore), on the strength
of the Tamil poem. On the evidence available in 1989 that was a fair call.

It no longer is. Since 2007, excavations at **Pattanam**, about 7 km to the south, have
produced the Roman material — amphorae, fine tableware, Indian rouletted ware, glass,
beads — that Kodungallur has never yielded. So there are now two sites: one with the name
and the poem, the other with the finds. Some recent sedimentary work argues Pattanam was a
satellite of a port at Kodungallur rather than Muziris itself, and the Periyar's mouth has
shifted since antiquity in any case.

What is certain is that a major Indo-Roman port complex worked in the lower Periyar. Which
settlement in it was called Muziris is not certain, and treating the question as closed
would quietly import an open one into everything measured from it.

## Nelkynda — up a river in the Pandian kingdom

This is the most precisely described of the six, which is why it can be pinned down
furthest. The *Periplus* (ch. 53–55) places Nelkynda 500 stadia beyond Muziris, in a
different kingdom — the Pandian — and, crucially, **120 stadia from the sea, up the river
whose mouth is Bakare**. Pliny knows the people as the Neacyndi and says their king
Pandion ruled from Madura inland.

Casson prefers **Niranam**, on the Pambiyar river. Schoff had chosen **Kottayam**, on the
Minachil. Dayalan lists Kallada, Neendakara, Nirkunnam, Kannetri and Kollam as further
suggestions.

Casson's case is one of internal consistency rather than evidence on the ground: only
Niranam puts Nelkynda and its harbour Bakare on the *same river*, roughly the right
distance apart — about twelve miles east of Pirakkad. Kottayam fails on both counts, being
on the wrong river and far too distant.

**Something I can add.** A hundred and twenty stadia is 19–22 km. **Kollam and Neendakara
sit about 4 km from the sea. The text excludes them.** Niranam, at roughly 15 km
*(measured)*, is the only candidate in the right range. Federico De Romanis reached a
compatible conclusion from different reasoning, placing Nelkynda in the southern part of
Vembanad Lake.

One complication: Ptolemy lists Melkynda — his form of the name — among a people called
the Aioi rather than in Limyrike, putting it in a different kingdom from the one the
*Periplus* gives it. Nobody has explained that.

As with Tyndis, there is no archaeology at any candidate.

## Bakare — the river mouth below Nelkynda

Bakare is defined by the text rather than described independently. The *Periplus*
(ch. 55) says it stands at the mouth of Nelkynda's river, and that ships sailed down to
it empty to take on cargo, because the river was too shallow to carry a loaded hull.
Pliny knows it as Becare, port of the Neacyndi, with the pepper country of Cottonara
behind it.

Schoff identified it with **Porakad** (Purakkad) and Casson agreed. McCrindle had
suggested **Kallada**; Dayalan records Markari, Varakkai and Thevalakara.

Bakare cannot be solved on its own, because the text makes it a function of Nelkynda. And
the pairing works: Niranam and Purakkad lie 21 km apart *(measured)* against a stated
19–22, on the same river, with Bakare seaward as the text requires. Pliny's independent
association of Becare with the Neacyndi supports the pairing from outside the *Periplus*.

One thing to keep in view: **the name is an editor's conjecture.** The manuscript reads
*Bararē*. Casson corrected it to *Bakarē* on the strength of Ptolemy's Bakarē and Pliny's
Becare. Almost certainly right — but a reconstruction, not something the manuscript says.

## Where this leaves us

| port | best answer | how strong |
|---|---|---|
| Leuke Kome | Khuraybah, on Aynunah Bay | strong — the only candidate with excavated Nabataean–Roman material |
| Barbarikon | Banbhore, provisionally | weak — contested with Daybul, and 180 km short on the stated distance |
| Rhapta | Rufiji delta | moderate — three lines converge, but no site has been identified as Rhapta |
| Tyndis | Ponnani | moderate — best fit to the stated distance; Tanur still possible; no excavation |
| Muziris | lower Periyar; Pattanam or Kodungallur | the complex is certain, the name is not |
| Nelkynda | Niranam | moderate — the only candidate the text's own figure allows |
| Bakare | Purakkad | moderate — follows from Nelkynda, corroborated by Pliny |

**Ruled out by the text's own numbers:** Koyilandy and probably Kadalundi for Tyndis;
Kollam and Neendakara for Nelkynda; Pemba for Menuthias, and so Dar es Salaam for Rhapta.

**Still to chase.** Eight candidates appear in the regional literature with no first
proposer I could find: Tanur and Koyilandy for Tyndis; Thevalakara, Markari and Varakkai
for Bakare; al-Qusayr for Leuke Kome; Nirkunnam and Kannetri for Nelkynda. Until those are
traced, the candidate list cannot be called complete.
