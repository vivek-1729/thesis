# Reconstructing the Kerala channels

What the geomorphological literature actually supports, after three surface
methods failed and the borehole evidence turned out to say something other than
what I expected.

## The buried sands are old shorelines, not old rivers

Eight of the seventeen boreholes in Maya et al. 2017 contain a sand body sealed
under finer sediment, which is the classic signature of a buried channel. They
are not channels. The paper's own interpretation is explicit:

> "The medium to fine grained, well sorted quartzose sand lying directly above
> the greenish grey clay in the Komallur borehole core is nothing but the beach/
> littoral sediments formed under regressive phase."

> "The sediments in the inland wetlands on the east give indications of
> transgressive-regressive sequence with marine sediments at the base followed
> upwards by transitional and fresh water environments."

The fauna agree. Marine indicators, pelecypods, gastropods, dinoflagellates and
foraminiferal linings, appear in eight of the seventeen cores including every
one of the thick buried sands. These are transgressive and regressive beach
deposits recording the sea advancing and retreating, not rivers.

**So the borehole route does not deliver palaeochannels either.** That is four
methods now negative for Kerala: optical, radar, terrain, and subsurface
lithology. Recorded in `data/raw/kerala_boreholes.csv`.

## But two things in the same paper do bear on the question

**The inland wetland chain is drowned river valley.** The paper states it
directly:

> "The chain of wetlands and water bodies that are seen in the eastern periphery
> of the coastal lands indicates the remnants of the upper drainage channels of
> the previously existed coastal plain rivers of Late Pleistocene age that are
> later broadened due to coastal erosion under the transgressive phase."

That is a palaeochannel statement about a feature we can already map, because
the wetland chain is in the JRC water layer. It does not date to the first
century, but it constrains where drainage ran.

**Two beach-ridge sets bound what land existed.** The paper distinguishes a
NNE-SSW trending inland set marking the Late Pleistocene transgression from a
coast-parallel younger set marking the Mid Holocene one:

> "The present beach parallel ridges in the younger coastal plain indicate the
> limit of the Mid Holocene shoreline."

Everything seaward of the inland edge of the younger ridge set accreted after
the mid-Holocene. That is a mappable line, and it is the nearest dated shoreline
constraint to antiquity that exists for this coast. Any candidate sitting
seaward of it needs its ground shown to have existed by AD 50.

## Two findings in the library that bear directly on this

**The coast may have been essentially finished before the Periplus.** Shajan,
Tomber, Selvakumar and Cherian 2004, reporting Mathai and Nair:

> "these sand ridges and backwater bodies emerged between 7000 and 2000 years
> BP, and that the development of the coast was almost completed by 2000 years
> BP."

If that holds, the first-century coastline around the Periyar is closer to the
modern one than I have been assuming, and the reconstruction problem for Muziris
is much smaller than for the ports further south. It is a strong claim resting
on one 1988 paper and it needs checking, but it is the most consequential
sentence I have found in the library on this question.

**The Vembanad is a different case, and the experts say it is not
reconstructible.** Gurukkal and Whittaker 2001 on the 1341 event:

> "the great cyclone and flood of 1341, which opened up the present harbour of
> Kochi and the Vembanad backwater lake system to the sea while forming ... the
> putuveppu, the new deposit of land called Wypen island"

and on what follows from it:

> "the coastal changes in the formation of the Vembanad Lake backwaters have
> been so profound that it is almost anyone's guess where a plausible port of
> Becare might have been situated, or how it would have linked with the inland
> town of Nelcynda."

> "the huge changes in the Vembanad waterways make it impossible to base much
> upon this evidence."

So the consensus position is that the Vembanad cannot be reconstructed, and the
Vembanad is exactly where Nelkynda and Bakarē sit. That is worth stating plainly
rather than working around, and it means our contribution there is bounding the
uncertainty rather than removing it.

**A target profile, from the same paper.** Gurukkal and Whittaker summarise what
is being looked for: "a port on a river inlet that silted up a few kilometres
from the sea." Silting is the mechanism they propose for why Bakarē displaced
Muziris, since a loaded ship could no longer cross the bar.

## Two leads this opens

**Mathai, T. and S. B. Nair (1988)**, "Stages in the emergence of the
Cochin-Kodungallur coast: evidence for the interaction of marine and fluvial
processes." The source of the 2000 BP claim, cited by both Shajan et al. and
Maya et al. Acquire and check it, because a great deal rests on it.

**Ibn Battuta.** He was on this coast in 1342, within a year of the flood. Any
description he gives of the ports and the waterways is the closest thing to an
eyewitness account of the coast at the moment it changed, and it predates every
European map by a century and a half.

## What this means for the thesis

The honest position on Kerala is that no method recovers a first-century channel
network, and four have been tried. What the geomorphology gives instead is a
set of constraints: which ground is too young, where drainage formerly ran, and
how far the sea reached at two dated moments.

That is a prior on the landscape, not a map of it. The distance analysis has to
be run over the modern and 1857 water networks with that uncertainty carried,
rather than over a reconstructed first-century one that cannot be built.

## Still to read

Narayana, Priju and Chakrabarti (2001) on a palaeo-delta at the mouth of the
Periyar is the one remaining lead, and it addresses Muziris directly. Maya et
al. 2022 for the Kuttanad boreholes specifically, and Alappat et al. 2015, whose
OSL dates may reach closer to the first century than the radiocarbon does.


## The pre-1700 Dutch chart, and what it shows

`library/charts-gallica/malabar-canara-soundings-c1700.jpg`, the "Carte
particuliere des costes de Canara et de Malabar, avec les Sondes", 8856 by 6200
pixels, covers 9°N to 16°N from Goa to below Cochin. Figure 29 is a full-scale
crop of its southern end.

It lands directly on our candidates and it is Dutch, from the VOC period when
they held Cochin, so it predates the 1857 Admiralty survey by roughly 150 years
and every modern intervention by far more.

Legible on that crop:

- **Cranckenoore**, which is Cranganore, Kodungallur, the traditional Muziris
  identification.
- **Porcae**, which is Purakkad, the principal Bakarē candidate, with the Dutch
  legend *Hyerbegint het Ryck Porcae*, "here begins the kingdom of Porcae".
- **Baspin** and **Balyporte**, the Vypin and Vaipin shore formed, by tradition,
  in the 1341 event.
- The barrier spit drawn continuously, with the backwater behind it.
- Soundings in fathoms along the whole approach, and the latitude scale on the
  border, which is what makes the sheet georeferenceable.

**The soundings are the point.** Off Purakkad they read 6, 7, 10, 11 and 12
fathoms close inshore, roughly 11 to 22 m, which is ample for any ancient
vessel. Around the Cranganore and Vypin entrance they read 3, 4½ and 5 fathoms,
roughly 5 to 9 m, and shoal patches are drawn at the mouth itself.

That contrast bears directly on Gurukkal and Whittaker's proposed mechanism for
Bakarē displacing Muziris, that "silting up of the river mouth made it
impossible for a fully loaded ship to cross the sand bar". It does not
demonstrate the mechanism, since this is around 1700 and not AD 50. What it does
is show the pattern they describe as a measured fact at the earliest date
anybody recorded it: a deep approach at Purakkad and a shoal, bar-obstructed
entrance at Cranganore.

**This is the earliest bathymetry for the Malabar coast we have found anywhere**,
and it is free, public domain and already downloaded.
