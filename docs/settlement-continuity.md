# Later settlement at the candidate sites

`data/raw/later_settlement.csv` — 20 sites with attested occupation after the
Periplus horizon, each with a period range, the evidence, a confidence grade
and a source. Overlaid on the port panels as a muted outer ring, so the map
gains a dimension without gaining clutter.

## Why it belongs on these maps

A location that works as a harbour in AD 50 usually still works in AD 900 and
AD 1500: the same river mouth, the same shelter, the same road to the interior.
Later occupation is therefore weak independent evidence that a spot is a viable
port location. It also explains absence — a candidate under a living town
cannot be excavated, which is the stated reason Ponnani, Kodungallur and Kollam
have never been dug.

## The pattern, and it cuts both ways

| Candidate | Periplus-era evidence | Later occupation |
|---|---|---|
| Kodungallur | none | medieval to early modern |
| Ponnani | none | late medieval to early modern |
| Tanur | none | late medieval to early modern |
| Kadalundi / Beypore / Chaliyam | none | late medieval to early modern |
| Koyilandy | none | medieval to early modern |
| Niranam | none | medieval |
| Kottayam | none | medieval to early modern |
| Kollam / Quilon | none | medieval |
| Purakkad | none | early modern |
| Neendakara | none | modern |

**Every Kerala candidate for Tyndis, Nelkynda and Bakarē is a documented later
port with no first-century evidence whatever.** Koyilandy is Panthalayani
Kollam, named by Ibn Battuta, called Flandarina by Friar Odoric and Pandarani by
the Portuguese. Kollam gives its name to the Kollam era, which begins in AD 825.
Beypore built ocean-going *uru* into the twentieth century. Kodungallur held a
Portuguese fort from 1523.

That is a real finding about how the candidate lists were built. These places
were not proposed because anything Roman was found at them; they were proposed
because they are known harbours in later sources, and the identification was
then projected backwards onto a first-century text. The ring on the map is the
evidence that got them nominated.

Which cuts two ways, and the thesis should say both:

- **For.** Continuity is genuine evidence of viability. A river mouth that
  supported a port for a thousand years was probably usable in the first
  century too, and the absence of early material at a site buried under a
  medieval and modern town is close to uninformative.
- **Against.** The reasoning is circular if left unexamined. "Medieval port,
  therefore ancient port" does not follow — coastlines prograde, rivers switch
  channels, and the Malabar harbours that mattered in AD 1400 need not be the
  ones that mattered in AD 50. Kodungallur is the warning: excavated in 1969–70
  precisely because it was the traditional Muziris, and it returned nothing
  earlier than the ninth century.

## The exceptions worth noting

- **Kallada, Thevalakara, al-Qusayr, Fukuchani and the Rufiji delta** have no
  attested later occupation either. They are candidates on topographic or
  textual grounds alone.
- **Aynuna** is the reverse of the Kerala pattern: real Periplus-horizon
  evidence (25 excavated coins, 2nd c. BC – AD 40) and only limited later
  material. It was a port at the right date and then stopped being one, which
  is why it can be excavated at all.
- **Banbhore** has both, and that is its problem: 30 seasons of excavation
  dominated by Islamic-period Daybul, with the pre-Islamic levels thin and
  contested beneath.

## Confidence

Eleven entries are graded high (excavation, coins, or a named medieval source),
five medium, four low. The low ones — Niranam, Kottayam, Neendakara and the
Aynuna Islamic material — rest on regional tradition rather than tested
stratigraphy and are marked as such in the table.
