# Casson's distance claims, tested against his own figures

Casson's edition of the *Periplus* (Princeton 1989) is the standard text, and
its Appendix 1 supplies most of the identifications still in circulation. The
passages below are claims about the accuracy of the author's distances that do
not survive being checked. Every number used to check them is Casson's own: his
transcription of the stated stadia, his measurements in nautical miles, and his
identifications fixing the endpoints.

Page references are to the PDF in `library/ancient-sources/`. Printed pages run
about 22 lower.

---

## 1. The error bound for short legs

> "For short distances, around 2500 stades or less, the author's estimates are
> rather good, being either exact or at most twenty percent off."
> — Appendix 2, PDF p.297

**False on his own table.** Okēlis to Eudaimōn Arabia is stated as 1200 stadia
and measured by Casson at 95 nautical miles, an error of **26.3 per cent**. The
leg is well inside his 2500-stade threshold and appears in the very table that
follows this sentence.

## 2. The error bound for long legs

> "For long distances around 5000 stades or more, his figures are high, from
> twenty-five percent to as much as fifty percent."
> — Appendix 2, PDF p.298

**False on his own table.** Adulis to Avalitēs is stated as 4800 stadia against
his own 250 nautical miles, an error of **92 per cent**, nearly double the
ceiling he states. Casson does flag the endpoint of this leg as contested in
his footnote 7, but he does not exclude it from the table or adjust the claim.

## 3. The direction of error in Indian waters

> "For Indian waters, however, he again offers figures and in stades; either he
> knew these better or, what is more likely, was able to talk with knowledgeable
> informants ... His figures are quite good, considering the length of the
> distances involved."
> — Appendix 2, PDF p.300

**Both halves fail.** The two Indian legs are **underestimates** of 33 and 22
per cent, which breaks the rule stated in §2 that long legs run high, and they
break it in the one region the present project most depends on. And 33 per cent
is worse than several of the legs he grades as merely "rather good". Casson
himself records the size of the discrepancy two sentences later, writing that
the true Barbarikon to Astakapra distance "is actually some 450 nautical miles,
one-half of his figure greater," and still calls the figures quite good.

## 4. That error grows with distance

The short-leg and long-leg claims together assert that accuracy degrades with
distance. **Regressing log error on log stated distance across his eleven legs
gives a slope of +0.067 with r = +0.24.** There is essentially no relationship.
The apparent pattern is driven by two legs, and the Indian legs run the other
way.

## 5. The cumulative validation of the African route

> "If we turn from the figures for individual courses to their cumulative
> totals, we find that for the African trade route, which began at Myos Hormos
> and ended at Rhapta, the author's under- and overestimates balance out to
> produce a remarkably accurate result."
> — Appendix 2, PDF p.299

> "The actual distance from Myos Hormos to Rhapta (Abu Sha'r to the vicinity of
> Dar es Salaam or to the Rufiji River some seventy-five miles further south) is
> ca. 3000 nautical miles. This is a mere five percent less."
> — Appendix 2, PDF pp.299–300

**Circular, and built on a superseded endpoint.** Three problems.

The northern endpoint is **Abu Sha'r**, which Casson defends at PDF p.115 on the
strength of Pliny's mention of a spring. Myos Hormos is now placed at Quseir
al-Qadim, so the measurement starts from the wrong place. The same error
underlies Nappo's 2010 argument for Leukē Kōmē.

The southern endpoint is **Rhapta**, whose location is the thing in dispute.
Validating the text's distances against an assumed position for an unlocated
port assumes what it sets out to show. Casson's own hedge on that endpoint,
"seventy-five miles further south," is about 2.5 per cent of 3000 nautical
miles, which is half the accuracy he claims to have demonstrated.

**And error cancellation in a sum is not accuracy.** His individual legs run
from 15 per cent low to 92 per cent high. That they sum to within 5 per cent
shows the errors are roughly mean-zero. It says nothing about any single leg,
and single legs are all that locating a port can use.

## 6. The overall verdict

> "In a word, when one considers the means he had at his disposal, the author's
> performance is impressive."
> — Appendix 2, PDF p.301

**Overstated, and the qualifier is doing more work than it appears to.** Across
the nine legs of Casson's own accuracy tables the implied stadion runs from
**96.5 m to 217.6 m**, a 2.3-fold spread. The standard deviation of
log(stated/actual) across his eleven legs is **0.311**, which is a typical
multiplicative error of about 1.3×.

The charitable reading is the one Casson's own Appendix 2 supplies and does not
follow through: the ancients "never actually measured distances at sea" and
estimated from travel time at 500 stades to a day's run. On that reading the
spread is not error at all. It is the range of speeds a square-rigged vessel
makes between coasting into a headwind and running with the monsoon, **26 to 59
nautical miles per day**. The performance is not impressive or unimpressive; it
is a record of sailing conditions rather than of distances.

## The conversion everyone inherits

> "Assuming the use of the most common stade, that of 600 or so feet,
> approximately ten stades correspond to a nautical mile."
> — Appendix 2, PDF p.297

Not false, but worth stating plainly: **the conventional 185 m stadion used
throughout this literature is 1852 divided by 10.** It is a rule of thumb for
converting a rough figure, not a metrological determination, and it has been
treated as a constant ever since.

## Claims in Appendix 1 that the above undermines

These are not errors of fact. They are identifications resting on the accuracy
Appendix 2 fails to establish, and Casson never runs the Malabar legs through
the accuracy tables at all.

> "Schoff puts Tyndis at Ponnani, which is somewhat short of 500 stades, while
> others put it at Kadalundi near Beypore (11°10'N), which is somewhat over; the
> odds are slightly in favor of Ponnani." — Appendix 1, PDF p.316

Tyndis to Muziris on his own identifications is 39 nautical miles against a
stated 500 stadia, an error of **28.2 per cent**, which violates the short-leg
bound in §1. Muziris to Nelkynda, stated as the same 500 stadia, is 55 nautical
miles. **The two legs the text calls equal differ by 41 per cent**, implying
144 m and 204 m respectively. Neither leg appears in Appendix 2.

> "Five names—Semylla, Muziris, Bakare, Red Mountain, Komar—can be identified
> with more confidence than the others." — Appendix 1, PDF p.315

Bakarē is graded as one of the securer identifications, which is at odds both
with the state of the modern discussion and with the fact that no candidate for
it has ever been excavated.

> "The suggestion that Nelkynda was Niranom (Neranom), which is about 500 stades
> from Muziris and which is on the Pambiyar twelve miles east of Pirakkad, at
> least puts the port of trade and its harbor on the same stream and the proper
> distance from each other." — Appendix 1, PDF p.317

The strongest version of the pair argument, and it half-survives. Twelve miles
is 19.3 km, and our measurement of Niranam to Purakkad gives 21.2 km, close to
120 stadia at a 155 m stadion. But Niranam sits 106 km from Muziris along the
coast against a 72–102 km band, so **the two constraints cannot both be
satisfied under straight-line measurement**. The text's own "by river and sea"
may resolve this, and nobody has measured it that way.

## Where Casson is right, and it matters

> "A further complication is that extensive changes have taken place in the
> coastline." — Appendix 1

> Smith "prudently observes that 'the extensive changes which have occurred in
> the rivers of Sind during the course of eighteen centuries preclude the
> possibility of satisfactory identifications of either of these towns
> [Barbarikon and Minnagar].'" — PDF p.207

Both are correct, both are load-bearing, and both are routinely ignored by
arguments that measure across the modern coastline. The coastline reconstruction
is not a refinement of this project. It is a precondition Casson himself states.
