# What we know, and what it lets us say about the ports

A plain account of where the project stands. Part I is what has been
established. Part II is the only part that discriminates between candidates, and
says how strongly. Part III scores the four questions. Part IV is what is
missing.

Nothing here claims a port has been located. The governing plan puts that at
step five and this is step two. What has changed is that two lines of evidence
now separate candidates rather than merely describing them.

---

# Part I. What is established

## 1. The text, as an instrument

**The stadion calibrates to about 155 m**, not the conventional 185 m, from the
median of four legs with securely identified endpoints.

**Eighty-two per cent of the stadia figures are multiples of 100**, and every
value at or above 200 is a round hundred. The only unround numbers in the text
are 20, 60 and 120, and all three are local. So the long legs are rounded
gestures and the short upriver notices are the precise ones, which is the
reverse of how they are usually weighted.

**The Malabar distances are given "by river and sea"**, not along the coast.
Until this month nobody had measured them that way.

**Casson's own table is internally inconsistent**, implying 144 m and 204 m on
two legs the text gives as equal.

## 2. The material record cannot point at a harbour

**Thirteen of twenty-two disputed candidates have never been excavated**, and in
Kerala only four fieldwork entries in sixty-one years of *Indian Archaeology: A
Review* mention material of Periplus date. Absence is absence of digging.

**Roman coins are an interior phenomenon.** Seventy-three per cent lie more than
25 km inland, concentrated on the Palghat corridor. They mark a trade route, not
a landing place.

**Every Kerala candidate is a documented later port** with no first-century
evidence, which is a fact about how the candidate lists were assembled rather
than about the first century.

**Nothing on these coasts dates to within five centuries of AD 50.** That was
asserted from the literature; it has now been checked over the ground. Four open
databases hold 828 records within 200 km of any of the six port boxes, and
exactly one is both within 50 km and spans AD 50. It is a sea-surface
temperature curve 49 km off Tyndis, which is not evidence about a harbour.

## 3. The landscape splits in two

**Imagery finds buried channels in arid terrain and cannot do it in Kerala.**
Four independent methods failed there, because the Kuttanad polder system has
re-cut the ground into features that mimic palaeochannels.

**Terrain beats optical** (0.696 against 0.414 on real dead channels), and the
riparian control was flattering the detector (0.814 on it). **DEM quality is not
the bottleneck**: synthetic terrain at real noise scores 0.971. The ceiling is
the ground, not the instrument.

**The satellite cannot see a narrow river under canopy.** The Pamba does not
appear in the JRC water record at all above the Vembanad, which left Niranam 13
km from navigable water although it sits on the river. The C-band SAR literature
says Sentinel-1 would not have helped; L-band would be needed.

## 4. The environment

**There is no measured bathymetry at any of the six port approaches.** Not
scarce, zero: 2.5 million marine cells and not one resting on a real sounding,
while the controls are 100 per cent covered. Searched across nine sources.

**The tide, fitted from observation, runs 3.72 m at springs at Rhapta against
0.58 m at Muziris.** A sixfold difference in what a harbour must cope with.

**The 1857 Admiralty sheets georeference from their own graticule to about 20 m**,
and one states its own tidal datum, which the project had recorded as unknown.
They are the only measured depth that exists for this coast, and they predate
the reclamation that defeats the satellites.

# Part II. What discriminates between candidates

Two things, and they are new this month. Both come from routing the Malabar
legs through water instead of across it.

## 5. The routed distances separate the candidates by a factor of two

The text gives Tyndis to Muziris as 500 stadia and Muziris to Nelkynda as about
500 stadia. Routing those through the water network and dividing by 500 gives
the stadion each candidate pair would require.

**Tyndis, against a calibrated 155 m and a conventional 185 m:**

| Candidate | Implied stadion |
|---|---|
| Ponnani | 167 to 174 m |
| Barchi gazetteer position (the same place) | 166 to 173 m |
| Tanur | 218 to 225 m |
| Kadalundi, Beypore, Chaliyam | 256 to 263 m |
| Koyilandy | 337 to 343 m |

**Nelkynda:**

| Candidate | Implied stadion |
|---|---|
| Nirkunnam | 180 to 207 m |
| Kottayam | 198 to 226 m |
| Copeland model position | 260 to 288 m |
| Nakkada | 262 to 290 m |
| Niranam | 268 to 296 m |
| Kannetri | 289 to 316 m |
| Neendakara | 302 to 329 m |
| Kollam | 320 to 347 m |

**What this does and does not establish.** It is a ranking, not a fit. But the
spread is a factor of two, and the lower end sits close to a figure derived
independently from four other legs, while the upper end requires a stadion
longer than any value attested anywhere. That is the distance evidence
discriminating for the first time in this project.

**It only discriminates because the route goes through water.** Routed distance
exceeds the straight line by a median of 1.13 and by as much as 1.69, and the
ratio is not constant, so no straight-line reconstruction can be rescaled into
this. Every existing reconstruction of these legs is short, and short by a
different amount for each pair.

## 6. No candidate satisfies the two short notices

The text says Muziris lies 20 stadia up the river from the shore and Nelkynda
120 stadia from the sea. These are the unround figures, which Part I.1 shows are
the reliable ones. At 155 m they are 3.1 km and 18.6 km.

Measured through water from the open sea on the modern network:

| Port and stated distance | Candidates, measured through water |
|---|---|
| Muziris, 20 stadia = 3.1 km | Pattanam 9.0, Copeland position 9.0, Kodungallur 12.4 |
| Nelkynda, 120 stadia = 18.6 km | Neendakara 0.7, Nirkunnam 0.8, Kannetri 4.4, Kollam 8.6, Copeland 29.9, Nakkada 30.8, Niranam 33.9, Kottayam 77.6 |

All distances in kilometres.

**Nothing fits.** For Muziris every candidate is three to four times too far. For
Nelkynda the candidates fall into two groups, one an order of magnitude too
close and one roughly twice too far, with nothing near 18.6 km.

**This is the strongest negative result the project has, and it points
somewhere.** Either the candidate lists are wrong, or the shoreline has moved far
enough to change the measurement. The second is now testable, because Narayana,
Priju and Chakrabarti 2001 map an ancient shoreline running inland of the modern
coast through Parur and past the Muziris candidates, and that figure has been
georeferenced to 67 m RMS, checked against five places it names. If the
first-century shore lay on that line, Pattanam's 9.0 km shrinks toward 3. The
paper assigns no date to it and says so, so this is a hypothesis with
coordinates rather than an answer.

## 7. The sailing evidence corroborates the text without locating a port

**The sailing season is forced, not conventional.** Okelis to Kane is impossible
for seven months of the year, open only May to September. Departures from
October to May strand a ship for two to nine months. July, August and September
are the only ones that work. The text names July, four times, in the same words.

**Chapter 57's distinction between the two courses is real in the wind field.**
The text says those bound for Limyrike throw the ship's head considerably off
the wind while those bound for Barygaza hold their course straight out to sea.
In July the Malabar course sits 132 degrees off the wind and the Barygaza course
159 degrees, which is nearly dead downwind. A 27 degree difference, from 78
years of wind record, on a claim no one appears to have checked.

Ship performance is Whitewright 2011 on ancient Mediterranean rigs, measured
from replica trials: sixty to sixty-five degrees off the wind close-hauled with
two knots made good, four to six knots reaching or running, with Palmer 2009
reaching the sixty-degree limit independently.

This bears on the stadion as a speed, which is question four, and it does not
separate candidates.

# Part III. How the four questions stand

## Q1. Where was the coastline?

**Least answered of the four, and the satellite comparison has just failed a
check.** Landsat MSS 1972-76 against the JRC record gives median shifts of +60 m
at the Periyar, +179 m at Tyndis, +893 m at the Pamba and +1244 m at the Indus,
all landward. The direction is what condemns it: 124 of 124 significant
transects at Tyndis move landward, 132 of 133 at the Pamba, 75 of 78 at the
Indus. A real coast erodes in some places and builds in others, so a shift that
is unanimous in direction is a systematic bias and not a measurement. The
likely source is that the two epochs define water differently, a frequency over
eleven MSS scenes against a JRC occurrence percentage, compounded by 1970s
georegistration. The Sentinel-2 epoch failed to build at every site, so there is
no third line to arbitrate. These numbers are not usable and are recorded only
so the attempt is not repeated blind.

**What is solid here** is the 1857 line at about 20 m, which is 115 years before
the earliest satellite epoch, and Narayana's ancient shoreline georeferenced to
67 m. Neither is dated.

**What would finish it.** A third satellite epoch that actually builds, a
consistent water definition across epochs, and a date on the ancient shoreline
or the Kerala sea-level curve.

## Q2. How deep was the water, and could a ship get in?

**Not answered, and the evidence is in hand but not extracted.** No modern
source has a single measured sounding at any approach. The 1857 sheets carry
several thousand and are georeferenced; twenty have been transcribed. This is
the question Part I calls the one that genuinely separates candidates, and it is
blocked on transcription rather than on anything hard.

## Q3. Where did the rivers run?

**Answered for the modern network, not for the first century.** The legs are
routed and sections 5 and 6 are the result. Building the network took six
corrections and the honest limitation stands: the 1341 flood reorganised the
Vembanad and the modern water record is not the ancient one. The 1857 sheets
draw the network before the reclamation and would give a second, much older
epoch; they have not been traced.

## Q4. How fast did ships actually sail?

**Answered as well as the evidence allows.** The season, the courses and the
passage times are computed from a measured performance envelope and 78 years of
wind. The results corroborate the text on two independent points. What they
cannot do is convert a stadion into a distance without knowing which leg a
sailor was timing.

# Part IV. What is missing, in order of what it would settle

1. **Transcribe the Admiralty soundings.** Unblocks Q2 entirely. Several
   thousand points, georeferenced, no acquisition needed.
2. **Fix the shoreline comparison**: one water definition across epochs, and a
   Sentinel-2 composite that builds. Until then Q1 has no satellite answer.
3. **Trace the 1857 backwater network** and re-route sections 5 and 6 through
   it. That replaces a modern network with one that predates the engineering.
4. **Date the ancient shoreline**, or acquire something that does. It is the
   hinge of section 6.
5. **The 69 JGSI papers**, of which 26 are directly about dated coastline
   position, including *Quaternary Geology of Kerala: Evidence from Radiocarbon
   Dates*. The Malabar dating evidence is not missing; it is behind a
   subscription.
6. **Priju's unpublished resistivity reports** at CWRDM, which read the sand
   body under the surface that defeated four methods.

---

# What this does not say

No candidate has been located, confirmed or eliminated. Sections 5 and 6 give a
ranking and a constraint, not a position. The routed distances rest on a modern
water network, the implied stadia are ratios rather than fits, and the one
result that would convert section 6 from a puzzle into evidence depends on
dating a line that its own authors left undated.
